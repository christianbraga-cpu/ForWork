faq:
  - q: "Who can you have as witnesses at a wedding?"
    a: "In Australia, any two people who are, or appear to be, over 18 and are present for the ceremony. Registries add that family and friends are fine, and Queensland's says they need not be citizens."
  - q: "How many wedding witnesses do you need?"
    a: "Two in Australia. Australians marrying in Italy also need four unrelated witnesses for the Atto Notorio beforehand. Confirm the number with the authority that marries you."

    a: "Yes. Victoria's registry says any family member or friend aged 18 or over can witness, and AGD's guidelines say your own children can sign as witnesses if they are 18 or older."
  - q: "Can our celebrant, photographer or planner be a witness?"
    a: "The Marriage Act asks for two people who are, or appear to be, over 18 and are present; it does not list professions. Plan on two people besides your celebrant, ideally people who know you, and confirm your choice with your celebrant."
  - q: "Do you need witnesses at a registry office wedding?"
    a: "Yes, the same two adult witnesses. The Brisbane Registry, for example, says it cannot supply witnesses, so bring your own."

    a: "According to AGD's guidelines, a commitment ceremony is not a marriage and needs no witnesses. Your legal witnesses belong at the legal ceremony, wherever that takes place."

# Wedding Witnesses and How Many You Need

[IMAGE: hero]

To marry in Australia you need two wedding witnesses who are, or appear to your celebrant to be, over 18, and who are physically there with you and your celebrant ([section 44 of the Marriage Act 1961](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1961A00012/2024-07-09/2024-07-09/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)). Invite as many guests as you like, but only those two sign.

The harder questions come after: can your mother be one, does your best friend from London qualify, what exactly do they sign, and what changes in France, Italy, Greece, Bali or Fiji? This guide answers each, and our pillar covers [everything else Australia requires before you marry](/expert-advice/marriage-licence-requirements). We checked every source on 13 November 2026. Confirm anything that shapes your plans with your celebrant, your registry or the authority abroad.

## How many witnesses do you need to get married in Australia?

Two. The law asks for two people who are, or appear to be, over 18 and are present. Your celebrant, both of you and both witnesses must be physically present, so a witness cannot join by video link, according to the [Attorney-General's Department (AGD)](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married).

Everyone else in the room is a guest. AGD's guidelines say no one other than the two witnesses adds a signature to your certificate, so if you love the idea of everyone signing, use a guest book instead.

## Who can be a wedding witness, and who cannot

The good news for most families: [Births, Deaths and Marriages Victoria](https://www.bdm.vic.gov.au/getting-married-victoria) says your witnesses can be any family member or friend who is at least 18. That covers parents, siblings, grandparents and your partner's relatives. The [Brisbane Registry](https://www.qld.gov.au/family/marriage-and-civil-partnerships/brisbane-registry-weddings/the-brisbane-registry/on-the-day-accordion/what-to-bring) adds that witnesses do not need to be Australian citizens, which matters when your closest friend flies in from abroad.

[IMAGE: who-can]

Age is the firm line. According to AGD's guidelines, your own children can sign as witnesses only if they are 18 or over. A 16-year-old can stand beside you, read, or carry the rings, but cannot sign.

AGD also notes that it is best practice, though not a legal requirement, for your witnesses to know you, since their evidence can later be used to confirm who you are and how the ceremony took place; the [NSW registry](https://www.nsw.gov.au/family-and-relationships/marriages/getting-married-nsw) gives the same advice. Finding them is your job: your celebrant is not responsible for providing witnesses, and the Brisbane Registry says it cannot supply them.

**Language.** Here the official sources do not quite agree. The NSW and Queensland registries ask that witnesses speak and understand English, while AGD's guidelines for celebrants allow an interpreter under section 112 when a witness does not understand the language of the ceremony. Your celebrant decides whether an interpreter is used. At its own ceremonies, the Brisbane Registry asks that the interpreter is not one of your witnesses. If a parent or friend you would love to have sign is more at home in another language, raise it with your celebrant early.

**What about the celebrant, photographer or planner?** Section 44 names no professions; it asks only for two people who are, or appear to be, over 18 and are present. Plan on two people besides your celebrant, and ask your celebrant before you choose anyone working on the day.

Practically, choose two people who will certainly be there, on time and clear-headed, and name a third adult as a backup marriage witness. The Brisbane Registry asks that witnesses attend the entire ceremony, so a late arrival is a real problem.

## What your witnesses actually do and sign

Your witnesses watch the ceremony itself: the vows and the celebrant's statement are said in their presence. Then the two of you, your celebrant and both witnesses sign three certificates: two official certificates of marriage (one your celebrant keeps, one for the registry) and the certificate of marriage you take home on the day. Within 14 days, your celebrant sends the paperwork to the registry in the state or territory where you married.

[IMAGE: signing]

AGD's guidelines ask witnesses to write their names in full, including middle names. Brief them before the day so nobody signs a nickname. One more reason to linger: your celebrant can correct an error on the certificates only while you and your witnesses are still there. Once everyone has left, a correction becomes a matter for the registry.

The choreography is simple: your witnesses sit in the front row, step up to the signing table after the vows, and sign in turn.

Is the role important? Legally, yes: section 44 of the Marriage Act says a marriage cannot be solemnised unless two witnesses over 18 are present.

## Witnessing your NOIM is a different job

The person who witnesses your Notice of Intended Marriage (NOIM) is not one of your ceremony witnesses. They are an authorised official from a set list, and by signing they confirm they have identified you. Your friends cannot do it unless they hold one of the listed roles, and a different list applies if you sign overseas. Our pillar guide explains [who can witness your notice and how it works from overseas](/expert-advice/marriage-licence-requirements).

When the legal ceremony takes place overseas, the destination's rules replace Australia's. The count, the age and the ID your witnesses carry all change. **France.** A legal wedding there needs at least one of you to be a French citizen or legal resident. If that applies, your witnesses can be family or not, of any nationality, declared in advance and carrying original ID on the day.

**Italy.** Australians first need an Atto Notorio, signed by the Australian partner and four witnesses not related to the couple, at the Italian consulate for their state or at a tribunal in Italy, according to the [Italian Consulate in Adelaide's 2022 guide](https://consadelaide.esteri.it/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/How-To_ATTO_NOTORIO_EN-2022.pdf). These four are paperwork witnesses, separate from the two at your ceremony. The Atto Notorio leads to a Nulla Osta from the Australian Embassy in Rome and is valid for six months, so it cannot be requested more than six months before the wedding. For the ceremony itself, the [US Embassy in Italy](https://it.usembassy.gov/getting-married-in-italy/) says you need two witnesses of any nationality, over 18, with valid photo ID, and that a witness cannot double as interpreter. Confirm the current rules with your consulate and comune.

**Greece.** Heraklion's municipality asks for one adult witness for each of you, and says a witness may be a relative, even a parent. Rules can differ between municipalities, so ask yours.

**Bali and Fiji.** Indonesia's marriage regulation requires two witnesses, and for non-Muslim couples the marriage must be registered with the Civil Registry Office to be legal. Fiji asks for two witnesses with photo ID. Neither official source we read sets an age, so confirm with the local registry. ## Legal at home, celebration abroad: who witnesses which ceremony

You might sign the legal paperwork in Australia and hold the ceremony everyone remembers in Bali or on the Amalfi Coast. If so, your two legal witnesses belong at the Australian ceremony, which can be small: the two of you, your celebrant and two adults.

The ceremony abroad is then symbolic. According to AGD's guidelines, a commitment ceremony is not a marriage, needs no witnesses and produces no certificate of marriage. That frees the honour to go to anyone: a 12-year-old niece, or a grandparent who could not make the legal ceremony.

Some ways to honour symbolic witnesses, none of which carry legal weight:

- A reading or a blessing in their own language.
- Signing a keepsake certificate you design yourselves.
- Holding the rings, or standing beside you during the vows.

Our guide to [how symbolic and civil rites differ in Italy](/destination-weddings/civil-ceremony-in-italy-vs-catholic-and-symbolic-rites) explains the choice in more depth.

## Getting your witnesses there: the planning nobody sees

Two adults with the right ID in the right room at the right time sounds simple. Across two countries, it rarely is. This is the checklist we would work through:

- **Passports and ID:** check both witnesses hold the ID the authority asks for, valid for travel.
- **Details in advance:** if the authority wants witness details before the day, as France does, gather them early.
- **Arrival:** fly witnesses in at least a day before the civil appointment, not the morning of it.
- **A backup:** name a third adult already on the guest list who meets the same rules.
- **Language:** if an interpreter is needed, the deciding authority sets who can act, so ask before you book anyone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who can you have as witnesses at a wedding?

In Australia, any two people who are, or appear to be, over 18 and are present for the ceremony. Registries add that family and friends are fine, and Queensland's says they need not be citizens.

### How many wedding witnesses do you need?

Two in Australia. Abroad it varies: two to four in France and two in Greece, Bali and Fiji according to the official sources we checked, and the US Embassy in Italy says two for an Italian civil ceremony. Australians marrying in Italy also need four unrelated witnesses for the Atto Notorio beforehand. Confirm the number with the authority that marries you.

### Can family members or parents be wedding witnesses?

Yes. Victoria's registry says any family member or friend aged 18 or over can witness, and AGD's guidelines say your own children can sign as witnesses if they are 18 or older.

### Can our celebrant, photographer or planner be a witness?

The Marriage Act asks for two people who are, or appear to be, over 18 and are present; it does not list professions. Plan on two people besides your celebrant, ideally people who know you, and confirm your choice with your celebrant.

### Do you need witnesses at a registry office wedding?

Yes, the same two adult witnesses. The Brisbane Registry, for example, says it cannot supply witnesses, so bring your own.

### Do we need witnesses for a symbolic ceremony overseas?

According to AGD's guidelines, a commitment ceremony is not a marriage and needs no witnesses. Your legal witnesses belong at the legal ceremony, wherever that takes place.
