# How to Get Married in France as a Foreigner

[IMAGE: hero]

Perhaps it is a chateau in Provence, or a long table among the vines in Champagne. Before the guest list grows, you need a plain answer on the legal side of getting married in France, so here it is. Two foreigners who do not live in France cannot marry at a town hall (mairie) in mainland France. At least one of you must be French or a legal resident of France, and one of you, or a parent of one of you, must have lived in the commune for at least one month of continuous living by the time the banns are published.

That leaves two routes, both set out below: the legal French route if you qualify, and a legal wedding in Australia with your celebration in France. We checked every source on 16 November 2026. Confirm each requirement for your own passports with your mairie, the Australian Embassy in Paris or DFAT.

## Can foreigners marry legally in France?

Yes, when one of you has a real tie to France. The [Australian Embassy in France](https://france.embassy.gov.au/pari/marriage.html) says a marriage must take place at a mairie to be legally recognised, and at least one of you must be a French citizen or a legal resident of France. It adds that marriages are not performed at Australian embassies or missions.

[Service-public.gouv.fr](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F930), the French government's official guide, agrees. If you are both foreigners and do not live in France, it lists two places where you can marry: your own country's consulate, or a commune in a French overseas collectivity or New Caledonia.

So getting married in France comes down to which of two routes fits you.

| Who it suits | Couples where one partner is French or a legal resident of France | Couples who both live in Australia, or anyone who prefers the legal step at home |

Requirements change, and each mairie applies them to your file, so confirm with the mairie and for your own passports before you book.

## Residency and eligibility: who qualifies for a French civil wedding

Two conditions sit side by side: the Embassy's (one of you is French or legally resident) and service-public's rule on which commune can marry you.

That commune is where one of you has your domicile or residence, or where a parent (father or mother only) of one of you lives, which can be a second home such as a family house in Provence. The residence must be established by at least one month of continuous living at the date the banns are published. Proof must be in that person's name at an address in the commune: an energy bill, a home insurance certificate or a rent receipt dated more than one month before the banns.

A parent's house chooses the commune; it does not change the rule for two foreigners who do not live in France. If neither of you is French or resident, the Australian route below is the one open to you.

Same-sex couples are covered on the same terms: service-public addresses couples of different sex and of the same sex throughout. We do not give visa or residency advice.

## Documents from Australia

Service-public says a foreign national must present a certificat de coutume, generally issued by their embassy or consulate. It sets out your country's marriage rules, proves your civil status and shows you are not already married. The mairie may ask for other documents specific to your nationality, and it sets the final list.

[IMAGE: documents]

Australians have two ways to get it:

- **From the Australian Embassy in Paris**, which produces the certificat de coutume in French. You sign its form before a person listed on it, who certifies a copy of the Australian's passport, then post it with certified copies of both passports to the Consular section, 4 rue Jean Rey, 75015 Paris. The Embassy sends a payment form once the application is complete and estimates up to ten working days, plus postage.
- **From DFAT in Australia.** If you already hold an English-language Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI) from a DFAT office (see [Smartraveller's CNI guide](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-certificate-no-impediment-marriage)), have it translated by a sworn translator, in Australia or in France. You then do not need to apply in France.

Under French law the Embassy's certificate is valid for only 90 days from issue, so time it to the banns, not to your engagement. Two Australians living in France who both apply need two separate applications.

| Document | Issued by | Apostille | Translation into French | Fee and timing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Certificat de coutume (CNI) | Australian Embassy Paris (in French) or DFAT in Australia (in English) | Ask your mairie | Sworn translation for the DFAT English version | AUD 181 per certificate (2026 fee); up to ten working days at the Embassy; valid 90 days |
| Birth certificate | Your state or territory BDM registry | Ask your mairie; the Embassy can apostille original BDM documents, AUD 105 each (2026 fee) | Sworn translator | Ask your mairie whether it must be under six months old |
| No Record of Marriage | Your state or territory BDM registry | Ask your mairie | Ask your mairie | Stands in for the certificat de célibat, which the Embassy cannot issue |
| Proof of residence | Your energy supplier, insurer or landlord in the commune | Not needed | Not needed | Dated more than one month before the banns |

The mairie's file also asks for ID, your occupations (in French), your parents' details and your witnesses'. Fees are on the [Embassy's fee schedule](https://france.embassy.gov.au/pari/Notarial-Fees.html) and rise each 1 January, so a 2027 wedding pays the 2027 rate.

## Translations and apostilles

The Embassy sets the rule for Australian papers: a DFAT CNI in English must be translated by a sworn translator, and you contact a sworn translator directly to put your birth certificate into French. That is not the same as NAATI, the Australian accreditation, which [NSW](https://www.nsw.gov.au/departments-and-agencies/births-deaths-marriages/about-registry/using-our-services/proof-of-identity), for example, requires for foreign documents used at home. Ask your mairie which translators it accepts.

Both the Embassy and DFAT charge AUD 105 per apostille (2026 fee). Whether your mairie wants one on each document, and before or after translation, is the mairie's call; get its answer in writing before you order anything. DFAT publishes no fixed [processing time](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-your-documents-legalised) and warns of delays at the end of the school year and in peak holidays.

Before the translator starts, check that every spelling matches across your passport, birth certificate and CNI.

## Banns and timeline

The banns (publication des bans) are notices posted at the door of the mairie where you marry and of the mairie of each partner's domicile, for 10 days. They give your names, occupations, residence and the place of the marriage. A dispensation from publication can be requested from the procureur (the public prosecutor). The wedding must take place within one year of the end of those 10 days.

The registrar will usually interview you together, and may also see you separately; where one partner lives abroad, a diplomatic or consular authority may hold the hearing. If needed, the registrar can refer your file to the procureur, who has 15 days to respond or may set a delay of up to one month, renewable, for an enquiry.

Here is a backward timeline, as an illustration only; your mairie sets the real one.

| When | Legal in France | Legal in Australia |
|---|---|---|
| 12 months out | Contact the mairie of your commune | Choose your celebrant; a NOIM can be given up to 18 months ahead |
| 6 to 4 months out | Order birth certificates and No Record of Marriage certificates; ask the mairie about apostilles and translators | Plan the symbolic celebration in France |
| About 3 months out | Apply for the certificat de coutume (valid 90 days); book sworn translations | Celebrant meets each of you in person |
| By the banns | One month of continuous residence in the commune, with dated proof | NOIM given at least one month before the Australian ceremony |
| Banns | 10 days at the mairie door, plus time for the hearing | Small legal ceremony at home |
| Wedding | Civil ceremony at the mairie within a year of the banns | Fly to France for the celebration |

For the Australian side in detail, see [how to marry legally in Australia first](/expert-advice/marriage-licence-requirements).

## The civil ceremony at the mairie

Service-public says the marriage must be celebrated at the mairie, in a room open to the public. The mayor may hold it in another communal building, such as a salle des fêtes, though the procureur can object. The mayor or a deputy (adjoint) conducts it, with both of you and your witnesses present. So the legal ceremony cannot move to a chateau or a vineyard; that is where your celebration comes in.

You choose two to four [witnesses](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F1248), at least 18 on the day, family or not, of any nationality. You can change them until the ceremony; each brings original ID and stays to sign the register. A witness who does not understand French may have an interpreter, and a translator-interpreter may attend for you, at your cost if paid. Ask your mairie about any charge for the ceremony itself.

Afterwards you receive a livret de famille (the French family record book), and in the following days you can ask the mairie for a full copy (copie intégrale) of your marriage certificate. With 70 to 110 guests, you might keep the mairie to family and witnesses on a weekday, then celebrate with everyone at the chateau.

## Religious and symbolic ceremonies

Service-public is unambiguous: it is forbidden to celebrate a religious marriage before the civil marriage. In France the mairie comes first, and a church or other religious ceremony follows.

A symbolic ceremony is different. It is not a marriage in law, so it can sit wherever your celebration does: among the vines or on a chateau lawn, in your own words, led by someone who knows you.

[IMAGE: symbolic-ceremony]

If neither of you qualifies in France, the legal part happens in Australia. The [Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married) says you give a NOIM to an authorised celebrant at least one month and no more than 18 months before the wedding; you need not be citizens or permanent residents. Since 12 June 2024 the NOIM can be witnessed by video link, or overseas by an authorised witness such as an Australian consular officer or a notary public.

The ceremony cannot be remote. The celebrant must meet each of you in person and be physically present with you and two witnesses over 18, and an Australian celebrant cannot legally marry you overseas. A small ceremony at home before you fly, or after you return, sits comfortably with a full celebration in France.

Planning a religious ceremony in France after marrying at home? Speak to the officiant and the mairie early, because service-public frames its rule around the French civil marriage. ## Recognition and name change back in Australia

If you married in Australia, your celebrant sends the NOIM, the declaration and the Official Certificate of Marriage to the registry in the state or territory where you married within 14 days. That Australian record is your legal record, and the French celebration needs no recognition.

If you married at a French mairie, [Smartraveller's advice on marrying overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) says you cannot register the marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is recognised if it was valid where it happened and would have been valid under Australian law (Part VA of the Marriage Act 1961). Exceptions include a partner already married or under age, and Smartraveller suggests a lawyer if you want certainty. For the general steps, see [what to do with a marriage certificate issued overseas](/expert-advice/overseas-marriage-certificate).

Names take one more step. You can keep travelling on your current passport, and the [Australian Passport Office](https://www.passports.gov.au/change-name-or-gender) recommends booking travel in the name on it. For a new passport in a married name, an applicant born in Australia who married overseas needs a change of name certificate from their state or territory registry, and some registries do not accept overseas certificates for a name change.

In NSW, a person legally married in Australia can take their spouse's family name without registering a change of name. Check your own registry.

## FAQ

### Can foreigners legally get married in France?

Yes, if at least one of you is a French citizen or a legal resident of France and you marry at a mairie, says the Australian Embassy in France. The commune must be one where one of you, or a parent, has lived continuously for at least a month by the banns.

### Can two Australians get married in France without living there?

Not at a mainland mairie. For two foreigners who do not live in France, service-public lists only their own country's consulate or a commune in a French overseas collectivity or New Caledonia, and the Australian Embassy says it does not perform marriages. The alternative is a legal ceremony in Australia, then your celebration in France.

### How long do you need to live in France to get married?

At least one month of continuous living in the commune by the date the banns are published, for one of you or a parent of one of you, with proof in that person's name. At least one of you must also be French or a legal resident of France.

### How much does it cost to get married in France?

The official Australian costs are AUD 181 per certificat de coutume or CNI and AUD 105 per apostille (2026 fees, rising each 1 January). Sworn translators and interpreters set their own prices. Venue and guest costs depend on your plans; start with [our guide to weddings in France](/destinations/europe/france).

### Is a French marriage legally recognised in Australia?

In most cases, yes, if it was valid in France and would have been valid under Australian law, says Smartraveller. You cannot register it in Australia; the French certificate is your proof. For a passport in a married name, an Australian-born spouse first needs a change of name certificate from their state or territory registry.

### Can we have a church wedding in France without a civil ceremony?

No. Service-public says it is forbidden to celebrate a religious marriage before the civil marriage, so the mairie comes first. If you married legally in Australia, ask the officiant and the mairie early how that rule applies to you.
