# Funny Wedding Vows That Still Mean Something

[IMAGE: hero]

Funny wedding vows work when every laugh leads somewhere. Make your guests laugh about the way you both load the dishwasher, then turn and say the thing you actually mean. The joke opens the door; the promise is why everyone came.

This guide is a method, not a joke list. It covers the words Australian law still keeps beside your personal wedding vows in a legal ceremony, a four-part structure you can reuse, which humour lands in front of grandparents, original example lines to adapt, and what changes when you marry at home and celebrate abroad.

Sources current as at November 2026.

## What makes a funny vow still mean something

Funny vows can be funny without being flippant. The rule is simple: laugh, then land. Each joke should set up a sincere promise, so the room relaxes and then goes quiet at exactly the right moment.

The second rule is specificity. A line anyone could say ("I promise to love you even when you steal the doona") gets a polite smile. A line only you could say, about the café you argued your way into on your second date or the way your partner narrates the dog's inner thoughts, gets a real laugh, because it proves you know this person better than anyone in the room.

The test: if a line could appear in someone else's vows unchanged, rewrite it until it could not.

## The legal words you still need in Australia

You can make your vows as funny as you like, but at a legal ceremony in Australia they sit alongside the words the law requires, never instead of them.

Under [section 45(2) of the Marriage Act 1961](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1961A00012/2024-07-09/2024-07-09/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html), where the celebrant is not a minister of religion, each of you says to the other, in the presence of the celebrant and the witnesses:

> "I call upon the persons here present to witness that I, A.B. (or C.D.), take thee, C.D. (or A.B.), to be my lawful wedded wife (or husband, or spouse)"

or words to that effect. A.B. and C.D. stand for your names. A minister of religion may instead use the form of ceremony their religious body recognises, so religious weddings follow their own rites.

[IMAGE: legal-words]

Before that, a celebrant who is not a minister of religion of a recognised denomination says the celebrant's set statement (the monitum), set out in [section 46(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1961A00012/2024-07-09/2024-07-09/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html), in front of you and your witnesses:

> "I am duly authorised by law to solemnise marriages according to law. Before you are joined in marriage in my presence and in the presence of these witnesses, I am to remind you of the solemn and binding nature of the relationship into which you are now about to enter. Marriage, according to law in Australia, is the union of 2 people to the exclusion of all others, voluntarily entered into for life."

or words to that effect. That is a moment in the ceremony with no jokes, and it gives your funny vows something to push against.

Your celebrant decides the final wording on the day and will place everything in order; send them your full script early. To see how the two fit together, read [the standard vow wording, line by line](/expert-advice/traditional-wedding-vows), or look at [a simple ceremony script with the legal lines marked](/expert-advice/simple-wedding-ceremony-script).

## How to write funny wedding vows in four parts

In this order:

1. **The opener.** Name a shared truth. "We have been together long enough to have a favourite argument."
2. **One or two light jokes.** Small, specific, affectionate. Two is plenty; three starts to feel like a set.
3. **The turn.** One short sentence that changes the temperature. "But here is what I actually want to say."
4. **The promise.** Plain, sincere, in the present tense. "I choose you, and I will keep choosing you."

[IMAGE: structure]

Agree on tone and length with your partner, not the words. If one of you writes a stand-up routine and the other writes a sonnet, the ceremony can feel lopsided. A five-minute conversation ("one or two jokes each, then serious") keeps the words a surprise and the ceremony even.

Write the promise first. It is the hardest part and the most important, and once it exists you will know which jokes set it up and which are just noise. For more examples by recipient, browse [vows written for her](/expert-advice/wedding-vows-for-her) and [vows written for him](/expert-advice/wedding-vows-for-him).

## Humour that lands and humour to avoid

Self-deprecating humour lands, because you are the punchline. Shared habits land, because the room recognises you both. Gentle teasing about something your partner is proud of (their spreadsheets, their sourdough, their parking) usually lands, because it is really a compliment.

Leave out:

- Ex-partners, in any form.
- Bodies, weight, ageing or appearance.
- Money, debts or who earns what.
- In-laws and family tensions, even affectionate ones.
- In-jokes nobody else gets. If it needs a backstory, it is not a vow line.
- Anything your partner would not want their grandmother to hear.

Remember who is sitting in the front rows. Parents, grandparents and the people helping pay for the day want to laugh, but they also want the ceremony to feel dignified. Kind humour gives them both.

Test your draft on one trusted friend, not the group chat. One honest listener will tell you which line falls flat; a group will mostly tell you it is great.

## Funny wedding vow examples you can adapt

These lines are our own, written for this guide as illustrations. Each pairs a joke with its sincere turn. Swap in your own details; the point is the shape, not the words.

- "You have read every review of every restaurant within ten kilometres before we book. I promise to trust your research, and to keep being glad I trusted you with my life."
- "I flew halfway around the world for this, and I am running on airline coffee and four hours of sleep. Which tells you how much I wanted to be standing here."
- "You alphabetise the spice rack, and I put the cumin back wherever it lands. I promise to keep learning your system, because you have made a home that makes sense of me."
- "You packed for this trip with a spreadsheet. I packed with optimism. I promise to bring the optimism to every plan you make, and to let you check my bag before we leave."
- "You answer work emails like each one is a personal invitation. I promise to be the message you always reply to first, today and every day after."
- "I will keep cooking my one signature dish until you can no longer pretend it is a treat. When that day comes, I will learn a second one, for you."
- "I promise to keep our houseplants alive, or at least to replace them before you notice. The things that matter, I will tend properly: you, us, and the life we are building."
- "You have never once let me navigate. That is fair. I promise to follow you anywhere, map or no map."
- "I will keep losing the playlist argument in the car. I will never lose sight of how lucky I am to be in the passenger seat beside you."
- "We have a favourite argument, and I promise to keep losing it graciously. I also promise that no argument will ever be bigger than us."

## Funny vows at a destination or symbolic ceremony

If you are marrying overseas, the legal side changes first. [The Attorney-General's Department says](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married) an Australian-authorised celebrant cannot perform a marriage overseas, so a ceremony your celebrant from home leads abroad is symbolic. A legal marriage abroad follows that country's rules, which its registry or embassy confirms for your passports, and is generally recognised in Australia if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid here. Compare [where Australians can legally marry abroad](/expert-advice/weddings-abroad-where-can-you-legally-get-married), and read [what happens to a marriage certificate issued overseas](/expert-advice/overseas-marriage-certificate).

One route is to marry legally at home first. You give a Notice of Intended Marriage to an authorised celebrant [at least one month and no more than 18 months before](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married) the legal ceremony, say the legal words in a small ceremony in Australia, then fly out for the celebration. Because a symbolic ceremony is not a legal marriage, the section 45 words are not required there, and your vows can be entirely your own. For one country's options side by side, see [civil, Catholic and symbolic ceremonies in Italy](/destination-weddings/civil-ceremony-in-italy-vs-catholic-and-symbolic-rites).

Humour travels less well than love does. Before the day, plan for:

- **Language.** If some guests do not share your language, print bilingual cards or have a friend translate the sincere lines afterwards. Puns and wordplay rarely survive translation; stories do.
- **Sound.** Outdoors, wind and waves swallow punchlines. Ask for a microphone for each of you, not only for the celebrant.
- **Light and timing.** A sunset ceremony is beautiful and also fast-moving. Keep vows tight so the light holds for the moment you finish.
- **A spare copy.** Give a printed copy of both vows to your planner or a trusted friend. Phones die; paper does not.

## How to deliver funny vows on the day

Read from a card or a small vow book, not your phone. A phone screen dims, buzzes and looks like you are checking messages in every photograph. A card also becomes a keepsake.

Pause after the laugh. Let it finish completely before you start the turn; if you talk over it, the sincere line gets lost. The pause is also where the emotion arrives, so let it.

Rehearse aloud twice: once alone, once with your celebrant at the rehearsal so they know where the laughs fall and can wait for them. Speak more slowly than feels natural. Nerves speed everyone up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can wedding vows be funny and still legal in Australia?

Yes. The legal words in an Australian ceremony are a few set sentences; everything else, including your vows to each other, can carry as much humour as you like.

### How long should funny wedding vows be?

Our guidance is about one minute each when spoken, roughly 120 to 180 words. Laughter adds time, so a 150-word vow with two good laughs can run longer than you expect. Time yourself reading aloud, then cut the weakest joke rather than the promise.

### Should we tell each other our vows before the wedding?

It is your call. Some couples like the surprise; others want no surprises at all. A middle path: keep the words private, but each send your vows to your celebrant, who can check that the two are a similar length and tone without spoiling either.

### Can we use funny vows at a vow renewal?

Yes, and with more freedom. [Attorney-General's Department guidelines for celebrants](https://www.ag.gov.au/sites/default/files/2020-03/guidelines-marriage-act-1961-authorised-celebrants.pdf) say a renewal of vows is not a marriage: no Notice of Intended Marriage or marriage certificate is prepared, and witnesses are not required. Your celebrant confirms what the ceremony can say. For ideas, see our [guide to writing vows for a renewal](/celebration-ideas/vow-renewal-vows).

### What if one of us is funny and the other is not?

Write to your own voice. A dry, sincere vow beside a funny one often makes both stronger, because the contrast is a portrait of you as a couple. Agree that neither vow will make the other the punchline, and let the funnier partner go first so the sincere vow lands last.
