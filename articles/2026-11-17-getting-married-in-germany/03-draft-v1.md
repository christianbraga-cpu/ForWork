key_takeaways:
  - "Two Australians with no German address can register to marry in Germany: under § 12 of the Civil Status Act, the Standesamt before which the marriage is to take place receives the registration."
  - "German civil law expects a certificate of capacity to marry from your home country, but the Australian Embassy Berlin stopped issuing Certificates of No Impediment for Germany on 1 December 2021 and has told German authorities so."
  - "The Embassy's guideline lists a birth certificate no older than six months with a certified German translation and a single status declaration from your state registry; the Standesamt sets the final list."
  - "Timing runs on six-month windows: Berlin accepts registration no earlier than six months ahead, the Standesamt's confirmation lapses after six months, and an exemption, which takes at least three weeks in Berlin, is valid for six months."
  - "Marrying legally in Australia first (NOIM at least one month ahead) takes every German legal step out of your wedding week."
faq:
  - q: "How much does it cost to get married in Germany?"
    a: "Each city sets its own registry fees. Berlin's include EUR 45 to register, plus EUR 45 for each partner whose foreign law must be considered, EUR 40 to marry at another Standesamt and EUR 12 per certificate. The Kammergericht charges EUR 15 to 305 for an exemption, depending on income. The Australian Embassy charges from AUD 90 per signature witnessed (2026 fee)."
  - q: "Do you have to speak German to get married in Germany?"
    a: "No, but you need help if you do not. Under § 1312 of the Civil Code, the registrar asks each of you individually whether you wish to marry. Munich asks couples who do not speak German well to bring a publicly appointed, sworn translator to the registration appointment, so plan for language support at both stages."
  - q: "Can you get married in a German castle?"
    a: "Only if your Standesamt can marry you there. Berlin charges EUR 75 to 150 for a marriage outside its official premises and notes that special wedding locations can add further costs. The Australian Embassy Berlin adds that an alternative location means higher registration fees and longer processing. Otherwise, marry at the Standesamt and celebrate at the castle."
  - q: "Is a marriage in Germany legally recognised in Australia?"
    a: "Generally yes. Smartraveller lists the exceptions: a marriage is not recognised where either person was already married, not old enough, too closely related or forced to marry. It also suggests talking to a lawyer to confirm an overseas certificate is valid in Australia."
  - q: "How long does it take to get married in Germany as a foreigner?"
    a: "German law sets no national processing time, so plan around its expiry dates instead. An exemption granted by the President of the Higher Regional Court is valid for six months only, and a home-country certificate of capacity loses force if you do not marry within six months of issue. Every document and approval has to line up inside that window."

# How to Get Married in Germany as a Foreigner

[IMAGE: hero]

Getting married in Germany is open to two Australians who live at home. Under § 12 of Germany's Civil Status Act (Personenstandsgesetz), if neither of you has a residence or habitual abode in Germany, the registry office (Standesamt) before which the marriage is to take place is responsible for receiving your registration to marry. The legal ceremony is the civil one before a registrar. Confirm the requirements with the Standesamt where you register, and for your own passports with DFAT.

Sources current as at November 2026.

## Getting married in Germany: can foreigners marry legally?

Yes, by registering with the Standesamt in the town where you want to marry. German civil law says a marriage is concluded only when the couple declare before the registrar that they wish to marry each other (§ 1310 of the Civil Code, the Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch or BGB), so a church or symbolic ceremony alone is not a legal wedding.

You have two routes:

| | Marry legally in Germany | Marry in Australia, celebrate in Germany |
|---|---|---|
| Who it suits | Couples who want the legal moment to happen in Germany | Couples who would rather keep every legal step at home |
| Where the legal part happens | Before a German registrar | Before an authorised celebrant in Australia |
| Extra paperwork | Registration with the Standesamt, translated Australian documents, the capacity-to-marry question | A Notice of Intended Marriage (NOIM) |
| The Germany day | Your civil ceremony, then any celebration | A symbolic ceremony with no legal effect, shaped as you like |

## Residency and eligibility for getting married in Germany

German law places the registration, not a residence test, at the centre. Berlin's service portal puts it plainly: if neither of you has a residence in Germany, the Standesamt where the marriage is to take place is responsible. Standesamt I in Berlin, which the Federal Foreign Office names as the office issuing certificates to Germans who never lived in Germany, is not where a foreign couple registers. [§ 12 of the Civil Status Act](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/pstg/__12.html) asks you to prove by public documents your civil status, your residence, your nationality and, if either of you was married or in a registered partnership before, that the last one was dissolved. If that last marriage was not concluded at a German Standesamt, any earlier ones must be shown as dissolved too.

**Capacity to marry.** [§ 1309 of the Civil Code](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__1309.html) says a person whose capacity to marry is governed by foreign law should not marry before producing a certificate from their home state confirming there is no impediment under that state's law (an Ehefähigkeitszeugnis). The same section lets the President of the Higher Regional Court (Oberlandesgericht) for the registering Standesamt's district grant an exemption (Befreiung), intended among others for nationals of states whose authorities do not issue such certificates. You apply through the Standesamt that receives your registration.

In Berlin, the [Kammergericht](https://www.berlin.de/gerichte/kammergericht/das-gericht/ehefaehigkeitsverfahren/) (Berlin's Higher Regional Court) explains that its President checks, in place of the foreign authority, whether your home law raises any impediment. Its list of countries that regularly issue the certificate is not complete and does not include Australia, so expect your Standesamt to raise the exemption. In Berlin, processing takes at least three weeks, longer if further documents are needed, and only continues once the fee is paid.

The Australian side is specific. The [Australian Embassy Berlin's marriage page](https://germany.embassy.gov.au/belngerman/MarriageGer.html) says the Embassy and the Consulate-General in Frankfurt stopped issuing Certificates of No Impediment to Marriage (CNIs) for Germany on 1 December 2021, and that German authorities have been advised you should not be required to provide one. If your Standesamt still asks for a CNI, the Embassy's [notarial services](https://germany.embassy.gov.au/beln/notarial_services.html) page says you can email it for a written statement to forward to the registry office. The Embassy adds that requirements may vary with regional interpretations by German courts.

**Age and status.** § 1303 of the Civil Code says no one may marry before coming of age (18), and a marriage with a person under 16 cannot validly be concluded.
**Same-sex couples.** § 1353 of the Civil Code defines marriage as concluded for life by two persons of different or the same sex (see [where same-sex couples can legally marry abroad](/expert-advice/where-can-same-sex-couples-legally-marry-abroad)).

**Entry.** [Smartraveller's Germany page](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/germany) says Australians can visit the Schengen area, which includes Germany, without a visa for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, shared across every Schengen country, so a honeymoon in Italy or France counts too. Under the EU Entry/Exit System you give fingerprints and a photo on arrival. Smartraveller advises passports be valid for at least six months beyond the trip (see [whether a wedding trip needs a special visa](/expert-advice/do-you-need-a-wedding-visa-to-get-married-abroad)).

## Prefer to marry in Australia? Marry at home, celebrate in Germany

The [Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married) sets the steps. You give a NOIM to an authorised celebrant no more than 18 months and at least one month before the legal ceremony. The celebrant meets each of you in person and must be physically present with you and two witnesses over 18 (no video-link weddings), then lodges the paperwork with your state or territory registry within 14 days. For the detail, see [how to marry legally in Australia first](/expert-advice/marriage-licence-requirements).

This route removes the Standesamt registration, the German translations, the capacity-to-marry question and the German certificate from your plans, and gives you an Australian certificate for any name change. Your Germany day is then a celebration, in any setting your venue allows.

## Documents from Australia

The Standesamt where you register sets the final list and can ask for further documents; confirm it in writing before ordering anything. As a guideline, the Australian Embassy Berlin says a registry office may ask Australians who live in Australia for:

| Document | Issued by | Preparing it for Germany (Embassy guideline) |
|---|---|---|
| Passport | Australian Passport Office | Proof of identity and nationality; check names match every other paper |
| Birth certificate | Your state or territory registry | Original, no older than six months, certified German translation, possibly an apostille |
| Single status declaration (Ledigkeitsbescheinigung) | Your state or territory registry | Original with a certified German translation; replaces the CNI the Embassy no longer issues |
| Proof of residence | Whatever the Standesamt accepts for people living outside Germany | Ask the Standesamt what it accepts |
| Salary or bank statement | Your employer or bank | The Embassy says the fee depends on your financial status |
| If divorced: divorce certificate; if widowed: former partner's death certificate | The court that granted it; state or territory registry | Original, translated if applicable |

[IMAGE: documents]

The six-month limit on the birth certificate is the tight one. Order it, apostille it if asked and have it translated close to the date you register, not a year ahead.

The Embassy in Berlin and the Consulate-General in Frankfurt offer apostilles and authentications of Australian public documents, certified copies and signature witnessing. Every service except apostilles is in person and by appointment. Witnessing costs from AUD 90 per signature and an apostille from AUD 105 (2026 fees), and notarial fees rise on 1 January each year. In Australia, DFAT apostilles cost from AUD 105 (2026 fee), per [DFAT's notarial fees](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services).

## Translations and apostilles

Germany has been a party to the Apostille Convention since 13 February 1966 and Australia since 16 March 1995, according to the [Hague Conference status table](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/status-table/?cid=41), so an Australian public document for Germany is legalised by apostille rather than consular legalisation.

The Embassy's marriage page says that if a court doubts the authenticity of Australian documents, it may request an apostille, available from DFAT in Australia or from the Embassy in Berlin or the Consulate-General in Frankfurt. DFAT says [processing times vary](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-your-documents-legalised), with delays in peak holiday periods, so build that into the six-month window.

The order that keeps documents current:

1. Certificate from the issuing registry or court.
2. Apostille from DFAT, if the Standesamt asks for one (Berlin attaches it directly to the original).
3. German translation by the kind of translator your Standesamt accepts.
4. Lodge with the Standesamt.

Who translates depends on the city. [Berlin's registration page](https://service.berlin.de/dienstleistung/318962/) requires documents not in German to be translated by an interpreter or translator sworn in Germany, and wants originals that have not been altered, perforated or laminated. [Munich's registry office page](https://stadt.muenchen.de/service/info/anmeldung-einer-eheschliessung-mit-auslandsbezug/10396802/) accepts translations by a translator recognised in Germany or at a German mission abroad. The Australian Embassy does not provide translations. Spell every name exactly as your passport does on every form.

## Registration and timeline

Registration (Anmeldung zur Eheschließung, the formal notice that you intend to marry) happens at the Standesamt in your wedding town, and the rules are local:

- **Berlin** asks for a personal consultation first, because the documents depend on your circumstances. You can apply no earlier than six months before your date, and both of you register in person. If one of you cannot attend, the other may register alone with written authority; if both are prevented for important reasons, registration can be in writing or through an authorised representative.
- **Munich** asks couples with a foreign link first to book a paid information appointment on the documents (the fee is credited if you then register). Appointments can be booked up to three months ahead, and you both attend together with all documents.

Once the Standesamt finds no impediment, it tells you the marriage may take place. If more than six months then pass without the wedding, [§ 13 of the Civil Status Act](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/pstg/__13.html) says you must register again. Berlin adds that within those six months you may marry at any Standesamt in Germany. Your registration and your wedding date are therefore one decision.

Until the Standesamt confirms in writing that it will register you, and whether you need the exemption, hold off on paying venue deposits.

As an illustration only (the Standesamt sets the real timeline):

| When | Marry legally in Germany | Marry in Australia |
|---|---|---|
| 12 months out | Choose your wedding town; write to its Standesamt with the questions below | Give the NOIM to your celebrant (no more than 18 months and at least one month ahead) |
| 9 to 7 months out | Consultation or information appointment; agree the document list | Plan the Germany celebration |
| 7 to 6 months out | Order birth certificates and single status declarations; apostille if asked; sworn translation | Fix the legal ceremony date |
| Within six months of the wedding (Berlin's earliest point) | Register in person; exemption application if needed (at least three weeks in Berlin); receive the notice that you may marry | Legal ceremony in Australia, a few weeks before you fly |
| Wedding week | Civil ceremony with a sworn interpreter if needed; order certificates | The celebration (no legal step) |

**Questions to ask the Standesamt**

- Does Australia issue a certificate you accept, or do we need the exemption?
- What is your full document list for Australians living in Australia, and does any document need an apostille?
- How recent must each document be, and how early can we register with you?
- What are your fees, if you are not in Berlin?
- Can the registrar marry us outside the registry office, and at which locations?
- Does our German marriage certificate need an apostille for use in Australia?

## The civil ceremony in Germany

A German registrar (Standesbeamter) marries you. § 1311 of the Civil Code says you must both make your declaration in person and while present together, and it cannot be made subject to a condition or time limit, so there is no marriage by proxy. Witnesses are optional: § 1312 says the marriage can take place with one or two witnesses if you wish. [Berlin's Standesamt page](https://www.berlin.de/standesamt/ehe/) adds that witnesses must be adults with a valid ID card or passport (see [who can act as a witness and what they do](/expert-advice/wedding-witnesses)).

Language is a firm rule in Berlin: if either of you lacks sufficient German, a sworn interpreter is required, and you arrange them. On the day, both of you, your witnesses and the interpreter show a valid passport or ID card.

Before choosing a venue, find out whether your Standesamt can marry you there and how far it is from the registry office. Berlin issues both a German marriage certificate and a multilingual international one; agree before the day who orders yours and how it reaches you in Australia.

## Religious and symbolic ceremonies

Because German law concludes a marriage only before the registrar, a church or symbolic ceremony has no legal effect on its own. The legal part happens at the Standesamt or in Australia; any religious or symbolic ceremony you add is for meaning, not for the law. For ideas, read about [the customs behind a German celebration](/expert-advice/german-wedding-traditions).

## Recognition and name change back in Australia

If you married in Germany, [Smartraveller's advice on marrying overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) says the marriage cannot be registered in Australia, but in most cases it is recognised if it was valid where it happened and would have been valid under Australian law. The certificate is usually accepted as proof of the marriage. The Attorney-General's Department points to Part VA of the Marriage Act 1961 and notes that some states and territories do not accept overseas certificates for a change of name. See [what to do with a marriage certificate issued overseas](/expert-advice/overseas-marriage-certificate). If you married in Australia first, your Australian certificate is the record.

You can keep travelling on your current passport; the [Australian Passport Office](https://www.passports.gov.au/change-name-or-gender) recommends booking travel in the name on it. For a passport in a married name, a spouse born in Australia who married overseas needs a change of name certificate from their state or territory registry. A foreign certificate is accepted only where no Australian equivalent exists, must be legalised, and a non-English one must be translated in full by an approved service.

In NSW, a person legally married in Australia can take their spouse's family name without registering a change of name, and the NSW registry wants foreign documents not in English translated by a NAATI-accredited translator. Check your own registry.

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to get married in Germany?

Each city sets its own registry fees. Berlin's include EUR 45 to register, plus EUR 45 for each partner whose foreign law must be considered, EUR 40 to marry at another Standesamt and EUR 12 per certificate. The Kammergericht charges EUR 15 to 305 for an exemption, depending on income. The Australian Embassy charges from AUD 90 per signature witnessed (2026 fee).

### Do you have to speak German to get married in Germany?

No, but you need help if you do not. Under § 1312 of the Civil Code, the registrar asks each of you individually whether you wish to marry. Munich asks couples who do not speak German well to bring a publicly appointed, sworn translator to the registration appointment, so plan for language support at both stages.

### Can you get married in a German castle?

Only if your Standesamt can marry you there. Berlin charges EUR 75 to 150 for a marriage outside its official premises and notes that special wedding locations can add further costs. The Australian Embassy Berlin adds that an alternative location means higher registration fees and longer processing. Otherwise, marry at the Standesamt and celebrate at the castle.

### Is a marriage in Germany legally recognised in Australia?

Generally yes. Smartraveller lists the exceptions: a marriage is not recognised where either person was already married, not old enough, too closely related or forced to marry. It also suggests talking to a lawyer to confirm an overseas certificate is valid in Australia.

### How long does it take to get married in Germany as a foreigner?

German law sets no national processing time, so plan around its expiry dates instead. An exemption granted by the President of the Higher Regional Court is valid for six months only, and a home-country certificate of capacity loses force if you do not marry within six months of issue. Every document and approval has to line up inside that window.
