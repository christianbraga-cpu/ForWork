# Paying Wedding Vendors Overseas in Another Currency

[IMAGE: paying-wedding-vendors-overseas-couple-planner-terrace-documents-01.webp]

The venue has accepted your date, the photographer has sent a contract, and every invoice is in euros. Paying wedding vendors overseas means sending large sums, months apart, to people you may have met only on a video call, in a currency you do not earn. It is also where a few steady habits remove the most worry.

This guide covers how deposits and balances run abroad, who carries the exchange rate movement between them, what to compare before each transfer, and how to check an invoice before you pay it. Official guidance is linked where we rely on it. Sources current as at November 2026.

## What changes when you pay a wedding vendor overseas

At home, a deposit moves between two Australian accounts under Australian rules. Abroad, four things change at once: the currency, the cost of moving the money, the time it takes to arrive, and the law your contract sits under.

Moneysmart, ASIC's money guidance site, says to [look for a good exchange rate and check the fees when sending money overseas](https://moneysmart.gov.au/banking/sending-money-overseas). Providers choose the exchange rate they use and also charge fees, and Moneysmart says the costs depend on how much money you send, where you send it and how you send it.

Our guide to [the costs a headline quote can leave out](/destination-weddings/hidden-costs-destination-wedding) gives the wedding version. Before transferring a deposit, review the payment schedule, cancellation policy, refund conditions and accepted currencies. If paying internationally, compare the costs of bank transfers and card payments rather than assuming the quoted amount is the amount that will leave your account.

Distance changes the checks, too. What you would settle at a counter at home happens by email and phone, across time zones, so build that lag into every deadline.

### What the taxes on an invoice mean

Invoices abroad can carry notations you will not see at home. Three examples from our cost guides, as each words them:

- Our [Phuket cost guide](/real-weddings/wedding-in-thailand-cost) notes that resort quotations may use the notation '++', with a common hotel calculation applying a 10% service charge followed by 7% VAT. Where both apply in sequence, it puts the combined increase at 17.7%.
- Our [Dubai cost guide](/destination-weddings/dubai-wedding-cost) notes that Palazzo Versace's published rate included a 7% municipality fee, a 10% service charge and 5% VAT, and suggests asking whether a quoted rate includes those charges or adds them on top.
- Our [guide to a small wedding in Italy](/destination-weddings/small-wedding-in-italy-cost) says VAT is 22 percent on most services and 10 percent on hotel stays and restaurant meals.

Each is one page's example, not a rule for your wedding. For every quote, ask whether tax and service are in the figure or added on top, and have the answer in writing.

## How deposits, instalments and final balances usually run abroad

A vendor contract abroad sets its own payment schedule, and no two need match. Expect a deposit to hold your date, possibly one or more interim payments, and a final balance due by a date the contract names. Your venue, planner and photographer may each run on a different timetable, in a different currency.

Terms can be firm. Our hidden-costs guide notes that Le Ciel in Santorini requires a 50 percent deposit of €2,100, with the remaining venue fee due 365 days before the wedding, and that payments are non-refundable and by bank transfer only (EUR, as our guide words it, no year stated). That is one venue's terms, not a norm. It does show the three things to find in every contract: how much, by when, and by what method.

Keep any refundable bond, such as a security deposit against damage, on its own line.

Then read what happens if plans change. The ACCC's guidance on [paying for services you have not received](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/not-receiving-products-or-services-that-are-paid-for) says that under the Australian Consumer Law, businesses must not accept payment if they don't intend to supply, or if they know, or should know, that they won't be able to supply by the promised date or within a reasonable time. Where a third party prevents supply, your right to a refund will generally depend on the terms and conditions of the agreement. Its page on [refunds and cancelling a service](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/problem-with-a-product-or-service-you-bought/repair-replace-refund-cancel) adds that the consumer guarantees give no refund if you simply changed your mind.

That guidance is written for Australian businesses. With a vendor abroad, treat the contract as the document that governs refunds, and read its cancellation and postponement terms before the deposit leaves your account. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

## Which currency to pay in, and who carries the exchange rate risk

If an invoice is in euros, the euro figure is fixed and the Australian dollar cost is not. Between deposit and balance, which can be a year or more apart, the rate can move either way. With a quote in the vendor's currency, that movement sits with you.

Picture a venue balance in euros, due a year after your deposit. The invoice figure will not change; its cost in Australian dollars depends on the rate on the day you pay, which nobody can tell you when you sign. That is why a currency buffer belongs in your budget as its own line, apart from contingency.

A quote in Australian dollars moves the conversion, not the cost: someone has still converted, and their rate is in the price. Ask which rate an AUD quote uses and whether it is fixed until you pay. Moneysmart's list of features to compare includes an exchange rate guarantee: whether the rate stays the same or can change.

## What to compare between a bank transfer, a card and a transfer service

Moneysmart says it's a good idea to [shop around before you send money overseas](https://moneysmart.gov.au/banking/sending-money-overseas), because different companies can charge very different prices. Its list of features to compare includes:

- **Exchange rate:** how much your money is worth in another country, and whether the advertised rate is the rate you get.
- **Transfer fees and commission:** whether the conversion fee is a flat rate, a percentage or both; whether the receiver pays any fees; and the 'margin' fee to convert your currency.
- **Transfer time:** it could be instant, take a few minutes, or take up to five business days.

Ask each vendor whether their bank charges them to receive a payment and who covers it, so the amount that arrives matches the amount due. When a deposit holds a date, send early enough that it lands before the deadline in the vendor's time zone, and keep the confirmation.

Moneysmart also notes that in Australia, money transfer services must be registered with AUSTRAC (the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre), and says to check that a money transfer business is registered before you use it.

Cards work differently. Moneysmart's glossary defines an [international transaction fee](https://moneysmart.gov.au/glossary/international-transaction-fee) as a fee that may be charged by credit card providers for purchases made with overseas-based merchants, or with Australian-based merchants who process payments overseas. It adds that the fee may still be charged even if the transaction is in Australian dollars. Protections such as a chargeback, where a disputed card payment is reversed, depend on your card issuer, so read its terms before you pay a deposit by card.

## When a vendor asks for the balance in cash

If a vendor asks for part of the balance in cash, treat it like any other payment term and settle the detail well before you fly.

- **Put it in the contract:** the amount, the currency, the date and the person who will receive it.
- **Ask for a written receipt** for every cash payment, showing the vendor's name, the date and the amount.
- **Speak to your bank before you travel** about how you will access the funds abroad, and plan where the cash is kept until it is handed over.
- **Choose one trusted person**, a planner or a family member, to hand it over on the day, so neither of you is counting notes in your wedding clothes.

Keep those receipts with your contracts and policy documents, in case you ever need to make a claim. Tips, if you give them, are simplest in local currency, in named envelopes handed over by that same person.

## How to protect every payment from invoice scams

Wedding payments are large, and they often arrive as emailed invoices from businesses you have never visited. In an April 2024 warning about [fake invoices from scammers impersonating businesses](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/beware-of-fake-invoices-from-scammers-impersonating-businesses), the ACCC urged Australians to check payment details directly with a business before paying an emailed invoice. It said travel companies and their customers had been targeted.

The ACCC describes how it works. Scammers gain access to a business's email account, or change the email address, and modify the payment details on the invoice, so you pay the scammer instead of the business. If you reply to the email to query the change, the scammer will respond justifying it. Invoices can look legitimate because scammers copy logos and ABNs.

Its advice is to take the time to call the business on a number you have found yourself to confirm that the payment details are correct. For a wedding abroad, make that a routine:

1. Before the first transfer, call the vendor on a number from their website or your signed contract, never the invoice email, and read back the account name and number.
2. Treat any change to bank details as a stop sign until you have confirmed it the same way.
3. Do not let a deadline rush you; a day spent confirming costs less than a lost deposit.
4. Note the date of each call and who confirmed the details.

[IMAGE: destination-wedding-couple-phone-call-checking-bank-details-01.webp]

If something feels wrong, the ACCC says to act quickly: if you have shared financial information or transferred money, contact your bank immediately, and report it to Scamwatch. Moneysmart warns that if you send money to someone who turns out to be a scammer, it's almost impossible to get your money back. For a problem with a genuine transfer, contact the bank or transfer business first; if you are not happy and they are a member of the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA), Moneysmart says you can complain to AFCA.

## Matching each payment to its contract, cancellation terms and insurance

Every payment should trace back to a signed contract, a cancellation date and a line in your records.

**Who signs and who pays.** If a parent is paying for the welcome dinner or the band, agree whose name goes on that contract and who the vendor deals with on changes. Put it in writing, so the payer, the signer and the vendor all know.

**Cancellation dates beside payment dates.** For each vendor, list every payment next to the date it stops being refundable under the contract. A deposit that is non-refundable from the day it is paid carries more risk than a balance with a later cut-off.

**Records.** Keep the contract, each invoice, each transfer confirmation, your note of each phone check and every cash receipt in one place. [A vendor list with contact and payment fields](/planning/wedding-vendor-list-template) holds the details for each supplier, and [a spreadsheet that tracks each deposit and balance](/expert-advice/wedding-expenses-spreadsheet) shows what is still due.

**Insurance.** Smartraveller says [the right travel insurance is as essential as a passport](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/travel-essentials/travel-insurance) for overseas travel, for every traveller. Once the first deposit is paid, look at what wedding cover would protect it and what it would not. ## Paying vendors overseas, your questions answered

### How do you pay wedding vendors in another country?

Follow each contract's payment schedule and method. Before each payment, compare the exchange rate, fees and transfer time, confirm the vendor's bank details by phone on a number you found yourself, and keep proof of every payment with the contract.

### Should I pay my overseas wedding vendors in AUD or their currency?

With an invoice in the vendor's currency, the amount in that currency is fixed and your Australian dollar cost moves with the rate until you pay. A quote in Australian dollars still involves a conversion, so ask which rate it uses and whether it is fixed. Moneysmart notes that a card international transaction fee may still be charged even if the transaction is in Australian dollars.

### How much deposit do overseas wedding vendors ask for?

It varies, and each contract sets its own amount and dates. Before you sign, find the deposit, any interim payments, the balance date, the payment method and the point at which each payment stops being refundable.

### Is it safe to pay a wedding vendor overseas by bank transfer?

It can be, if you confirm the details first. The ACCC urges Australians to [check payment details directly with a business](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/beware-of-fake-invoices-from-scammers-impersonating-businesses) before paying an emailed invoice, by calling a number you have found yourself. Moneysmart warns that if you send money to a scammer, it's almost impossible to get it back.

### What if my vendor abroad wants to be paid in cash?

Treat it like any other payment term. Have the amount, currency, date and recipient written into the contract, ask for a written receipt for every cash payment, choose one trusted person to hand it over, and keep the receipts with your contracts.
