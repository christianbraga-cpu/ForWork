# Bali Marriage Religion Requirement Explained

[IMAGE: hero]

The Bali marriage religion requirement is real, and it sits in Indonesia's Marriage Law rather than in any venue's rules. Law No. 1 of 1974 makes a marriage legitimate when it is performed according to the laws of the couple's religions and beliefs, and the Australian Embassy in Indonesia reads that as both partners holding the same religion. If that does not fit you, there is a practical alternative: marry legally in Australia and celebrate in Bali.

This guide sets out what the official texts say, in their own words, and leaves the interpretation to the authorities who decide. Requirements change and can vary by province, so confirm everything for your own passports with the office named in each section.

Sources current as at November 2026.

## The Bali marriage religion requirement, in plain words

Article 2(1) of Law No. 1 of 1974 on Marriage reads, [as quoted in English by Indonesia's Constitutional Court](https://en.mkri.id/news/details/2025-11-12/Petitioner_Claims_Ban_of_Interfaith_Marriage_Discriminatory): "A marriage shall be legitimate if it is performed according to the laws of the respective religions and beliefs of the parties concerned." Article 2(2) adds that every marriage must be registered according to statutory regulations.

The [Australian Embassy in Indonesia](https://indonesia.embassy.gov.au/jakt/MarriageInd.html) puts the practical effect this way, in a page it describes as a general guide: "Under Indonesian Law No.1 of 1974 concerning marriage (the 'Marriage Law'), both parties must hold the same religion, if not, one party must convert to the other religion."

The Embassy adds that, for a marriage to be legal, "it must be conducted according to the religion and conform to the laws of the countries of the parties involved." Your own country's law matters as much as Indonesia's.

One point to settle early: the Attorney-General's Department says an Australian-authorised celebrant cannot perform a marriage overseas. A Bali ceremony led by your celebrant from home is symbolic, however beautiful, unless the Indonesian legal process is also followed.

[IMAGE: rule]

## Which religions are recognised for marriage

The Australian Embassy says that, generally, Indonesian nationals, and persons of any other nationality, may marry in Indonesia "provided they hold a religion recognised by the Indonesian Government (Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism or Christian)."

That is the Embassy's own short list, quoted as it appears. Ask the Civil Registry Office (Kantor Catatan Sipil) or the Office of Religious Affairs (Kantor Urusan Agama, or KUA) which religions and beliefs it recognises, and what evidence of religion it needs from each of you.

## Couples of different faiths, or no religious faith

One official statement on this is a decision of Indonesia's Constitutional Court. In short, the Court dismissed a challenge in 2023, so Article 2 still reads as quoted above. In case 24/PUU-XX/2022, a petitioner asked the Court to review Article 2 and Article 8 letter f of the Marriage Law. The Court decided on 31 January 2023: "To dismiss the Petitioner's petition entirely." According to [the Court's summary of decision 24/PUU-XX/2022](https://en.mkri.id/download/summary/summary_1740_1693294648_65c504259ee7adf223a9.pdf), it "remains in its stance on the constitutionality of valid marriages, which are those carried out according to their religion and belief and that every marriage must be registered in accordance with statutory regulations."

The same summary quotes two earlier decisions on how responsibility is divided: "the validity of a marriage is a religious domain through religious institutions or organizations that are authorized or have the authority to provide religious interpretations." And: "religion determines the validity of marriage and at the same time the state determines the administrative validity of marriage within the legal corridor."

On registration ordered by a court, the Court said that even though an elucidation in the Population Administration Law describes such marriages as being between people of different religions, "it does not necessarily mean that the state recognizes interfaith marriages because the state, in this case, follows the interpretation that has been carried out by religious institutions or organizations."

For couples who do not hold a religion, how the Embassy's condition that persons may marry "provided they hold a religion recognised by the Indonesian Government" applies is for the Civil Registry Office, the KUA or the Indonesian Embassy or Consulate-General in Australia to answer.

One firm piece of advice: do not pay a venue deposit for a legal Bali wedding until the deciding authority has answered your questions in writing. A written answer, dated and from the office itself, is the document you want in hand before money leaves your account.

## How the legal ceremony and registration work

The Constitutional Court's summary says citizens have the right to register a marriage "at the civil registry office for non-Muslim couples and at the Office of Religious Affairs (Kantor Urusan Agama or KUA) for Muslim couples."

The Australian Embassy describes the same split:

- **Muslim marriages** are performed by the KUA, which issues a Marriage Book (Buku Nikah) as evidence. Registering with the Civil Registry Office is usually not required, but the Embassy recommends a Civil Registry Marriage Certificate if you plan to relocate to another country.
- **Other marriages** (Christian, Hindu and others): you lodge a Notice of Intention to Marry with the Civil Registry Office at least 10 days before, then register the marriage there afterwards for it to be legal. The Embassy notes requirements can vary from province to province.

Both offices normally require a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI) from the Australian mission responsible for the area. For Bali, that is the Australian Consulate-General in Bali, in Denpasar; Smartraveller lists its contact details on [its Indonesia travel advice page](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/asia/indonesia). DFAT also explains [how to apply for a CNI](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-certificate-no-impediment-marriage). Timing matters: "We do not place an expiry date on the CNI," the Embassy says, "however the local Civil Registry Office do not normally accept CNIs that were issued more than 3 months prior."

The Embassy is also clear about its limits: "Neither DFAT nor its missions overseas can advise on other countries' specific requirements for Australians to get married overseas." Indonesian requirements come from the Indonesian side.

This is where the invisible work sits: originals and photocopies of passports, birth certificates and four 4x6cm photos of you as a couple, any translations the office asks for, a CNI timed to fall inside that three-month window, and office appointments in the same week your guests are landing. For the wider choice between this route and a legal ceremony at home, see [what Bali packages include and the two legal routes](/destination-weddings/bali-wedding-packages).

## The alternative: marry in Australia, celebrate in Bali

The route that sidesteps the religion requirement is to make the marriage legal at home. You marry before an authorised celebrant in Australia, and the Bali ceremony becomes a symbolic one.

Australia has no marriage licence. The [Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married) says the formal step is giving a Notice of Intended Marriage (NOIM) to an authorised celebrant at least one month and no more than 18 months before the wedding. Your celebrant must meet each of you separately and in person, then be physically present with you and two witnesses over 18 for the legal ceremony. There are no video-link weddings.

Hold the legal ceremony a few weeks before you fly, and the Bali week carries no legal step at all. That removes a great deal of Indonesian paperwork: no CNI, no notice to the Civil Registry Office, no registration, and no religion requirement for the legal marriage. Your celebration in Bali can then reflect both families, both traditions or neither, because your legal marriage happens at home.

The legal marriage is Australian, so there is no overseas certificate to deal with. If you do marry in Indonesia instead, the Attorney-General's Department says an overseas marriage is generally recognised in Australia if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid here; [Smartraveller's advice on marrying overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) adds that you cannot register it in Australia. ## Questions to ask the Civil Registry Office or KUA

Put these to the Civil Registry Office of the regency where you would marry, or to the KUA for a Muslim marriage. If you are still in Australia, the Indonesian Embassy or a Consulate-General can point you to the right office. Ask for each answer in writing.

1. Which religions and beliefs does your office recognise for a marriage, and what evidence of religion do you accept from a foreign national?
2. If we hold different religions, or one of us holds none, will your office accept our marriage for registration?
3. Has any ruling since 2023 changed how you handle applications like ours?
4. Which religious authority must conduct our ceremony before you will register it, and do you need a document from them?
5. How many days' notice does your office need in our regency, and can we lodge it before we arrive?
6. Can guests travelling with us act as our witnesses, or must they live in Indonesia?
7. Which documents need a certified translation, and do you accept translations made in Australia?
8. How long after the ceremony is the certificate issued, and can it be collected before we fly home?

The Australian Consulate-General in Bali confirms the CNI: what it needs from you, how long it takes, and what it charges. If you marry at home first, your celebrant confirms the NOIM and ceremony steps. Neither we nor any advisor can confirm legal requirements on an authority's behalf.

## FAQ

### Is religion required for a civil marriage in Indonesia?

The Australian Embassy lists officers of the Civil Registry Office among those who may perform marriages, alongside ministers of religion and the KUA. It also says that, for a marriage to be legal, it must be conducted according to the religion. Confirm the steps with the Civil Registry Office where you would marry.

### How many witnesses does a legal wedding in Bali need?

[Government Regulation No. 9 of 1975](https://peraturan.bpk.go.id/Details/67678/pp-no-9-tahun-1975), which implements the Marriage Law, says the marriage takes place before the Registrar, following the procedure of each religion or belief, and is attended by two witnesses. Your Civil Registry Office confirms the witness rules.

### Can we marry at the Australian Consulate-General in Bali?

No. The Australian Embassy says you cannot marry at the Embassy or a Consulate-General, and you do not register an Indonesian marriage with the Embassy afterwards. For a Bali marriage, the Consulate-General issues the Certificate of No Impediment, usually while you wait if your documents are correct and complete.

### Can we marry in Bali if one of us is divorced?

Not until the divorce is final. The Australian Embassy says you cannot marry before a divorce decree absolute is issued, and its document list for non-Muslim marriages includes proof that a previous marriage was dissolved, or the death certificate of a former partner. Bring originals and photocopies, and check the list with your Civil Registry Office.

### Can we lodge our Notice of Intended Marriage from overseas?

Yes. The Attorney-General's Department says that since 12 June 2024 the notice can be witnessed by audio-visual link, and a couple outside Australia can complete it before an authorised witness overseas, such as an Australian consular officer or a notary public. That overseas witness cannot be a celebrant.
