Choosing wedding flowers in season starts with a date, not a mood board. The month you marry decides which stems are at their freshest, which will need to travel, and how much room your florist has to move if a crop comes in late.

What grows well varies with the region, the grower and the year, so rather than a fixed list of stems, this guide follows the Australian year, season by season and month by month, with the questions worth putting to your florist for each one. It then answers the question of whether to choose local stems or order imported ones, and what changes when the wedding is overseas.

## Key takeaways

- Set the date before you settle the design, because the month decides which flowers are at their peak, as with waratah, whose flowering is concentrated in spring.
- Australian summer is December, January and February, winter is June, July and August, and the northern tropics run on a wet and dry season instead.
- Imported cut flowers must meet Australian biosecurity import conditions, so ask your florist which stems are local, which are imported and what the backup is.
- For a wedding abroad, the season follows the destination, so the florist there is the one to ask.

## Why choose wedding flowers in season

A flower cut close to home and close to its natural moment spends less time in transit and more time looking the way it should. Petals hold their shape, stems drink well, and arrangements last through a long evening rather than tiring by the speeches.

Season also shapes the look. Stems that grow at the same time of year tend to share a mood: the colour of the light, the warmth or coolness of the day, the feel of the venue. Building a design around what is ready gives a bouquet and a ceremony arch a natural coherence that is hard to force out of season.

Then there is flexibility. When your florist works with what is plentiful, there is room to swap one stem for another on the morning of delivery without the design falling apart. Ask for a flower at the edge of its season, or far from it, and the margin narrows.

None of this rules out a favourite bloom. It simply means knowing, early, whether that bloom will be at its peak on your date, or whether it will need to be brought in.

## How the seasons run in Australia

Australia's seasons run opposite to the northern hemisphere's. The [Bureau of Meteorology](https://www.bom.gov.au/news-and-media/solstices-equinoxes-and-the-seasons) defines summer as the warmest three months, December, January and February, and winter as the coolest, June, July and August. Spring and autumn are the transitions between them. For Australia, the Bureau starts each season on the first of the month: 1 March, 1 June, 1 September and 1 December.

The northern tropics follow a different pattern. There, as the [Bureau of Meteorology](https://www.bom.gov.au/news-and-media/solstices-equinoxes-and-the-seasons) sets out, the year divides into a wet season from October to April and a dry season from May to September, and the four-season model does not apply. If you are marrying in the far north, plan your flowers around wet and dry, not summer and winter.

This hub is written for weddings in Australia. If you are reading from Europe, North America or anywhere else above the equator, every season here is reversed: an Australian December wedding falls in summer, not winter.

Each month below gives you questions to ask, not a fixed list of stems. Take them to your first meeting with your florist and ask each question for your month. Where we have a deeper guide for a month or season, it is linked.

### Summer in December, January and February

**December.** Summer begins on 1 December, and the month fills quickly with Christmas parties and end-of-year events. Ask your florist how busy their calendar already is in the week of your date, and how arrangements will be kept cool between the studio and the venue. If the ceremony is outdoors in the afternoon, ask which stems hold their shape in full sun, and which belong inside. Our notes on [December](/8-in-season-flowers-for-december) flowers go into more detail.

**January.** High summer brings long evenings and heat that can last well after dark. Ask how close to the ceremony the florist can set up, so arrangements spend as little time as possible in warm air. Ask whether the venue has water and shade for buckets during the day, and whether a bouquet can be kept chilled until the moment you leave for the ceremony.

**February.** Mid February brings Valentine's Day, which [AgriFutures Australia](https://agrifutures.com.au/related-projects/farm-based-methods-for-control-of-flowering-in-waratahs/) names with Christmas as a peak demand period for flowers. If your date falls near it, ask your florist early how that week affects their supply and their team. Late summer is also a good moment to ask what is at its peak locally and what is starting to fade, so the design leans on stems with time left in them.

### Autumn in March, April and May

**March.** Autumn starts on 1 March. Early in the month, some summer stems may still be available, so ask your florist which ones are likely to carry into your date and which will have finished. This is the month to talk about palette: ask how the design can follow the warmer, lower light of the season rather than fight it.

**April.** Cooler evenings make a long reception kinder to flowers. Ask how long the arrangements are expected to look fresh, and whether ceremony pieces can be moved to the reception so the same stems work twice. If you love texture, ask what foliage, branches and seed heads are looking good locally that month.

**May.** Late autumn days are shorter, and an evening reception may be lit mostly by candles. Ask your florist to see the design under low light, as colours read differently after dark. In the northern tropics, May is the start of the dry season, which can make outdoor florals simpler to plan there. Ask what that means for the stems available locally.

### Winter in June, July and August

For ideas across the cooler months, see [our winter flower ideas](/7-blooms-you-need-in-your-2015-winter-wedding).

**June.** Winter begins on 1 June. This is the season to ask directly which stems are grown locally right now and which would need to be imported. If a flower you love is out of season, ask the florist to show you a local alternative in a similar colour and shape before you commit.

**July.** Heated rooms are kind to guests and hard on petals. Ask how arrangements will cope with a warm reception space, and whether pieces can be placed away from heaters and open fires. Ask about scale too: in a smaller winter room, a few generous arrangements can carry more presence than many small ones. Our guide to [July](/7-in-season-wedding-flowers-for-the-july-bride) flowers has more.

**August.** Late winter sits on the edge of spring. Ask your florist whether any early spring stems are likely to be ready by your date, and what the plan is if they arrive late. Agree in advance which stems are fixed and which can change. There is more in our guide to [August](/7-gorgeous-in-season-wedding-flowers-for-the-august-bride) flowers.

### Spring in September, October and November

Spring brings one Australian flower into its own. Waratah flowering is concentrated in spring, and an [AgriFutures Australia project](https://agrifutures.com.au/related-projects/farm-based-methods-for-control-of-flowering-in-waratahs/) looked at ways to extend it to meet peak demand periods such as Christmas. A spring wedding is the natural time to feature it, locally grown. For more ideas, see our guide to [spring](/in-season-flowers-for-your-spring-wedding) flowers.

**September.** Spring starts on 1 September. Ask your florist which native stems are ready locally for your date, and how they sit alongside softer garden flowers in the same design. If you want waratah, ask how its colour and scale work with your palette, as it is a bold, structural flower.

**October.** Mid spring is a good moment to ask your florist early about their calendar for your weekend. In the northern tropics, October is the start of the wet season. If you are marrying there, ask for a wet weather plan for every outdoor arrangement, and how stems will be protected from humidity and sudden rain.

**November.** Late spring runs into early summer, and the days warm quickly. Ask whether you can see a sample arrangement or a trial bouquet before the final order, so you can judge colour and size in person. Ask, too, how the design will hold if the day turns hot. Our guide to [November](/7-in-season-flowers-for-the-november-bride) flowers goes further.

## In season or imported flowers

Some couples find that the flower they have always pictured is simply not grown in Australia in their wedding month. That is where imported stems come in, and it is a reasonable choice, made with your eyes open.

Flowers grown overseas can carry pests and diseases Australia does not want, so the trade is managed through biosecurity rules. The Department of Agriculture's [2018 biosecurity advice on cut flowers](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/biosecurity-trade/policy/risk-analysis/memos/ba2018-12) sets out that import conditions require importers to manage biosecurity risk before they send cut flowers and foliage to Australia. As at November 2026, some species can only be imported under [specific conditions of import](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/biosecurity/legislation/fresh-cut-flowers-mainland).

For a couple, that is the florist's and importer's work, not yours. What it does mean is that an imported stem has more steps between the grower and your table than a local one, and each step is a point where timing can shift.

So the useful conversation with your florist is a plain one. Ask three things:

- **What is local?** Which stems in the design are grown in Australia and in season for your date.
- **What is imported?** Which stems will come from overseas, and when they need to be ordered.
- **What is the backup?** If an imported or late stem is not available, what goes in its place, and in what colour and shape.

A design that mixes both can work well: local stems carry the bulk of the arrangements, and a smaller number of imported flowers bring in the colour or form you cannot find at home. Agree in writing which flowers are essential to you and which the florist may substitute.

When you marry overseas, the flowers follow the destination's season, not Australia's. A June wedding at home is a winter wedding; the same date in the northern hemisphere falls in summer. The questions in the month by month notes above still apply; match them to the destination's season rather than Australia's.

The person to ask is a florist who works at the destination. They know what is grown nearby, what arrives from further away, and how the local weather treats an arrangement left out through a long lunch. Ask them the same three questions: what is local, what is brought in, and what the backup is. Then ask for the quote in writing, itemised, with any substitutions noted, and confirm which currency the deposit is paid in.

Resist the urge to pack flowers from home. A florist at the destination can build the look you want from what is already there, so nothing has to survive the journey with you.

Share your date, venue, guest count and the spaces to be dressed with them early, along with a few images of the mood you are after rather than a list of stems. A good local florist will translate that mood into the flowers of their own season. ## Planning your flowers and spend with your florist

Bring your florist in once the date and the venue are fixed. That is when the season becomes real, and when the conversation about substitutes is easiest to have, long before anything is ordered.

Use that first meeting to agree the shape of the design and the flowers that matter most to you. If the guest list is large or the celebration runs across several events, say how many spaces need dressing and ask how the florist will staff the set-up of each one. Then ask the florist to note, in the proposal, which stems could be swapped and what with. Revisit that list closer to the date, once the florist has a clearer view of how the season is running.

Season and sourcing both affect the cost of a design. An imported stem, a flower at the edge of its season or a date in a peak week can change a quote, and so can scale, labour and set-up. Ask for each proposal to be itemised, with imported stems, labour and set-up shown as separate lines and any likely substitutions noted, then read [what drives the cost of wedding flowers](/style-advice/wedding-flowers-cost) before you compare them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which season suits wedding flowers in Australia?

No season is wrong for flowers. The better question is which stems are at their peak in your month and region. Set the date first, then ask your florist what will be freshest for it.

### When should we talk to our florist about substitutes?

At the first meeting, once your date and venue are fixed. Agree which flowers are essential and which can be swapped, then review the list as the date approaches and the season's crop becomes clearer.

### Are imported flowers allowed into Australia?

Yes, under conditions. Importers must manage [biosecurity risk](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/biosecurity-trade/policy/risk-analysis/memos/ba2018-12) before cut flowers and foliage are sent to Australia, and some species are subject to [specific conditions of import](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/biosecurity/legislation/fresh-cut-flowers-mainland). Your florist handles this through their suppliers, so your role is simply to ask which stems are imported and what the backup is.

### What if the flower we love is out of season on our date?

Ask your florist for a local alternative in a similar colour and shape, or whether a small number of imported stems could carry it within a mostly local design. Agree in writing what happens if that stem is not available.

### Which flower is in season in Australian spring?

Waratah is a spring flower in Australia, with its flowering [concentrated in that season](https://agrifutures.com.au/related-projects/farm-based-methods-for-control-of-flowering-in-waratahs/). For other stems, ask your florist what is ready locally for your date.

### Do Australian seasons apply to a wedding overseas?

No. The flowers follow the destination's season. In the northern hemisphere, the seasons run in reverse, so ask a florist at the destination what is in season for your date.
