If one of you grew up with a grandmother's embroidered towel folded in the linen cupboard, you already know that Ukrainian wedding traditions are more than a list of curiosities. They are a sequence: a visit from the matchmakers, a joining of hands, a wreath, a loaf, a game at the door, a blessing, a church rite, then a long evening of song.

This guide walks through those customs in the order they traditionally unfold, separates what the encyclopaedia records from what families often tell, and shows how Ukrainian-Australian and mixed-heritage couples carry them into a wedding in Australia or elsewhere. It sits alongside our wider guide to [wedding traditions worth keeping](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions).

## Key takeaways

- The traditional Ukrainian wedding was a staged sequence of acts with many regional variations, and the customs families keep today include the rushnyk, the vinok, the korovai, the playful vykup and the parents' blessing.
- The korovai, a decorated wedding bread, was traditionally baked with song, used to bless the couple and shared with every guest.
- The church ceremony gradually became the centre of the day, so ask your parish priest how a family rushnyk or other custom may be used in the rite.
- Smartraveller says "Do not travel" to Ukraine (updated 2 November 2026), so hold these customs in Australia or another destination.

## What makes Ukrainian wedding traditions different

The Internet Encyclopedia of Ukraine describes the traditional wedding, the vesillia, as a well-planned ritual drama in which the bride and groom were called princess and prince. It combined the spoken word, song, dance, music and visual art into one whole.

That scenario became well established between the 16th and 19th centuries and had many regional variations. The traditional wedding usually took place in early spring or autumn and lasted several days.

### Folk custom and church rite

The honest framing is this: the church rite is set by the Church, and the folk customs around it vary by family and region. Over time, the church ceremony assumed the central role in the wedding.

Today many of the old rituals are no longer practised, and those that survive, such as the blessing and the wedding bread, are considerably simplified. So treat what follows as a menu your family chooses from, not a script every Ukrainian household follows.

### Why the rushnyk runs through the day

The rushnyk is a long embroidered cloth, and it is often the object families recognise first. According to the encyclopaedia, embroidered rushnyky were used in folk rites, particularly for weddings and for decorating holy icons.

It has also outlasted much of the old ceremony: the encyclopaedia records that guests at Ruthenian weddings in the former Yugoslavia were still decorated with rushnyky. If your family has one, it is the most portable heirloom you can bring to the day.

## Before the wedding: matchmaking and betrothal

These first acts happened in family homes, long before any guest list existed. Few couples stage them in full now, but they explain much of what comes later.

### Svatannia and the matchmakers

Traditionally, matchmaking (svatannia) was a discussion about the marriage between the groom's representatives, the starosty or svaty, and the bride's parents or relatives.

It is often told that a family accepting the proposal would present the matchmakers with embroidered rushnyky. The encyclopaedia's wedding entry does not describe this, so treat it as family memory rather than a fixed rule.

### The pumpkin refusal

You may also hear that a bride's family could decline a proposal by handing the matchmakers a pumpkin, the harbuz. This is a story told in many families rather than a rite the encyclopaedia records, and its meaning shifts with the teller.

### Zaruchyny, the betrothal

The betrothal, called zaruchyny (and also rukovyny or khustky), was the marriage agreement between the young couple. It was symbolised by the joining of hands.

For a modern couple, this could be as simple as a family dinner where both sets of parents are present and the two of you join hands before them.

## The bride's evening and the vinok

Here the folk calendar turns to the bride and her friends. The details below are partly recorded and partly remembered, and the two are marked as such.

### Divych vechir

The divych vechir, the bride's evening, is often told as a gathering of the bride and her friends on the eve of the wedding, with songs and the making of her wreath. The encyclopaedia's wedding entry does not use the term, so families describe it in their own way.

What the encyclopaedia does record is the unbraiding (rozpleteny), the ritual combing of the bride. If it appeals, a quiet version is easy to keep: a mother or grandmother brushing out the bride's hair before she dresses.

### Weaving the vinok

The wreaths, the vinky, were ritually made using periwinkle. Periwinkle (barvinok, Vinca minor) plays an important role in Ukrainian folklore and in Easter and wedding rituals.

A practical note: if periwinkle matters to you, ask your florist early whether it is available in season where you marry. Ribbons, other flowers and the order of the weaving vary from family to family.

## The korovai, the wedding bread

The encyclopaedia names the wedding bread as one of the customs that has survived, and traditionally every guest shared in it.

### Who bakes the korovai

The encyclopaedia describes the korovai as a traditional braided wedding bread of wheat flour, embellished with little dough flags and figurines of the sun, moon, birds and animals. Women prepared it while singing traditional wedding songs at the new home of the couple.

It was baked and decorated from flour donated by all the wedding participants. That detail translates well: some couples ask each family branch to contribute something to the baking, even if a professional finishes the loaf.

### How the korovai is shared

The couple were blessed with the korovai before the marriage ceremony. After the nuptials, the best man served it to all the guests. The cutting and distributing of the bread among the guests was its own act, the rozpodil.

For a reception, give the korovai a visible place from the moment guests arrive, and plan a short moment after the ceremony for it to be cut and shared. Decide in advance who serves it.

## The vykup, a playful bride ransom

On the morning itself, the mood often lightens with a game at the bride's door. The encyclopaedia is clear that this was play, not a transaction.

Ransoming the bride is among the rituals the encyclopaedia links to medieval influence, alongside a simulated military campaign and fighting between two camps. By the time these customs were recorded, they had lost their original significance and become mere play. Later acts included the interception (pereima), when young people captured the bride and she had to be ransomed.

The vykup as families often tell it today: the groom and his friends arrive at the bride's home, and her bridesmaids or relatives block the door. He answers riddles, sings or completes small tasks before he is let in. Keep it short, keep it kind, and build it into the run sheet before you leave for church.

## The parents' blessing and the bread and salt welcome

The encyclopaedia names the blessing as one of two customs still preserved, and for many families it is the emotional centre of the morning.

The bride's parents blessed the couple, the blahoslovlennia, before they left for church. The blessing is one of the rituals still preserved today, if in simpler form. Later in the scenario came the reception (zustrich), the welcoming of the bride in her new home by her mother-in-law.

The welcome with bread and salt is often told: the parents greet the couple with a loaf and a little salt, sometimes held on a rushnyk, as they arrive at the celebration. The encyclopaedia's wedding entry does not describe it, and families explain its meaning in different ways, so let your own parents decide how they want to offer it.

## The church ceremony: the rushnyk and the crowning

The rite in church belongs to the Church, not to family custom. The two moments below are described as families tell them, in general terms only; your priest has the final word.

The church marriage ceremony, the vinchannia, was usually held on a Sunday. Gradually, the church ceremony assumed the central role in the wedding.

### Stepping onto the rushnyk

It is often told that the couple stand on an embroidered rushnyk in the service, and whoever steps onto it first will have the final say in the marriage. It is not church teaching.

### The crowning in Orthodox and Greek Catholic rites

Families often describe a crowning, with crowns held above or placed on the couple's heads; ask your priest about it. You will find a similar rite described in our guide to [Greek wedding traditions](/expert-advice/greek-wedding-traditions).

The order of the service, and whether a rushnyk is used to bind the couple's hands, can differ. Ukrainian Catholic and Orthodox parishes may differ, so ask your parish priest.

Start this conversation before you book anything else. Ask your parish priest what the rite involves, how a family rushnyk may be used, and what the parish requires of you as a couple.

## The celebration: toasts, songs and dances

After the church, the vesillia opens up. This is where regional and family differences are widest, so treat the details as a starting point.

### Toasts and the call for a kiss

It is often told that guests interrupt the meal with cries of "Hirko!" and the couple answer with a kiss. Families explain the call in different ways, so let an uncle or the master of ceremonies introduce it in his own words for guests who did not grow up with it.

### Wedding songs

Song was never an extra. The encyclopaedia notes that each act of the wedding involved wedding songs and rituals. Even the baking of the korovai was done to traditional wedding songs.

If an older relative knows the songs, ask them to lead one at a turning point: the korovai, the blessing or the welcome.

### Circle and couple dances

Dance was one of the arts the traditional wedding combined, along with song and music. Many families tell of a small folk band, often remembered as fiddle, drum and hammered dulcimer, playing for circle and couple dances late into the night.

## What to wear to a Ukrainian wedding

Embroidery is where heritage is easiest to wear, whether you are the couple, a parent or a guest. Dress codes are set by the couple, so guests should follow the invitation first.

### The vyshyvanka and embroidery

The encyclopaedia records that embroidery is used mostly on clothing, and that a traditional form is used for the shirt for both men and women. On a woman's shirt, the main design sits on the upper sleeve, the polyk or ustavka. Cross-stitch is today the most popular method.

Patterns vary by region. Strict geometric patterns are preserved in Polisia and the Hutsul region, while floral designs predominate in central and eastern Ukraine along the Dnipro. Colours are often limited to one or two, such as black and red, while Hutsul and Bukovyna geometric patterns are multicoloured.

That gives you a lovely detail to plan around: match an embroidered shirt or the rushnyk to the region your family comes from, rather than choosing a generic pattern.

## Bringing Ukrainian traditions to a wedding in Australia

Ukrainian customs have long travelled with the families who keep them. The encyclopaedia notes that Ukrainians in Canada and the United States hold on to some elements of the traditional wedding as an expression of national identity. Ukrainian-Australian families do much the same, each in their own way.

### Which customs translate to a modern celebration

You do not need to stage every act. Choose the two or three customs that matter most to your family. The parents' blessing and the wedding bread are the customs the encyclopaedia says have been preserved, if simplified. A family rushnyk, a vinok and a song led by a grandparent sit easily alongside them.

For mixed-heritage couples, pairing one Ukrainian custom with one from the other family helps both sides recognise themselves in the day. ## Travel and legal notes for couples

This section reflects official guidance as at November 2026 and is not legal advice.

Smartraveller's advice for Ukraine is "Do not travel", updated 2 November 2026. The Australian Embassy is not able to provide in-person consular or passport services in Ukraine, and its ability to provide consular assistance is severely limited. Read the current [Smartraveller advice for Ukraine](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/ukraine) before making any plans.

If one of you has family ties to Ukraine, Smartraveller notes that Ukraine does not recognise dual nationality for Australian citizens, and that "We may not be able to help you if you have Ukrainian nationality." It also says that under Ukrainian law, you may be a Ukrainian citizen if you were born, or have a parent born, in Ukraine.

If you marry outside Australia, Smartraveller's guide to [getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) says you cannot register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised here. To be recognised, the marriage must be valid under the law of the country where it took place and valid under Australian law had it happened in Australia. Smartraveller suggests speaking to a lawyer to confirm that an overseas marriage certificate is valid in Australia.

Some countries ask you to prove you are not already married, and Smartraveller directs couples to that country's embassy or consulate for what they will need.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the traditional gift for a Ukrainian wedding?

No single gift is recorded as traditional in the sources we used. Families differ, so the simplest course is to follow the couple's invitation or ask a close relative what is usual in that family.

### What finger do Ukrainians wear their wedding ring on?

It is often told that the wedding ring is traditionally worn on the right hand in Ukrainian families. Practice varies between families, so it is worth simply asking the couple.

### What does the rushnyk mean at a Ukrainian wedding?

The encyclopaedia records that embroidered rushnyky were used in folk rites, particularly for weddings. The meanings families give it, such as the story about who steps onto it first, are family stories rather than recorded facts, so ask your own family what theirs means to them.

### What is a korovai?

The korovai is a traditional braided wedding bread of wheat flour, decorated with small dough figures such as the sun, moon, birds and animals. Traditionally it was used to bless the couple before the ceremony and shared with every guest afterwards.

### What should guests wear to a Ukrainian wedding?

Follow the couple's dress code first. Embroidered shirts are a traditional form for both men and women, so an embroidered piece is a thoughtful choice if the couple welcome it.

### Can we include Ukrainian traditions in a wedding in Australia?

Yes. Ukrainian communities abroad have long kept elements of the traditional wedding. Speak to your parish priest about the church rite, and choose the family customs, such as the blessing, the korovai or a rushnyk, that mean the most to you.
