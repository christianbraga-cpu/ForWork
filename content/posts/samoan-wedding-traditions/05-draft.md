A Samoan wedding is not only the joining of two people. It is the meeting of two families, carried out in the way of fa'a Samoa: chiefs and orators speak, fine mats change hands, the church blesses the marriage and the whole family sits down to eat.

Samoan wedding traditions follow that order, and this guide walks through them as the day unfolds. It closes with what Australian couples need to know about marrying in Samoa, or bringing these customs into a wedding at home.

## Key takeaways

- A Samoan wedding is led by the aiga, the extended family, and its matai, so the families' wishes shape the day as much as the couple's.
- Fine mats ('ie toga) are the most honoured gift at a Samoan wedding, and in marriage ceremonies the bride's family presents them to the groom's family.
- The church service sits at the heart of the day for many Samoan families, followed by speeches, the siva, the taualuga and a shared feast.
- As at November 2026, Smartraveller advises Australians to "Exercise normal safety precautions" in Samoa, and an overseas marriage can't be registered in Australia, though it is usually recognised.

## Samoan wedding traditions start with family

There is no single script for a traditional Samoan wedding. Practice varies from family to family and village to village, and between Samoa and the communities in Australia, New Zealand and the United States. In village areas local law prevails and can vary from village to village. <!-- fact_05 -->

Many traditions of rural life remain virtually unchanged, while some customs have changed markedly, particularly in Apia. <!-- fact_06 --> Tens of thousands of Samoans have emigrated to New Zealand, the United States, Australia and elsewhere, <!-- fact_12 --> so a Samoan-Australian wedding in Sydney or Brisbane will often blend both worlds.

The best first step is a simple one: ask both families what they expect. For how these customs sit beside other cultures, see our guide to [wedding traditions from around the world](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions).

## 1. Fa'a Samoa, matai and the families behind the wedding

Samoa's tourism authority describes Samoan culture as based on fa'a Samoa, "The Samoan Way", which for many is closely tied to honouring one's family. <!-- fact_01 --> It is a way of life stretching back more than 3000 years, built on being part of a community that looks after each other. <!-- fact_119 -->

Samoan society is organised by extended families, the aiga, each with its own matai titles connected to districts, villages and family land. <!-- fact_02 --> Each aiga designates at least one matai to lead and represent it, and the matai form village councils that administer local affairs. <!-- fact_22 -->

Matai are responsible for maintaining the traditions and customs of the village, and their status is highly respected. <!-- fact_03 --> Members of the aiga are expected to be generous and to put the group ahead of their own interests. <!-- fact_04 -->

That is why a Samoan wedding feels so full. Te Ara, the New Zealand government encyclopedia, calls aiga the bedrock of fa'asamoa. <!-- fact_23 --> In practice, the couple's own plans sit inside a larger conversation between two families and their matai.

## 2. The families meet: orators and formal speeches

In many Samoan families, the formal part of a wedding begins when the two sides meet and their orators speak. Te Papa's Pacific curator describes the tulāfale (orator), through lāuga (oratory), as the mouthpiece of families, villages and districts, influential in directing ceremonies, presentations and cultural protocols. <!-- fact_25 -->

Tulāfale are keepers of cultural knowledge such as history and genealogies, and they negotiate the relationships between different parties. <!-- fact_26 --> They usually speak indoors in a fale or outdoors on the malae, the village green, carrying the fue (fly whisk) and to'oto'o (staff) and wearing the 'ulafala, a pandanus key necklace, as symbols of their office. <!-- fact_27 -->

Matai are still expected to deliver rhythmic and poetical orations at council meetings and other major events. <!-- fact_08 --> At a wedding, the speeches set the tone: each side honours the other, acknowledges its guests and marks what the families are giving.

Couples often ask who pays for a Samoan wedding. In many families, the cost and the work are shared across the aiga on both sides, with relatives contributing food, gifts and time. Ask early who will speak for each family, and build the run sheet around the speeches and presentations rather than squeezing them in.

## 3. Fine mats ('ie toga) and gifts between families

If one object defines Samoan wedding customs, it is the fine mat. Te Papa describes 'ie toga as a valuable presented in ceremonies that cement relationships between people, and worn as a garment on important occasions by high-ranking matai and taupou, the daughters of chiefs. <!-- fact_13 -->

<!-- IMAGE SLOT fine-mats: Preferred: licensed detail photo of finely woven 'ie toga being presented or laid out at a consented family exchange, even daylight, hands only or consented people; no visible money, counts or amounts. Fallback only if Nic approves: AI neutral still life of a plain woven pandanus mat folded on linen, no people, dress, ceremony or place, captioned 'Illustrative image' (not presented as an 'ie toga). -->

'Ie toga are the highest-ranking item in the Samoan exchange system, sealing relationships between groups of people at special events and ceremonies. <!-- fact_14 --> In Samoan marriage ceremonies, the bride's family presents 'ie toga to the husband's family. <!-- fact_18 -->

Each mat is hand woven with fibres so fine that one could take months or years to complete, and in the past young women made them, finishing mats left incomplete by older female relatives. <!-- fact_15 --> Te Papa's collection holds an 1800s example woven from pandanus with red feathers. <!-- fact_19 -->

Some mats pass through several generations, gaining sentimental or historical value, and some are given names. <!-- fact_16 --> A particularly old and fine one brings great dignity to a ceremony. <!-- fact_20 -->

The language of marriage reaches beyond weddings, too. At a 2002 presentation of the named mat Le Ageagea o Tumua, a Samoan orator offered it "to seal our marriage" between two nations. <!-- fact_21 -->

For planning, treat heirloom mats as irreplaceable. Give the presentation unhurried time and a sheltered, dignified space, and agree handling, display and transport with the family, not with suppliers. Gifts for the church minister and the matai are a family decision; ask your parents or elders what is expected.

## 4. The church wedding at the heart of a Samoan wedding

Samoans readily adopted Christianity after European contact, and even remote villages built churches, often of grand proportions. <!-- fact_11 --> Most villages have a church and a meetinghouse, which doubles as a cultural centre. <!-- fact_07 --> The Congregational Christian Church of Samoa is the dominant church, with Roman Catholic, Latter-day Saint and Methodist communities also significant. <!-- fact_11 -->

The tourism authority calls the church central to the Samoan way of life. <!-- fact_120 --> For many couples in Australia the same holds: Te Ara notes that for New Zealand-born Samoans the church often provides the anchor. <!-- fact_24 -->

The service follows the order of the couple's denomination, including who walks down the aisle and when. Your minister, and your family's faifeau if you have one, will guide the order of service and any family roles within it.

## 5. Extended family and the village at Samoan weddings

Samoan weddings are rarely small. Aunts, uncles, cousins and family friends arrive with food, gifts and hands to help, and the elders take their places of honour.

At weddings and other family gatherings, Samoans often entertain one another with ula, in which two groups alternate between singing and dancing. <!-- fact_09 --> Kava, a traditional nonalcoholic drink from a tropical pepper plant, is consumed at social events, mainly by matai. <!-- fact_10 -->

If the wedding is in a village, the matai and village council administer local affairs, <!-- fact_22 --> so the family's matai is the right first conversation about what is appropriate. Guests should follow the village's lead: remove shoes when entering a fale, and do not stand while elders are seated. <!-- fact_122 -->

## 6. Samoan wedding attire: what the bride and groom wear

### Puletasi and the second outfit

Many Samoan brides marry in a white gown for the church service, then change into a puletasi for the reception, often in fabric that matches the bridal party or the groom. Samoa's tourism authority describes the puletasi as "a two-piece garment", worn by women in the fa'ataupati performance. <!-- fact_118 -->

<!-- IMAGE SLOT dress: Licensed photo from a consented Samoan or Samoan-Australian wedding: bridal party or family in coordinated puletasi and ie faitaga in matching fabric, outdoors in soft daylight; full-length, natural pose. At many weddings, both families dress in coordinated colours, and flowers in the hair or garlands around the neck finish the look.

### Lavalava and flowers for the groom and guests

Samoan men wear the traditional lavalava, <!-- fact_118 --> and many grooms pair a formal lavalava with a pressed shirt and jacket for the reception, sometimes matched to the bride's puletasi.

Guests take their cue from the church. For church, women wear a dress or blouse and skirt and men wear trousers and a shirt. <!-- fact_120 --> Visitors to villages are asked to wear a lavalava, pants or shorts and a t-shirt. <!-- fact_122 --> A short dress note in the guest pack saves everyone guessing.

## 7. Siva Samoa and the taualuga: the Samoan dance at a wedding

Dance is where a Samoan wedding opens up. The tourism authority describes the siva as a slow, fluid, graceful storytelling dance, traditionally performed by a young woman. <!-- fact_117 -->

<!-- IMAGE SLOT taualuga: Licensed photo from a consented Samoan or Samoan-Australian reception: the bride or taupou dancing the taualuga, oiled arms and graceful hand movement, family nearby, warm evening light; no money visible on the dancer or floor. The taualuga is the high point. Te Papa describes it as the classical Samoan dance, "a dance of negotiation and celebration". <!-- fact_113 --> At many Samoan weddings, the bride dances the taualuga to close the evening's formal dancing, while family members dance around her in support.

Couples planning the reception should give the taualuga its own moment in the running order, with the floor cleared and the music agreed with the family. For a real celebration from our pages, see [Mercy and Chanel's traditional Samoan wedding](/a-traditional-samoan-wedding-celebration).

## 8. The wedding feast and the umu

Special occasions bring a big to'anai, a feast for the whole family. <!-- fact_121 --> The umu, which the tourism authority describes as "underground Samoan oven cooking", <!-- fact_121 --> is still how many families prepare food for a celebration.

<!-- IMAGE SLOT feast: Licensed photo of a long shared table or umu food laid on banana leaves at a consented Samoan or Samoan-Australian wedding (for example taro, palusami and roast pork), warm daylight; guests absent or consented. No venue named unless licensed and cleared. Never AI. -->

At many weddings, the umu is lit early by the men of the family, and the table fills with roast pork, taro, palusami and seafood. In many families, food is also shared out among relatives to take home after the celebration.

For a destination wedding, ask early whether your venue allows an umu on the grounds, or whether the family will cook off site and bring the food in.

## 9. What UNESCO says about the 'ie Samoa

In 2019, UNESCO inscribed ['Ie Samoa, fine mat and its cultural value](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ie-samoa-fine-mat-and-its-cultural-value-01499) on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. <!-- fact_114 --> It is Samoa's only element on that list. <!-- fact_100 -->

UNESCO describes the 'Ie Samoa as a finely hand-woven pandanus mat with two rows of green and red feathers at the hem, which can take up to several months and even years to make. <!-- fact_116 -->

Its text names weddings directly: the mat is displayed and exchanged at festive celebrations or important gatherings such as weddings and funerals, and its exchange helps maintain the social structure. <!-- fact_115 --> Its value lies in exchange in ceremonies that reaffirm kinship ties. <!-- fact_115 -->

## 10. Weddings in Samoa: getting married as an Australian

As at November 2026, [Smartraveller advice for Samoa](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/pacific/samoa) is "Exercise normal safety precautions", updated 5 August 2026, with an ongoing dengue outbreak since 2025. <!-- fact_102 --> Cyclone season runs from November to April, though storms can occur year-round, and earthquakes and tsunamis can occur. <!-- fact_103 -->

### Travel and etiquette

Australians can apply for a 90 day Visitor Permit on arrival using the passenger arrival card, with a confirmed onward ticket, enough funds and a passport valid six months beyond the stay, and conditions can change at short notice. <!-- fact_107 --> Check every guest's passport expiry when invitations go out.

Sunday activities are extremely limited and alcohol can't be sold, though hotels and resorts operate normally; be respectful passing through villages. <!-- fact_104 --> Sundays are a National Day of Rest, and some villages observe an evening prayer curfew, Sa. <!-- fact_120 --> Outside tourist areas the dress code is conservative. <!-- fact_105 --> Same-sex relationships are not recognised in Samoa. <!-- fact_106 -->

Couples who marry in Samoa tend to choose Upolu or Savai'i, the two main islands, and plan the family ceremony and the feast around the island they pick.

### Legal steps

Smartraveller's guide to [getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) says some countries ask you to prove you are free to marry, commonly with a Certificate of No Impediment (CNI) or registry certificates. <!-- fact_109 --> You can't register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law; talk to a lawyer to confirm. <!-- fact_110 -->

CNIs are issued through Australian Passport Offices, embassies, high commissions and consulates overseas, and by mail, and fees apply. <!-- fact_111 --> The Australian High Commission is at 47 Beach Road, Apia. <!-- fact_108 -->

Before you book, put these questions to Samoa's Births, Deaths and Marriages registry and the Australian High Commission in Apia:

- Which documents does Samoa require to prove you are free to marry, and does it accept a CNI issued in Australia?
- Does any document need an apostille or authentication?
- How far ahead must you lodge the paperwork, and how long does it take?
- What fees apply, at the registry and for the CNI?

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the main Samoan wedding traditions?

The families' formal meeting and the orators' speeches, the presentation of 'ie toga, the church service, the siva and the taualuga, and a shared feast. Each family keeps its own version, so ask both sides early.

### Who pays for a Samoan wedding?

In many Samoan families, the cost and work are shared across the extended family on both sides, with relatives contributing food, gifts and time. The details are a family conversation, best held before you set the guest list.

### What is an 'ie toga and why is it given at weddings?

An 'ie toga is a finely hand-woven fine mat, the highest-ranking item in the Samoan exchange system. <!-- fact_14 --> UNESCO names weddings among the gatherings where the 'Ie Samoa is displayed and exchanged. <!-- fact_115 -->

### What is the taualuga?

Te Papa describes the taualuga as the classical Samoan dance, "a dance of negotiation and celebration". <!-- fact_113 --> At many weddings the bride dances it as the high point of the reception.

### What does a Samoan bride wear?

Many brides wear a white gown for church and change into a puletasi, a two-piece garment, <!-- fact_118 --> for the reception, often in fabric that matches the bridal party.

### What should guests wear to a Samoan wedding?

Dress as for church: a dress or blouse and skirt for women, trousers and a shirt for men. <!-- fact_120 --> Take your shoes off when entering a fale.

### Can Australians get married in Samoa?

Yes. Smartraveller notes that an overseas marriage can't be registered in Australia but is usually recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_110 --> Check the documents and fees with Samoa's registry and the Australian High Commission in Apia.
