A Lebanese wedding rarely starts on the wedding day. It starts weeks or months earlier, in a living room, when one family visits another to ask a question.

This guide walks through Lebanese wedding traditions in the order families usually keep them, from the formal asking and the henna night to the zaffeh, the dabke and the sword at the cake. It is written for Lebanese-Australian and mixed-heritage couples carrying these customs to a celebration in Italy, Cyprus, the UAE or at home in Australia. For other cultures, see our [wedding traditions worth keeping from around the world](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions).

## Key takeaways

- Lebanese families are Christian, Muslim and Druze, so ask each side which customs they actually keep before you plan the programme.
- The steps before the wedding (the asking, the engagement, the henna night and the ceremony itself) matter to families as much as the reception.
- The zaffeh and the dabke need real space on the day: a clear route for the procession and a long run of floor for the line.
- Smartraveller rates Lebanon "Do not travel" (as at November 2026), so this guide is about bringing the customs to a wedding elsewhere.

## One celebration, many families

There is no single Lebanese wedding. Britannica lists Shia and Sunni Muslims as Lebanon's two largest religious groups, with Maronite Christians making up more than one-fifth of the population, alongside other Christian communities, the Druze and a small Jewish minority. <!-- fact_06 --> The country has no official religion. <!-- fact_06 -->

So customs vary between Christian, Muslim and Druze families, and between regions and households within each. A Maronite family from the mountains and a Sunni family from the coast may share the dabke and the sweets, then mark the marriage itself in quite different ways.

The practical point for planning: ask both families early which steps they want, rather than working from a list. If one partner is not of Lebanese heritage, the same conversation tells you where the two traditions sit comfortably side by side.

Many of these customs are shared across the region. Our guide to [Arab and Levantine wedding traditions](/guides/arab-levantine-weddings) covers the wider picture.

## Before the wedding

### The formal asking (tulbeh)

The tulbeh (also written tulba, toulbe or tolbe) is the formal request for the bride's hand. Across the Levant it is described as a private visit limited to relatives: once both families have agreed to the couple's decision, the groom and his family ask the bride and her family, and the families then formally recognise that the couple will marry. <!-- fact_08 -->

Coffee is often told as part of the visit. In Arab weddings generally, the families reading the Fatiha after the father agrees is described as typically accompanied by Arabic coffee in the Levant, though that account is framed around Muslim families in Egypt, Palestine and Jordan rather than Lebanon. <!-- fact_10 --> Treat the details as your family's to decide.

### The engagement (khutbeh)

The engagement is the khutbeh (printed khutubah in some sources). <!-- fact_09 --> In Arab weddings generally, engagements are described as usually taking place at a simpler party or family dinner, without a zaffeh, and the couple exchange rings, placing them on each other's right-hand ring finger. <!-- fact_09 -->

Some Lebanese families do throw a large engagement party; others keep it to a dinner. Neither is more correct.

For a wedding abroad, a smaller engagement dinner can work well as an early welcome for guests who arrive a few days before, so the families meet before the big night.

### The henna night (laylat al-henna)

The henna night, laylat al-henna, is a pre-wedding evening that many families keep. Across the Levant it is described as having changed over time: historically, in accounts from Palestine, the groom's family danced through the village to the bride's house, where henna was mixed and applied to the couple's hands. <!-- fact_15 --> Today, especially outside Palestine, it is described as closer to a women's celebration with music, food and dancing, and henna drawn on the palms and feet of the bride and her guests. <!-- fact_15 -->

In Lebanese families who keep it, the evening is often told as an intimate one: the women of both families, a tray of henna, songs, and the bride at the centre. Some families call the pre-wedding gathering by other names, and some Christian and Muslim families skip it altogether.

### The bride leaves her family home

On the wedding day, many families keep a moment when the groom and his family come to collect the bride from her parents' home. It is often told as the most emotional hour of the day: music at the door, parents saying goodbye, and sometimes a small zaffeh leading her out.

The idea is old. A Melkite Greek Catholic history of the crowning describes how, in the Holy Land in the time of Christ, the groom led the bride from her father's house to his own in a procession of his friends and her family, with psalms sung. <!-- fact_19 --> That is background, not a description of modern Lebanese practice.

### The church ceremony and the crowning

Many Lebanese Christian couples marry in Eastern churches, where the heart of the service is the crowning. The Melkite Greek Catholic Eparchy of Newton calls the Eastern Churches' marriage service the Mystery of Crowning. <!-- fact_16 --> It describes a betrothal with rings, consent, prayers and the joining of hands, the crowning, readings from Ephesians and the Wedding at Cana, a shared cup of wine for the unity of married life, a procession around the table holding the Gospel Book, and the removal of the crowns. <!-- fact_16 -->

The Orthodox Church in America text follows the same shape: a Service of Betrothal at the back of the church, with the rings blessed and exchanged, and then the Service of Crowning in the centre. <!-- fact_01 --> The priest crowns the groom first and then the bride, and blesses them three times. <!-- fact_02 --> After the common cup, he leads the couple three times around the lectern while those behind them hold the crowns. <!-- fact_03 -->

What do the crowns mean? The Orthodox text links them to the crowns of the martyrs, the witnesses of Christ. <!-- fact_02 --> The Melkite page, citing St John Chrysostom, adds Christ's victory over sin, death and evil, and the royal authority of the children of God. <!-- fact_17 -->

One line stands out for Lebanese couples: a crowning prayer in the Orthodox text asks God to "exalt them like the cedars of Lebanon, like a luxuriant vine". <!-- fact_04 --> Some couples print it in the order of service.

How the rite runs in a Maronite or Antiochian Orthodox parish, and who holds the crowns, varies by church and family. Your priest is the person to ask.

### Katb al-kitab, the marriage contract

For many Muslim families, the marriage itself is the katb al-kitab, the signing of the contract (also called aqd nikah or aqd zawaj). In Arab weddings generally it is described as the focus of the official ceremony: a sheikh or imam gives a short address, the bride's father or eldest male relative accepts the proposal, the documents are completed and two witnesses sign. <!-- fact_11 -->

In the Levant, it is described as usually held at the bride's or groom's family home, and sometimes at the wedding hall, a mosque or a court. <!-- fact_12 --> The same source notes that the only Islamic requirement is the contract and its announcement; the proposal, engagement, henna and reception are cultural additions. <!-- fact_13 -->

The contract usually includes the mahr. Britannica describes the mahr as a marriage custom, in one entry the groom's commitment to provide his bride with wealth in her lifetime. <!-- fact_05 --> Details are a matter for the families and the officiant.

Practice differs between Sunni, Shia and Druze families and between officiants, so let the families lead on who signs, when and where.

## The celebration

### The zaffeh procession

The zaffeh (also zaffe or zaffa) is the procession that brings the couple into the celebration. It is often told as drummers, dancers and singers leading the way, the beat loud enough to be heard well before the couple appear, with guests clapping them in.

Some families hold a zaffeh at the bride's door before the ceremony, others save it for the entrance to the reception, and many do both. For a picture of how it feels, see [a glamorous traditional Lebanese wedding in Sydney](/real-wedding-a-glamorous-and-traditional-wedding).

### The dabke line dance

Britannica describes Lebanon's national folk arts as song, the dabke ("the national dance") and zajal, or folk poetry. <!-- fact_105 --> The dance is quite common in festivities in the area between northern Syria and southern Israel, and the Druze communities of Lebanon, Syria and Israel are very fond of it. <!-- fact_103 --> Britannica notes it is danced mainly by men, though at weddings many families invite everyone into the line. <!-- fact_103 -->

Dancers move in a straight line holding handkerchiefs high, and the first dancer in the row signals each step or jump. <!-- fact_104 --> The same line dance is shared across the region; see [the shared zaffeh and dabke in Palestinian weddings](/expert-advice/palestinian-wedding-traditions).

Zajal itself, the recited or sung poetry, is listed by UNESCO as Lebanese intangible cultural heritage and is heard at social and family celebrations. <!-- fact_100 --> <!-- fact_101 --> See the [UNESCO listing for Al-Zajal](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/al-zajal-recited-or-sung-poetry-01000).

### Zaghareet, the cry of joy

Zaghareet (singular zaghrouta, also written zalghouta) are the long, high, trilling cries that rise from the women in the room. They are often told as the sound of joy itself: heard as the bride leaves home, as the couple enter, as the rings or crowns are given.

### The sword and the cake

Many Lebanese couples cut the cake with a sword or a long ceremonial blade, often after a short, dramatic entrance with music and lights. It is often told as a sign of strength and of the couple cutting the first piece of their new life together, though families tell it in different ways.

The cake is usually tall and the moment is staged for photographs, so it tends to sit late in the evening, after the first dances.

### Sugared almonds and wedding sweets

Sugared almonds and wedding sweets turn up at many Lebanese weddings. Families often serve mlabbas (sugar-coated almonds) at the engagement and the wedding, often in small favour bags or bowls on every table, alongside trays of Lebanese sweets late in the night.

Their meaning is often told simply: sweetness for the marriage, and something for every guest to take home.

### Space for the zaffeh and the dabke line

A zaffeh needs a route, not just a doorway: a clear path from where the couple arrive to the dance floor, wide enough for drummers and dancers, with guests on either side.

A dabke line needs a long run of floor rather than a small square, because Britannica describes dancers moving in a straight line led by the first in the row. <!-- fact_104 --> Ask the venue for the floor's real dimensions once tables are set, and whether a terrace can take the overflow.

Drums are loud. Ask each venue for its music finish time and its rules on live percussion and amplification outdoors. Not published: music finish times and percussion rules for most shortlisted venues. Your Wedded Advisor confirms these with the venue in writing before you commit.

### Timing a henna night around travel

A henna night works best once the close family has landed and slept. For a destination wedding, that usually means two nights before the wedding, leaving the night before free for rest and a rehearsal.

Henna is not a quick job, so book the artist early in the evening and keep the room warm and unhurried. A private dining room or a villa's lounge suits it better than a public space.

If you are holding a katb al-kitab or a crowning, plan where it sits in the week as well. The contract can be signed at a family home, a mosque or the wedding venue, so a quiet room with a table for the signing and the witnesses is worth asking for. <!-- fact_12 --> A crowning procession circles the central table, so the ceremony space needs a clear aisle and room around it. <!-- fact_03 --> <!-- fact_16 -->

### Blades, venue rules and the cake

Check the venue's rules on ceremonial blades before you plan the cake moment, and ask whether it can supply one. If a family heirloom is travelling with you, check your airline's rules and the destination's rules on carrying a blade well ahead of the flight.

Agree the timing with the cake maker and photographer, and keep the blade with a named person until the moment it is needed.

## A note on travel and marriage law

As at November 2026, Smartraveller's overall advice for Lebanon is "Do not travel", because of the volatile security situation, armed conflict, civil unrest and terrorism (updated 24 September 2026). <!-- fact_106 --> That is why this guide is about holding the celebration elsewhere. Read the current [Smartraveller advice for Lebanon](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/middle-east/lebanon) before any travel plans.

A few points from that advice matter to Lebanese-Australian families. Smartraveller says religious law has the same standing as civil law in Lebanon, and that all family law matters, including marriage, are based on local religious laws. <!-- fact_107 --> Lebanon recognises dual nationality but treats Lebanese dual nationals as Lebanese citizens, which may limit consular help. <!-- fact_108 --> Check with the [Australian Embassy in Beirut](https://lebanon.embassy.gov.au/) and Smartraveller before any travel.

Wherever you marry, Smartraveller's guide to getting married overseas says to check the destination country's requirements with its embassy or consulate, and notes that some countries ask you to prove you are free to marry. <!-- fact_110 --> You can't register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is recognised if it was valid where it happened and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_111 --> Our guide on [whether you need to register an overseas marriage at home](/expert-advice/do-you-have-to-register-an-overseas-marriage-at-home) explains more.

This is general information, not legal advice. Smartraveller suggests talking to a lawyer to confirm a marriage's validity. <!-- fact_111 -->

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens at a Lebanese wedding?

It depends on the families. Many keep a formal asking (tulbeh), an engagement, a henna night, then a church crowning or a katb al-kitab, followed by a reception with a zaffeh entrance, dabke, the cake and sweets. Customs vary between Christian, Muslim and Druze families. <!-- fact_06 -->

### What is a tulbeh?

The tulbeh is the formal request for the bride's hand. It is described as a private visit in which the groom and his family ask the bride and her family, after which the families formally recognise that the couple will marry. <!-- fact_08 -->

### What is the zaffeh at a Lebanese wedding?

The zaffeh is the musical procession that brings the couple in, often told as drummers, dancers and singers leading the way. Families hold it at the bride's home, at the reception entrance, or both.

### What is zalghouta?

Zalghouta (or zaghrouta, plural zaghareet) is the long, trilling cry of joy that women in the room give at key moments, such as the bride leaving home or the couple's entrance.

### Why is the cake cut with a sword at Lebanese weddings?

The sword is often told as a sign of strength and of the couple's first act together, though families explain it in different ways. Check your venue's rules on blades before you plan it.

### What do you wear to a Lebanese wedding?

Follow the invitation and the families. Evening receptions are usually formal, and if there is a church or religious ceremony, dress for that setting first; ask the couple if you are unsure.

## Plan the celebration your families will recognise

The customs that matter most are the ones both families recognise as their own. Start there, then find a venue with room for the zaffeh, a long floor for the dabke and a quiet space for the moments before.
