Chinese wedding traditions are usually presented as a list of curiosities. In a family, they are a sequence: gifts carried to the bride's home weeks ahead, a comb drawn through the hair by candlelight, a door that only opens once the groom has earned it, and cups of tea offered on bended knee.

This guide walks that sequence in wedding order, then covers what Australians need to know about marrying in China or bringing the rites home. It sits alongside our guide to [Indonesian wedding traditions](/expert-advice/indonesian-wedding-traditions).

## Key takeaways

- Chinese wedding customs differ by dialect group, region and family, so ask both families which rites they expect before you set the day's order.
- In Singapore, most Chinese weddings still begin with guo da li, the betrothal gifts carried from the groom's family to the bride's home on an auspicious day.
- In Singapore, the tea ceremony is held at the groom's home and again at the bride's, with elders served in order of seniority.
- As at November 2026, Smartraveller advises Australians to "Exercise a high degree of caution" in China overall, and a marriage made there can't be registered in Australia, though it is usually recognised.

## Chinese wedding customs differ by family and region

There is no single traditional Chinese wedding. Customs vary by family, by region and by community, and Cantonese, Hokkien, Teochew and other families, in China and across the diaspora in Australia, Singapore and Malaysia, each keep their own versions.

Many of the detailed accounts below come from Singapore's National Heritage Board and National Library Board, so they describe Chinese weddings in Singapore, not every Chinese family. The Library Board notes that customs vary by dialect group, and that despite simplification, a wedding is often not considered complete until the customary rites are performed. <!-- fact_11 -->

Where a custom is passed on by word of mouth rather than documented, we say it is "often told", and we leave its meaning to your family.

## Before the wedding: betrothal gifts and a lucky date

### 1. The Three Letters and Six Rites

The classical framework is usually called the Three Letters and Six Rites. A community heritage summary from the Chinese Historical and Cultural Project says three old texts, the Book of Rites, the Book of Etiquette and Ceremonial and the Baihu Tong, outline the "Three Covenants and the Six Rites" as necessary elements of a marriage. <!-- fact_27 -->

Families name and order the six rites in their own ways. In Singapore, the National Library Board notes that matching horoscopes and hiring professional matchmakers are rarely practised today, while the betrothal gifts are still observed. <!-- fact_12 -->

### 2. Betrothal gifts (guo da li)

Singapore's National Heritage Board says most Chinese wedding practices there today begin with guo da li, a betrothal ceremony held two to four weeks before the wedding on an auspicious day. <!-- fact_01 --> The groom, with a female relative, brings gifts to the bride's family. <!-- fact_01 -->

The gifts are chosen for their symbolism, for example mandarin oranges for good luck and charcoal for a good life after marriage. <!-- fact_03 --> They also differ by community. In Singapore, Teochew gifts typically include peanut-sesame candy wrapped in red paper, while Cantonese families may send seafood such as sea cucumber, abalone, scallop, dried shrimp, oyster, mushroom and fish maw. <!-- fact_14 -->

Some items cross dialect lines. Two pairs of red dragon and phoenix candles are common to all, and the groom's parents may give the bride dragon and phoenix bangles (Cantonese) or si dian jin, four items of gold jewellery (Teochew). <!-- fact_15 -->

The exchange runs both ways. The bride's family returns some of the gifts (hui li) to show they accept the marriage, presents another set to the groom's family, and the bride receives her trousseau (jia zhuang). <!-- fact_02 -->

For your day: ask both families early which gifts each side expects, who carries them, and on which date.

### 3. Choosing an auspicious date

According to the community summary, fortune tellers interpret auspicious days from the couple's birth date and hour after consulting the Chinese almanac, and working it out yourself from the almanac is considered bad form. <!-- fact_31 --> In Singapore, betrothal gifts are likewise presented on a day usually chosen from the tongshu, the Chinese almanac. <!-- fact_13 -->

Lucky numbers, and months some families prefer to avoid, are often told and differ from household to household. Take them lightly, but take them early: in Singapore, hotel function rooms tend to be fully booked on dates seen as auspicious. <!-- fact_24 -->

## The night before and the wedding morning

In Singapore, the matrimonial bed is prepared (an chuang) three days to one week ahead, and on the day a young boy jumps and rolls on it to bless the couple with children. <!-- fact_10 -->

### 4. The hair-combing ceremony

Singapore's National Heritage Board describes shang tou, a hair-combing ritual on the night before the wedding that marks the couple's transition into adulthood. <!-- fact_04 --> It takes place in both homes: the bride and the groom each face a pair of lighted candles while a woman of good fortune combs the hair four times, repeating an auspicious phrase each time. <!-- fact_04 -->

It has traditionally been a Cantonese custom, also practised by other dialect groups in Singapore, and it may be performed by the parents or by a happily married elder female relative. <!-- fact_16 --> Afterwards, the bride and groom eat a bowl of glutinous rice balls. <!-- fact_17 -->

Each family words the four blessings its own way. For your day: decide early who will hold the comb: a parent or a happily married elder. <!-- fact_16 -->

### 5. Door games at the bride's home

In Singapore, the bride's father traditionally places the bridal veil on his daughter's head to mark her coming of age before the groom arrives. <!-- fact_25 -->

The groom does not walk straight in. The bridesmaids set him and his groomsmen a series of playful tasks, known in Singapore as "gatecrashing", before he can see the bride. <!-- fact_05 --> Her female friends and relatives, the jie mei or "sisters", hold the door until he meets their demands, which include a red packet and declaring his love in public in inventive ways. <!-- fact_18 --> Historically, the bride's friends would not "surrender" her until satisfied with red packets from the groom's representative, with good-natured haggling. <!-- fact_33 -->

Timing can matter. A Singapore wedding planner quoted by the Heritage Board says Teochew families believe the bride should leave home before sunrise, so they hold the door games very early in the morning. <!-- fact_06 --> If either family is Teochew, ask early, because it moves hair and make-up into the small hours.

### Older customs that are often told: the crying bride, the sedan chair and the groom's arrows

A bride who wept before leaving home, a red sedan chair carrying her to the groom's house, and a groom who shot arrows towards the chair appear in many family stories; how they were done varied too widely to set down as one account. ## The Chinese wedding tea ceremony, step by step

In Singapore, the first tea ceremony is at the groom's home: where deities and ancestors are worshipped at home, the couple pray at the family altar, then serve tea to the family elders, who give their blessings. <!-- fact_07 -->

The ceremony at the groom's home officially recognises the couple as husband and wife after the "three bows": to heaven and earth, to parents, and to each other. <!-- fact_19 --> The tea is usually brewed with lotus seeds, two red dates and two longans. <!-- fact_20 -->

### 6. Who serves whom, and in what order

In the order Singapore's National Library Board gives, tea is offered first to the gods, then to the groom's ancestors, then to the groom's parents, then to senior family members and other relatives. <!-- fact_21 --> Those being served sit, while the couple kneel, or bow if they prefer, and offer the cup with both hands. <!-- fact_21 --> Each person served gives the couple a red packet or a piece of jewellery. <!-- fact_21 -->

The bride's family has its own sitting. The couple hold a separate tea ceremony at the bride's house, after she changes into a kua, a red wedding dress embroidered with auspicious symbols. <!-- fact_08 -->

That means two ceremonies on one day, with travel between homes. Agree the list of elders in advance, and set out a cushion for kneeling.

### What UNESCO says about tea and social practice

In 2022, UNESCO inscribed [traditional tea processing techniques and associated social practices in China](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-tea-processing-techniques-and-associated-social-practices-in-china-01884) on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, and its text calls tea an important part of ceremonies such as weddings. <!-- fact_100 --> <!-- fact_101 --> It does not describe the wedding tea ceremony itself. <!-- fact_101 -->

## Chinese wedding symbols: red, double happiness, dragon and phoenix

### 7. Red and the double happiness character

The V&A says red is the primary colour for traditional Chinese wedding ceremonies. <!-- fact_112 --> The double happiness character (shuang xi) is used at weddings, and the British Museum holds a pack of red wedding envelopes printed with it. <!-- fact_118 -->

Paper-cuts carry the same motifs. UNESCO's listing for [Chinese paper-cut](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chinese-paper-cut-00219), inscribed in 2009, names weddings among the festivities paper-cuts are made for. <!-- fact_103 --> Many families paste a red double happiness on the doors and windows of the couple's homes, and the story of how the character came to be is often told, so ask your family how they use it.

### 8. The dragon and the phoenix

According to the V&A's record, the dragon was said to represent the male and the phoenix the female, and side by side they symbolise the balance of yin and yang of bride and groom. <!-- fact_114 -->

## What does a Chinese bride wear?

### 9. Qun kwa, qipao and outfit changes

The V&A describes the bride's matching red jacket and skirt as what is popularly known as hong gua ("red gown"), sometimes qun kwa, with a design still dominated by the dragon and phoenix, often in metal couched threads covering the satin. <!-- fact_113 --> In Singapore, the bride changes into a kua, a red dress embroidered with auspicious symbols, before the tea ceremony at her family's house. <!-- fact_08 -->

The V&A notes that white wedding dresses are popular today, but traditional bridal ensembles are still worn for rituals in front of parents and ancestors and during the wedding banquet. <!-- fact_115 -->

Some brides also choose a qipao (cheongsam), and in many families the bride changes outfits during the banquet. For your day: if you plan more than one outfit, build the changes into the run sheet. For more on the colour itself, read [why Chinese brides wear red](/why-chinese-brides-wear-red-dresses).

## The wedding banquet and red envelopes

### 10. The wedding banquet

The bride often appears at the banquet in her red ensemble. <!-- fact_115 --> The bridal cakes the bride's family once shared with friends and relatives served as an invitation to this feast. <!-- fact_30 -->

The dishes, the number of courses, what each is meant to bring and the rounds of toasting are often told, and they vary widely by community and by household. Ask the elders which dishes they would miss, and build the menu from there.

### 11. Red envelopes (hong bao, lai see)

The British Museum notes that lucky red envelopes are called hong bao in Mandarin and lai see in Cantonese, and that its pack was designed for making gifts of money at weddings. <!-- fact_117 --> The envelopes carry the double happiness character. <!-- fact_118 -->

They change hands at the bride's door and at the tea ceremony. <!-- fact_18 --> <!-- fact_21 --> Guests usually give one at the banquet too, and families have their own sense of a fitting, lucky amount, so ask the couple's family if you are unsure.

## After the wedding: the bride's return visit

### 12. The return visit (hui men)

A community heritage summary says that historically, about three days after the wedding, the couple visited the bride's family home, where she was received as a guest. <!-- fact_32 --> The National Museum of Singapore describes san zhao hui men as a traditional element of a Chinese wedding in which the bride returns home with the groom on the third day. <!-- fact_26 -->

In Singapore today, the National Library Board says, the bride returns home on the wedding day, and the tea ceremony is performed for her parents and relatives then. <!-- fact_22 --> Historically, the groom presented a whole roast pig to his in-laws; the meat was shared among the bride's relatives, and the head and tail were returned to the groom for "a good beginning and end". <!-- fact_23 -->

### Safety level and entry

When we checked on 6 November 2026, [Smartraveller advice for China](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/asia/china) was "Exercise a high degree of caution" overall, updated 28 September 2026, with higher levels in some areas. <!-- fact_104 --> It says "Reconsider your need to travel" to Gyirong County in Tibet because of the risk of severe flooding and landslides, and "Exercise a high degree of caution" in Tibet and Xinjiang because of the security situation. <!-- fact_105 --> The overall level applies elsewhere in China, including eastern cities such as Suzhou and Hangzhou, so check the advice for your exact location before you book.

Smartraveller also says Australians may be at risk of arbitrary detention, exit bans or harsh enforcement of local laws, including broadly defined National Security Laws. <!-- fact_107 --> China does not recognise dual nationality, and dual nationals who don't enter on their Australian passport may be unable to get Australian consular help. <!-- fact_107 -->

Australia is in China's visa waiver program until 31 December 2026, and ordinary passport holders may be able to enter visa-free for up to 30 days for tourism or visiting family, among other purposes; an electronic arrival card is required. <!-- fact_106 --> If your wedding is in 2027, check the entry rules again closer to the date.

### Paperwork and which office to ask

Some countries ask for proof that you are free to marry, commonly a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI), and Smartraveller says to contact the destination's embassy or consulate for the details. <!-- fact_109 --> DFAT issues CNIs through Australian Passport Offices, Australian embassies and consulates overseas, and by mail, and fees apply. <!-- fact_110 --> Some authorities only accept a CNI issued in their own country; in that case, the Australian embassy or consulate there can issue it. <!-- fact_110 --> The [Smartraveller guide to getting a CNI](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-certificate-no-impediment-marriage) sets out the Australian side.

In China, ask the [Australian Embassy in Beijing](https://china.embassy.gov.au/) <!-- fact_111 --> or the Consulates-General in Shanghai, Guangzhou and Chengdu whether you need a CNI for a marriage there and how to get one. ### Back in Australia

You can't register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law; Smartraveller suggests talking to a lawyer to confirm. <!-- fact_108 --> Its page on [getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) has the detail.

### Bringing Chinese customs to a wedding at home

Couples marrying in Australia can carry the rites into the day. Two tea sittings, one for each family, can fit into the morning before the ceremony if you allow travel time between the two homes, and a red ensemble for the banquet can sit beside a white gown. <!-- fact_07 --> <!-- fact_08 --> <!-- fact_115 --> Because each dialect group has its own practices, the best starting point is a conversation with both sets of parents. <!-- fact_09 -->

### Looking after older guests

Before you set the order of the day, ask how long each family expects its tea sitting to take, who drives elders between the two homes, and where they can rest. If you are marrying in China, ask early about flights and airport transfers for older guests, step-free access, and travel insurance and medical cover that suit their age and health.

## Chinese wedding traditions FAQ

### What are the main Chinese wedding traditions?

In Singapore's Chinese communities, heritage bodies describe betrothal gifts (guo da li), hair-combing, door games and the tea ceremony, and practice varies by dialect group. <!-- fact_01 --> <!-- fact_04 --> <!-- fact_05 --> <!-- fact_11 -->

### What happens at a Chinese wedding tea ceremony?

The couple kneel or bow and offer tea to their elders with both hands, in order of seniority. <!-- fact_21 -->

### Who attends the tea ceremony?

Parents, senior family members and other relatives, at the groom's home and then the bride's. <!-- fact_21 --> <!-- fact_08 -->

### What is the hair-combing ceremony?

Shang tou is held the night before in both homes, as a woman of good fortune combs the hair four times with an auspicious phrase each time. <!-- fact_04 -->

### What should a guest wear to a Chinese wedding?

Red is the primary colour of the ceremonies; families differ on whether guests should wear it, so ask the couple. <!-- fact_112 -->

### Do guests give red envelopes at a Chinese wedding?

Red envelopes (hong bao, lai see) are made for gifts of money at weddings; guest custom varies by family. <!-- fact_117 -->

### Can Australians get married in China?

Yes. As at November 2026, ask the embassy whether you need a CNI, and ask the registry and the embassy which documents, translations and fees apply. <!-- fact_109 --> <!-- fact_110 -->
