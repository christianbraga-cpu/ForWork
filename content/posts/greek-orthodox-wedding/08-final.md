## What is a Greek Orthodox wedding?

In the Orthodox Church, marriage is one of the sacraments. It is not a juridical contract, and the rite contains no vows or oaths. <!-- fact_01 --> The couple is led in procession, prayed over and blessed, hears the readings, and is crowned. <!-- fact_02 -->

The service has two parts. The Betrothal Service blesses the rings. The Marriage Service crowns the bride and groom. <!-- fact_301 --> In Greek the Marriage Service is called *stepsis*, which means crowning. <!-- fact_303 -->

As the Archdiocese of Australia describes it, a priest leads the rite before a covered ceremonial table in the middle of the Soleas, the raised area in front of the Holy Altar. On the table rest the Holy Gospel, a cup of wine, the betrothal rings and the wedding crowns. The groom stands on the right and the bride on the left. <!-- fact_300 -->

The words are sung and prayed by the priest and the chanter. The couple's part is to be present, to receive the blessings, and to share the cup. The koumbaros or koumbara, your sponsor, has the most active role of anyone else in the church, exchanging the rings and the crowns. <!-- fact_302 --> <!-- fact_304 -->

## Who can marry in the Greek Orthodox Church?

[The Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of Australia's regulations](https://greekorthodox.org.au/services-and-sacraments/marriage/) say its weddings are performed by a priest it recognises as canonical. The laws for weddings come from the Holy Canons of the Orthodox Church, and the Archdiocese applies them to every Greek Orthodox priest in Australia. <!-- fact_310 --> The rules shown here are the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of Australia's. If you are marrying elsewhere, ask your own parish priest which rules apply. For a wedding in Greece, confirm the requirements with your parish priest and the local Metropolis.

When both partners are Greek Orthodox, both must have been baptised in a canonical Orthodox Church. If either of you was baptised in a non-canonical church, speak to a canonical priest first. <!-- fact_316 --> Marriages with members of the Oriental Orthodox Churches, such as the Coptic, Syrian, Armenian and Indian Orthodox Churches, are permitted. <!-- fact_316 -->

There must also be no impediment of relationship under the Canons of the Church and civil law. The prohibited marriages cover close family and in-laws, first cousins, foster relationships, and godparents and godchildren, including godchildren of the same godparent. <!-- fact_311 --> In families where the same godparent has baptised several cousins, that last rule is worth raising early.

### If one partner is not Orthodox

A marriage cannot take place in the Orthodox Church between an Orthodox Christian and a non-Christian. The non-Orthodox partner must belong to a Christian denomination the Church accepts, for example Roman Catholic, Anglican, Uniting, Lutheran or Presbyterian. <!-- fact_313 --> If your partner belongs to another denomination, ask your priest about it before anything else.

The baptism itself matters too. A baptism performed by a female pastor or member of clergy is not accepted. <!-- fact_313 --> If you are not sure who baptised you, ask your parish of baptism for the certificate and raise it with your priest early. A partner who is not Christian, or who was never baptised, can marry in the Church only after baptism in a denomination the Church accepts. <!-- fact_315 -->

Having been baptised in the name of the Holy Trinity, both of you are asked to declare that any children will be baptised in the Eastern Orthodox Church and raised in the Orthodox faith. <!-- fact_314 --> The non-Orthodox spouse does not become a member of the Church through the wedding and may not receive Holy Communion, and non-Orthodox clergy may not co-celebrate the service. <!-- fact_315 -->

That last point shapes a mixed-faith family's day. If a minister from the other partner's church is close to you, they can be welcomed as a guest, or invited to speak at the reception.

### Second marriages and other cases the priest will raise

Under the Archdiocese's rules, the Church allows divorce and remarriage, but no more than two times. Second and third weddings are usually not considered sacraments, and their prayers have a penitential character. <!-- fact_309 -->

A divorced partner needs a civil divorce certificate and then an ecclesiastical divorce, the Church's own dissolution of the earlier marriage. For a second or third marriage, both must be issued at least one calendar month before the new wedding. <!-- fact_322 --> A widowed partner provides the late spouse's death certificate. <!-- fact_321 -->

Once the priest has confirmed that the Church can marry you, the Archdiocese's requirements come down to papers, timing, a sponsor and a date the Church allows. The parish priest gives directions on documents and on Archdiocese and local church fees, which vary with each couple's circumstances. <!-- fact_320 -->

### Documents the parish usually asks for

Both partners present:

- A birth certificate if born in Australia, or a passport if born overseas. <!-- fact_321 -->
- A baptismal certificate. <!-- fact_321 -->
- For an Orthodox partner who has never married, a Certificate of Celibacy (*Agamias*) from their local parish priest, signed by two witnesses. <!-- fact_321 -->
- If widowed, the late spouse's death certificate. <!-- fact_321 -->
- If divorced, the civil divorce certificate and the ecclesiastical divorce. <!-- fact_322 -->

Order baptismal certificates early, especially from parishes overseas or from another denomination.

### Civil licence and church licence

In Australia, one service covers both the Church and the State. Every Greek Orthodox priest in Australia is also a registered civil celebrant, authorised to marry you under both State and Ecclesiastical law. The priest performs the civil marriage only together with the religious service, on the same day. <!-- fact_319 -->

Two forms start the process: the Notice of Intended Marriage, for the Registry of Births, Deaths and Marriages, and the Application to the Archdiocese. Both are completed in the presence of the priest at least one calendar month before the wedding, and preferably three. <!-- fact_320 --> Build that window into your planning, particularly if you live interstate or overseas.

A wedding in Greece follows its own steps, which you confirm with the local Metropolis and your venue. That track is covered in the Greece section below.

### Choosing your koumbaros or koumbara

The koumbaros or koumbara, the sponsor who stands beside you, is usually Orthodox. A non-Orthodox Christian of an acceptable denomination may stand as sponsor at the wedding, but may not become godparent at the christenings of the couple's children. <!-- fact_312 -->

That second point matters if you hope your sponsor will also baptise your children, so choose with both roles in mind.

The sponsor's practical job on the day is to exchange the rings and the crowns. <!-- fact_302 --> <!-- fact_304 -->

### Dates when weddings are not held

Under the Archdiocese of Australia's rules, marriages are not permitted: <!-- fact_317 -->

- From 13 December until Christmas Day.
- On 5 January, the eve of Epiphany.
- From Great Lent until Easter Sunday, which moves each year.
- From 1 to 15 August, for the Dormition of the Theotokos.
- On 29 August.
- On 14 September.

Easter falls on a different date each year, so confirm the calendar with your priest for your year before you hold any date. For a wedding in Greece, check the closed dates with the Metropolis as well.

The order below follows the service as the Archdiocese sets it out. Each parish has its own pace and its own chanter, so walk through it with your priest at the rehearsal.

### The Service of Betrothal and the rings

Every Orthodox betrothal is a double ring ceremony. The priest blesses each of you with the rings three times, and the rings are placed on the fourth finger of the right hand. <!-- fact_302 --> The koumbaros then exchanges them between you, over and under, three times. <!-- fact_302 -->

There is no moment when you slip a ring onto each other's hand. Tell your photographer, so they are watching the sponsor's hands and not waiting for the couple.

### The candles and the joining of hands

Two candles are lit as a reminder that Christ is "the Light of the world". <!-- fact_300 --> Ask your priest who holds them and how your parish dresses them.

The Marriage Service then begins. The priest joins your right hands, and they stay joined for the rest of the service, a sign of the couple's "oneness". <!-- fact_303 -->

### The crowning with the stefana

The crowning is the climax of the Marriage Service. <!-- fact_304 --> The priest crowns you both, and the koumbaros exchanges the crowns three times between your heads while the priest and chanter sing "Lord our God, crown them in glory and honour." <!-- fact_304 -->

This is the moment for the photographer, and for the people in the front rows. Seat your parents where they can see it.

### Readings and the common cup

Two readings follow: the Epistle from St Paul's Letter to the Ephesians 5:20-33, and the Gospel from John 2:1-11, the wedding at Cana. <!-- fact_305 -->

In remembrance of Christ's first miracle at Cana, you share a cup of wine as a sign of unity with each other and with Christ. It is not Holy Communion. It stands for the "common cup of life", the sharing of joy and sorrow. The priest offers it first to the groom, then to the bride. <!-- fact_306 -->

### The Dance of Isaiah

The priest, holding the Holy Gospel, leads you in a procession in a circle, a symbolic dance for the joy of God's presence, while the priest and chanter sing "Rejoice, Isaiah". <!-- fact_307 -->

These are your first steps as a married couple. Keep the train and the veil manageable for the slow procession.

### The removal of the crowns and the blessing

After the procession the priest blesses the groom and the bride and removes the crowns during a prayer. <!-- fact_308 --> Before congratulating you, he separates your joined right hands with the Holy Gospel, signifying that nothing and no one except God should come between you. <!-- fact_308 -->

Then the line forms. Guests come forward to congratulate you, and the celebration can begin.

## What are stefana, the Orthodox wedding crowns?

Stefana are the wedding crowns. They are a sign of the bond between bride and groom, and represent the glory and honour God bestows on the couple. <!-- fact_304 --> Crowns were once usually plaited from lemon blossoms or flowers; today they are often silver or gold. <!-- fact_304 -->

The crowns are not optional. Under the Archdiocese's rules, without the rings and the crowns, the wedding cannot commence. <!-- fact_318 -->

Who buys the stefana is a family conversation. Agree it early, so no one arrives with a second pair.

## Are there vows in a Greek Orthodox wedding?

No. [The Orthodox Church in America](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/marriage) describes the sacrament as containing no vows or oaths. <!-- fact_01 --> The prayers, the blessings, the crowning and the shared cup carry the meaning instead.

If personal words matter to you, keep them for the reception. A letter read at the welcome dinner, or a few lines in the speeches, sits comfortably beside the service rather than inside it.

## How long is a Greek Orthodox wedding?

There is no fixed length, so ask your priest for your church's running time. The service is made up of the Betrothal and the rings, the joining of hands, the crowning, the readings and the common cup, the Dance of Isaiah, and the removal of the crowns. <!-- fact_301 --> <!-- fact_303 --> <!-- fact_307 --> <!-- fact_308 -->

If older guests will be in the church, ask whether there is seating for them.

The Archdiocese fixes two timings. The groom and groomsmen should arrive at least 15 minutes before the service, and the bride's party at least five minutes before. The custom of the bride arriving late has no basis in religious tradition. <!-- fact_318 -->

## What to wear to a Greek Orthodox wedding

The church is a place of worship first. Smartraveller advises dressing modestly in churches and religious buildings in Greece. <!-- fact_110 --> Ask your priest whether your parish has a dress expectation, and pass it on to guests with the invitation.

For the bride, covered shoulders inside the church are the simplest choice: a sleeve, a bolero or a light wrap that comes off for the reception. Consider the length of the train, too, with the Dance of Isaiah in mind.

For guests, a short note on the invitation saves awkward moments at the door. A shawl for the church, a change of shoes for the island streets, and lighter layers for the evening cover most summer weddings in Greece.

### The church and the paperwork

For a wedding in Greece, start with your parish priest in Australia, then confirm every step with the Metropolis where you will marry.

Venues describe the process in their own terms. Elounda SA Hotels & Resorts in Crete offers Greek Orthodox ceremonies alongside civil and symbolic ones. <!-- fact_114 --> Elounda SA lists these steps for its Orthodox weddings: <!-- fact_118 -->

1. Each document except passports gets its own Apostille. <!-- fact_118 -->
2. Then a Greek translation. <!-- fact_118 -->
3. Copies go for approval at least four weeks before the date, with the Orthodox approval coming from the Metropolis. <!-- fact_118 -->
4. All documents must be issued within three months of the wedding date. <!-- fact_118 -->
5. After the ceremony, the couple registers the marriage at the local Registry Office within 40 days. <!-- fact_119 -->

These are one venue's steps, not the rule for all of Greece. Elounda SA charges the Metropolis fee separately. <!-- fact_116 -->

Some venues set their own rules as well. Mykonos Grand states that both bride and groom must be Christian Orthodox to hold a religious wedding on the hotel premises. <!-- fact_113 -->

As at November 2026, the Australian side starts with proof that neither of you is already married. Smartraveller lists the Certificate of No Impediment (CNI) and certificates from a registry of births, deaths and marriages as the most common documents. <!-- fact_107 --> DFAT can issue a CNI in Australia, but some authorities only accept one issued locally, so ask whether you need it, where to get it, and whether it needs an apostille. <!-- fact_106 -->

[The Australian Embassy in Athens](https://greece.embassy.gov.au/athn/notarial.html) issues CNIs, witnesses notices of intended marriage, and legalises documents by authentication or apostille. Every notarial service needs an appointment, though CNI and apostille documents can be submitted by post, and a fee applies. <!-- fact_103 --> The Embassy does not give legal advice. <!-- fact_104 --> For the church papers, your guides are your parish priest and the Metropolis, along with the venue's own wedding specialist where the venue has one. <!-- fact_320 --> <!-- fact_118 -->

Back home, you cannot register an overseas marriage in Australia, but it is usually recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. Smartraveller suggests a lawyer confirms the certificate is valid here. <!-- fact_108 --> For any wedding in Greece, check with the Metropolis, the municipality and the Australian Embassy in Athens, including any fees. ### Questions to ask the priest and the venue

- **Music.** Will the chanter be provided, and can musicians play outside the church before and after?

- **Dress.** Are covered shoulders expected inside the church?

- **Language.** Will the service be in Greek, or partly in English for guests?
- **Guest numbers.** How many people fit inside the church, and where do the rest stand? If your guest list is larger than the chapel, ask the venue and the priest how they handle the guests who do not fit inside.

## Greek Orthodox wedding questions, answered

### Can a non-Orthodox partner marry in a Greek Orthodox church?

Under the Archdiocese of Australia's rules, yes, if they are a baptised Christian of a denomination the Church accepts, such as Roman Catholic, Anglican, Uniting, Lutheran or Presbyterian. A non-Christian partner can marry in the Church only after baptism in an accepted denomination. <!-- fact_313 --> <!-- fact_315 --> Raise your partner's denomination at your first meeting with the priest.

### What documents do we need for a Greek Orthodox wedding?

In Australia, the Archdiocese asks for a birth certificate or passport and a baptismal certificate for each of you, plus a Certificate of Celibacy for an Orthodox partner never married before. <!-- fact_321 --> Divorced or widowed partners need extra papers. The Notice of Intended Marriage and the Application to the Archdiocese are completed with the priest at least a month ahead. <!-- fact_320 -->

### Who can be a koumbaros or koumbara?

Usually an Orthodox Christian. Under the Archdiocese's rules, a non-Orthodox Christian of an acceptable denomination may stand as sponsor at the wedding, but cannot become godparent to your children. <!-- fact_312 -->

### Are there vows in a Greek Orthodox wedding?

No. The Orthodox sacrament contains no vows or oaths. <!-- fact_01 --> The blessing of the rings, the crowning and the common cup carry the meaning.

### How long is a Greek Orthodox wedding?

There is no fixed length, so ask your priest for your church's running time. The Archdiocese asks the groom to arrive at least 15 minutes early and the bride's party at least five minutes early. <!-- fact_318 -->

### Can a Greek Orthodox wedding be held outside a church?

Plan on a church or chapel. The Archdiocese of Australia describes the rite taking place in church, before the Holy Altar. <!-- fact_300 --> If you have a beach or garden in mind, raise it with your priest first. In Greece, several resorts have their own Orthodox chapel on the grounds, such as Nana Princess in Crete. <!-- fact_121 --> Confirm any chapel with your priest and the Metropolis.

### Can we have a Greek Orthodox wedding in Greece if we live in Australia?

Yes. Start with your parish priest, then confirm the Church's requirements with the local Metropolis. For the civil side, check with the municipality and the Australian Embassy in Athens, including any fees. <!-- fact_103 -->
