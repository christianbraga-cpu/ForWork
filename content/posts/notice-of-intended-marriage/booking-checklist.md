# Paperwork checklist: Notice of Intended Marriage

These details sit with the couple and their authorised celebrant (or, if marrying legally abroad, the destination's authorities), so they don't block publishing. The Wedded Advisor plans the celebration; the NOIM, documents and legal steps stay with the couple and their celebrant or the local authority. The article has no `[[VERIFY]]` markers in the copy and no prices (nothing for Nic to sign off); the only publishing blockers are image credits and licences (5 slots).

All items are as at November 2026, per the Attorney-General's Department and the Marriage Act 1961. Reconfirm each one with your celebrant for your date before relying on it.

## Marrying legally in Australia: the NOIM

### Timing window

- [ ] Choose and book your authorised marriage celebrant first: the NOIM goes to the celebrant who will marry you.
- [ ] Earliest: the NOIM can reach your celebrant up to 18 months before the wedding, and no earlier.
- [ ] Latest: it must be with your celebrant at least one month before the wedding. Treat this as a floor, not a target.
- [ ] Book the separate, in-person meeting each of you has with the celebrant before the ceremony.
- [ ] If your date moves, ask your celebrant whether your NOIM still stands.
- [ ] Changing celebrants: the NOIM can be transferred to another authorised celebrant, including at your request.

### Form

- [ ] Download the current NOIM from the Attorney-General's Department (PDF or Word; form updated 1 September 2021). Use the official version only.
- [ ] Complete your details; ask your celebrant about any field you are unsure of.

### Witness, by where you sign

- [ ] Check the list of authorised witnesses in the form's "Signatures of parties and witnesses" section before booking the appointment; ask your celebrant if unsure.
- [ ] Signing in Australia: in person, or remotely by audio-visual link with a witness who is also in Australia (an authorised celebrant can be the remote witness).
- [ ] Signing overseas: in person only, with an authorised witness who is also overseas and is not a celebrant. No online witnessing.
- [ ] Overseas: ask your celebrant how they would like to receive the signed NOIM, and leave room for the in-person meetings once you land.

### Documents

- [ ] Evidence of date and place of birth (birth certificate or passport).
- [ ] Evidence of identity (driver's licence or passport).
- [ ] Proof that any previous marriage has ended (divorce papers or a deceased spouse's death certificate).
- [ ] Ask your celebrant how to present the documents, and keep the originals together in one folder.

### To confirm with your celebrant (not stated in the article)

- [ ] Whether any document issued overseas or not in English needs an official translation, and what translations they accept.
- [ ] Whether documents issued overseas need an apostille or other authentication.
- [ ] The celebrant's fee, what it includes, and the cost of applying for the official marriage certificate if they do it for you.
- [ ] Where the legal ceremony can take place.
- [ ] Any local step in the state or territory where you will marry.

### If there is less than one month to go: shortening of time

- [ ] Talk to your celebrant first.
- [ ] A prescribed authority (for example a registrar of a local court or of a state registry of births, deaths and marriages) may approve it; find one on the list of prescribed authorities by state and territory.
- [ ] Grounds listed: employment-related or travel commitments; wedding or celebration arrangements; medical reasons; legal proceedings; an error in giving notice.
- [ ] Approval is never automatic: treat it as a fallback, not a plan.

### On the day and after

- [ ] You, your celebrant and two witnesses over 18 sign three marriage certificates; the celebrant gives you a ceremonial certificate.
- [ ] The celebrant lodges the paperwork with the registry of births, deaths and marriages in the state or territory where you married, within 14 days.
- [ ] Apply to the registry for your official marriage certificate (needed to change your name or prove you are married); your celebrant can do this for you, generally at a cost.
- [ ] Once married in Australia you cannot marry again here; a later ceremony abroad is a celebration, confirmation of vows or recommitment.

## Marrying legally abroad (no NOIM)

- [ ] An Australian celebrant cannot marry you overseas, and an overseas marriage cannot be registered in Australia. Follow the destination's own rules.
- [ ] Ask the destination's embassy or consulate for its document list and any extra rules, such as a residence period.
- [ ] Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI): whether you need one, where to get it (DFAT can issue one in Australia, but some local authorities accept only CNIs issued in their own country), and whether it needs an authentication or apostille.
- [ ] Other documents some countries ask for: registry certificates, divorce papers or a deceased spouse's death certificate, original birth certificate and passport.
- [ ] Translations and local fees: confirm with the destination's authorities.
- [ ] Recognition: generally recognised in Australia if valid where it took place and valid under Australian law. Check with your state or territory registry whether the foreign certificate works for a name change.
- [ ] Start with Smartraveller's "Getting married overseas" page.
