A Notice of Intended Marriage, or NOIM, is the written notice every couple marrying in Australia gives to their authorised marriage celebrant before the wedding. As at November 2026, it must reach your celebrant at least one month and no more than 18 months before the day. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_03 --> Without it, the marriage cannot go ahead. <!-- fact_100 -->

If you plan to marry legally in another country instead, the NOIM does not come into it: you follow that country's rules. <!-- fact_13 --> <!-- fact_17 -->

This guide takes the form step by step, then answers the question destination couples ask most: which paperwork applies to us? ## Key takeaways

- The NOIM is the written notice section 42 of the Marriage Act 1961 requires, and you give it to the authorised celebrant who will marry you. <!-- fact_100 -->
- It must reach your celebrant at least one month and no more than 18 months before your wedding. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_03 -->
- Since 12 June 2024 it can be witnessed in person or by audio-visual link, but a couple overseas needs a non-celebrant witness who is also overseas, in person. <!-- fact_101 --> <!-- fact_102 -->
- Destination couples either marry legally in Australia and celebrate abroad, which needs a NOIM, or marry legally abroad under local rules, often with a Certificate of No Impediment. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_13 --> <!-- fact_17 -->

## What is a Notice of Intended Marriage (NOIM)?

<!-- IMAGE SLOT couple-signing-celebrant: Landscape 3:2. Licensed real wedding with written couple consent only. Never AI. -->

The NOIM is a form published by the Attorney-General's Department. Section 42 of the Marriage Act 1961 says a marriage shall not be solemnised unless written notice of the intended marriage, on this form, is given to the authorised celebrant solemnising it. <!-- fact_100 --> The form downloads as a PDF or a Word document. <!-- fact_100 -->

The rules for marrying in Australia sit in the Marriage Act 1961 and the Marriage Regulations 2017. <!-- fact_01 --> To marry here you must give the notice to an authorised marriage celebrant within the time window, and be married by an authorised marriage celebrant. <!-- fact_02 --> You must also not already be married, not be marrying a close relative, be at least 18 (unless a court has approved a marriage where one person is 16 to 18), and understand what marriage means and freely agree to it. <!-- fact_10 -->

You do not need to be an Australian citizen or permanent resident to marry in Australia. <!-- fact_09 --> If one of you wants to live here after the wedding, look into a visa with the Department of Home Affairs. <!-- fact_09 -->

The NOIM is the first document your celebrant will ask for, so the choice of celebrant comes first. Our guide on [how to choose a wedding celebrant](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/how-to-choose-a-wedding-celebrant) covers what to look for. The Attorney-General's Department keeps the current form on its [Notice of Intended Marriage page](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/publications/notice-intended-marriage).

## When do you need to lodge your NOIM?

<!-- IMAGE SLOT timing-calendar: Landscape 3:2 or 4:3. Still life of a linen-bound paper calendar or planner opened to a month view, a few dates lightly circled in ink or marked with small dried florals, a fountain pen and a cup of coffee. No readable brand or personal data. -->

As at November 2026, the timing rule is simple and fixed. <!-- fact_02 -->

### The earliest you can lodge

Your celebrant can receive the NOIM up to 18 months before your wedding, and no earlier. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_03 --> For a couple who books a venue two summers ahead, that means the date is often secured long before the paperwork can begin.

### The latest you can lodge

The notice must be with your celebrant at least one month before the wedding. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_03 --> Treat that as a floor, not a target. Your celebrant also needs to meet each of you separately and in person before the ceremony, and that meeting takes diary time on both sides. <!-- fact_06 --> <!-- fact_07 -->

### What happens if you miss the window

If there is less than one month until your wedding, talk to your authorised celebrant. You may still be able to marry if a prescribed authority approves it. <!-- fact_05 --> The full rules are under "Can the one-month notice be shortened?" below.

## How to fill out the NOIM form step by step

The form itself is short. What matters is the order: form, witness, celebrant, documents. For a fuller picture of the celebrant's role, read [what a celebrant does, at home and abroad](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/what-does-a-celebrant-do-and-do-you-need-one-abroad).

### Step 1: Get the official form

Download the current NOIM from the Attorney-General's Department, in PDF or Word format. <!-- fact_100 --> The form was updated on 1 September 2021. <!-- fact_08 --> Use the official version only, so every field matches what your celebrant expects.

### Step 2: Complete your details

Complete your details on the form. If you are unsure about any field, the Attorney-General's Department's advice is to ask your authorised celebrant for help with the form. <!-- fact_03 -->

### Step 3: Sign in front of an authorised witness

Your signatures must be witnessed. The people who can act as authorised witnesses are listed on the form itself, in the section headed "Signatures of parties and witnesses". <!-- fact_103 --> Since 12 June 2024 that witnessing can happen in person or remotely by audio-visual link, with location rules covered in the next section. <!-- fact_101 -->

### Step 4: Give it to your authorised celebrant

The completed, witnessed NOIM goes to the authorised celebrant who will marry you, within the one month to 18 month window. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_100 --> If you change celebrants later, the NOIM can be transferred to another authorised celebrant, including at your request. <!-- fact_07 -->

### Step 5: Provide your documents

Your celebrant will then ask for evidence of birth, identity and, where it applies, the end of an earlier marriage. The list is set out under "What documents do you need?" below. <!-- fact_06 -->

## Who can witness a Notice of Intended Marriage?

The authorised witnesses are listed in the "Signatures of parties and witnesses" section of the NOIM. <!-- fact_103 --> Read that list on the current form before you book an appointment with anyone. As at November 2026, the requirements for authorised witnesses are unchanged, and where you are when you sign decides who can witness. <!-- fact_102 -->

### Witnessing in Australia

If you are in Australia, your NOIM can be witnessed in person or remotely by audio-visual link. <!-- fact_101 --> For remote witnessing, the witness must also be in Australia, and that witness can be an authorised celebrant. <!-- fact_102 -->

### Witnessing when you are overseas

If you are outside Australia, your authorised witness must also be outside Australia, and cannot be a celebrant. <!-- fact_102 --> A NOIM for a person or couple outside Australia cannot be witnessed online. <!-- fact_102 --> Plan for an in-person appointment with an authorised witness where you live.

The Attorney-General's Department sets out these rules on its [Notice of Intended Marriage page](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/publications/notice-intended-marriage).

## What documents do you need?

As at November 2026, the Attorney-General's Department lists what your authorised celebrant will need alongside the NOIM: <!-- fact_06 -->

- Evidence of your date and place of birth, such as a birth certificate or passport. <!-- fact_06 -->

Your celebrant will tell you how to present them.

Keep the originals together in one folder. Later, you will need your official marriage certificate to change your name or prove you are married. <!-- fact_12 -->

## Lodging a NOIM when you live overseas

Many couples planning a wedding in Australia are living abroad when the paperwork starts. As at November 2026, you can complete the NOIM while you are overseas if you plan to marry in Australia. <!-- fact_04 -->

The witnessing rule is the part to plan around. Your authorised witness must be outside Australia with you, cannot be a celebrant, and must witness in person, because a NOIM for a couple outside Australia cannot be witnessed online. <!-- fact_102 --> Check the list of authorised witnesses on the form before you make the appointment. <!-- fact_103 -->

Once it is signed and witnessed, the NOIM still has to reach your authorised celebrant at least one month and no more than 18 months before the wedding. <!-- fact_02 --> Ask your celebrant how they would like to receive it, and leave room for the in-person meetings once you land. <!-- fact_06 -->

Supporting documents issued overseas raise their own questions. Our guide on [when your paperwork needs an apostille](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/what-is-an-apostille-and-does-my-paperwork-need-one) explains the term, and your celebrant will tell you what they accept.

## Can the one-month notice be shortened?

Sometimes. If there is less than one month until your wedding, you may still marry if a prescribed authority approves it. <!-- fact_05 --> As at November 2026, the grounds the Attorney-General's Department lists are: <!-- fact_05 -->

- employment-related or travel commitments
- wedding or celebration arrangements
- medical reasons
- legal proceedings
- an error in giving notice

A prescribed authority is an officer or employee of the Commonwealth, a state or a territory, appointed by the Attorney-General. Among other things, a prescribed authority can authorise a celebrant to proceed where the minimum one-month notice has not been given. <!-- fact_104 --> They include registrars and deputy registrars of local courts and of state registries of births, deaths and marriages. <!-- fact_104 -->

The [list of prescribed authorities](https://marriage.ag.gov.au/stateofficers/authorities) is searchable by state and territory. <!-- fact_104 --> Approval is never automatic, so start with your celebrant and treat a shortening of time as a fallback, not a plan.

## Does the NOIM differ by state?

The NOIM is one form, published by the Attorney-General's Department under the Marriage Act 1961. <!-- fact_100 --> After the wedding, your celebrant must lodge the marriage paperwork with the registry of births, deaths and marriages in the state or territory where you married, within 14 days. <!-- fact_11 -->

For any local step in the state or territory where you will marry, ask your celebrant.

## NOIM or marrying abroad: which paperwork applies?

For a destination wedding, the NOIM question comes down to where the legal marriage happens. There are two routes.

### Option A: marry legally in Australia, celebrate abroad

You hold a small legal ceremony in Australia with an authorised celebrant, and the NOIM applies exactly as above. <!-- fact_02 --> On the day, you, your celebrant and two witnesses over 18 sign three marriage certificates, and the celebrant gives you a ceremonial certificate. <!-- fact_11 --> Your celebrant then lodges the paperwork with the registry within 14 days. <!-- fact_11 --> Apply to the registry for your official marriage certificate, which you need to change your name or prove you are married; your celebrant can do this for you, generally at a cost. <!-- fact_12 -->

The celebration abroad can then be whatever you want it to be. Once your marriage is recognised, you cannot marry again, but you can hold another kind of ceremony, such as a confirmation of vows or a recommitment ceremony. <!-- fact_22 -->

Some couples choose a religious celebrant for the legal ceremony at home. In the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of Australia, for example, every priest is also a registered civil celebrant who performs the civil marriage together with the religious service on the same day, and the NOIM is completed in the priest's presence at least one calendar month before the wedding. <!-- fact_24 --> <!-- fact_25 -->

### Option B: marry legally abroad

An Australian authorised celebrant cannot perform a marriage overseas, and an overseas marriage cannot be registered in Australia. <!-- fact_13 --> The NOIM is not part of this route. Instead, you follow the destination's own rules.

Many countries ask you to prove you are not already married. The most common documents are a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI) and certificates from a registry of births, deaths and marriages, and some countries add rules such as a residence period. <!-- fact_17 --> Local authorities may also ask for divorce papers, a deceased spouse's death certificate, and your original birth certificate and passport. <!-- fact_18 -->

DFAT can issue a CNI in Australia, but some local authorities accept only CNIs issued in their own country. Ask the destination's embassy or consulate whether you need a CNI, where to get it, and whether it needs an authentication or apostille. <!-- fact_19 --> Our guides explain [what a certificate of no impediment is](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/what-is-a-certificate-of-no-impediment-and-who-issues-it) and [when your paperwork needs an apostille](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/what-is-an-apostille-and-does-my-paperwork-need-one). Smartraveller's [Getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) page is the official starting point.

In most cases, a marriage abroad is recognised in Australia if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_20 --> A foreign marriage certificate is usually accepted as proof of the marriage, but you may not be able to use it for a name change, so check with your local registry. <!-- fact_21 --> Read more on [whether you have to register an overseas marriage](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/do-you-have-to-register-an-overseas-marriage-at-home) and on [getting your overseas marriage certificate](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/overseas-marriage-certificate). If you are still deciding, our guide to [marrying legally abroad or holding a symbolic ceremony](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/should-you-marry-legally-abroad-or-have-a-symbolic-ceremony) weighs the two.

Either way, the legal steps stay with you and your celebrant or the local authority. The celebration is where we come in.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a Notice of Intended Marriage last?

As at November 2026, the NOIM must be given to your celebrant at least one month and no more than 18 months before your wedding. <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_03 --> Lodge it inside that window and keep your celebrant informed of any change of date.

### Who can witness a NOIM?

The authorised witnesses are listed on the form, in the "Signatures of parties and witnesses" section. <!-- fact_103 --> In Australia, a remote witness must also be in Australia and can be a celebrant; overseas, the witness must also be overseas and cannot be a celebrant. <!-- fact_102 -->

### Can we lodge a NOIM if we live overseas?

Yes. You can complete the NOIM while overseas if you plan to marry in Australia. <!-- fact_04 --> Your witness must be outside Australia with you and cannot be a celebrant, and the NOIM cannot be witnessed online. <!-- fact_102 -->

### Can we get married with less than one month's notice?

Possibly. Talk to your celebrant: you may be able to marry if a prescribed authority approves it, on grounds such as employment or travel commitments, celebration arrangements, medical reasons, legal proceedings or an error in giving notice. <!-- fact_05 -->

### Do we need a NOIM if we are getting married overseas?

No. The NOIM is for marriages solemnised in Australia by an authorised celebrant, and Australian celebrants cannot perform overseas marriages. <!-- fact_100 --> <!-- fact_13 --> Abroad, the local rules apply, often including a Certificate of No Impediment. <!-- fact_17 -->

### Can we change celebrants after lodging our NOIM?

Yes. A NOIM can be transferred to another authorised celebrant, including at the couple's request. <!-- fact_07 -->
