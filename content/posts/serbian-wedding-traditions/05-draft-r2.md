A Serbian wedding is less a single ceremony than a sequence: a visit to ask for the bride's hand, a party sent to collect her, a church service with crowns and a shared cup, and a welcome at her new home. Serbian wedding traditions are often reduced to a list of quirks. Seen in order, they make far more sense.

This guide walks the customs as the day unfolds, separates what the Serbian Orthodox Church sets from what families do, and sits alongside our [wedding traditions worth keeping](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions). For Serbian-Australian couples, it also covers the two real choices: marrying in Serbia, or carrying the customs home.

## Key takeaways

- The church service is set by the Serbian Orthodox Church, while the folk customs around it vary by family and region.
- The kum is the first of two church witnesses and exchanges the rings, a far larger role than the English "best man" suggests.
- The service centres on rings, crowns, a white cloth binding the couple's right hands, a shared cup of wine and a threefold walk around the table.
- Smartraveller rates Serbia "Exercise normal safety precautions" as at November 2026, and paperwork for a legal wedding there should be checked with the registry and the Australian Embassy in Belgrade.

## Serbian wedding traditions: what the day looks like, in order

The current exhibition at the [Ethnographic Museum in Belgrade](https://etnografskimuzej.rs/en/izlozba/serbian-wedding-traditions/), "Serbian weddings 125 years ago", follows the wedding through its phases: the prosidba (the visit to ask for the bride's hand) and preparations, leading the bride out of her family home, the wedding itself, and her arrival at her new home. <!-- fact_01 --> The museum presents it as a major family, social and ritual event. <!-- fact_01 -->

It also shows customs from several regions, including Šumadija, Vojvodina and the Pirot area, a reminder of how much practice varies. <!-- fact_02 --> The museum's English page says wedding customs brought families and communities together and marked the move from one stage of life and family status to another. <!-- fact_05 -->

So one framing line for everything below. The church rite is set by the Serbian Orthodox Church. The folk customs around it belong to families, and no two families keep them in quite the same way. Where a custom is passed on by word of mouth rather than documented, we say it is "often told".

## Before and at the church

### 1. The prosidba: asking for the bride's hand

The prosidba, the proposal visit to ask for the bride's hand, opens the sequence. <!-- fact_01 --> It is a family occasion first, and the tone is set by the two households rather than by any rule.

What follows, the engagement or veridba, is where family habits diverge. Gifts exchanged between the families, and an apple or a ring offered to the bride, are often told as part of it. How much of this a family keeps is a question for the grandparents, not a guidebook.

### 2. The kum and kuma

If there is one person who holds a Serbian wedding together, it is the kum. As the [Serbian Orthodox parish of Gabba](https://socgabba.org/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%87%D0%B0%D1%9A%D0%B5/) in Brisbane explains, the Serbian Orthodox Church requires two witnesses at the wedding: the kum and the stari svat. <!-- fact_06 --> Their larger role comes before the day, at the pre-marriage examination (predbračni ispit), where they attest that there is no impediment to the marriage and sign the record. <!-- fact_06 -->

That is why "best man" undersells the role. The kum is a witness to the sacrament, and during the service he exchanges the rings between the couple. <!-- fact_07 --> Later, he and the stari svat hold the couple's candles until the service ends. <!-- fact_08 -->

The kuma is often told as the kum's female counterpart, frequently his wife, standing with the bride. Calling her the "maid of honour" is a loose translation, not a description the church uses.

Families often speak of kumstvo, the bond between the couple and their kum, as lasting well beyond the wedding, with the kum often told as the natural choice to baptise the couple's children. Treat that as family tradition rather than rule, and ask what yours expects.

Choose your kum early. The Church of Saint Sava in Belgrade, for one, asks for his church baptism certificate as well as yours, so this is not a decision to leave until the invitations go out. <!-- fact_12 -->

### 3. The barjak, the wedding flag

On the morning of the wedding, the groom's party (the svatovi) sets out to collect the bride, and the barjak is often told as leading the way. The barjak is a wedding flag, and the man who carries it is the barjaktar.

Families decorate the barjak in their own way. Flowers, a towel or an apple tied to the staff are often told details, and the role of barjaktar is often given to a relative or close friend.

### 4. Collecting the bride, and buying her back

Leading the bride out of her family home is one of the phases the Ethnographic Museum names. <!-- fact_01 --> Around that moment sits one of the most playful customs.

When the svatovi arrive, the bride's family is often told to "sell" her rather than simply hand her over. The dever, usually the groom's brother or a close male relative, is often told as the one who negotiates. Some families add a "fake bride", a relative in a veil presented first to tease the groom's side.

It is theatre, not commerce, and the haggling is the point.

### 5. The apple

The apple is the custom people most often ask about. As it is often told, an apple is set high, sometimes on a tree or a pole, and the groom or a member of his party tries to hit it before the bride is led out.

The details vary widely from family to family. If your family wants to include the apple, talk it through with them and with your venue well ahead, since what suits a village courtyard will not suit a city hotel.

### 6. The Serbian Orthodox service: betrothal and rings

Now the church. Across the Orthodox Church generally, as the Orthodox Church in America explains, the marriage rite is a service of prayer and blessing, with the couple led into the church in procession; it contains no vows or oaths and is not a juridical contract. <!-- fact_03 --> In Orthodox practice generally, marriage is a sacrament for baptised members of the Church, and an Orthodox Christian may marry a baptised non-Orthodox Christian in the Church. <!-- fact_04 -->

The service begins with the betrothal (obručenje) in front of the altar doors. After the priest places the rings, the kum exchanges them between the couple, and they are worn on the fourth finger of the right hand as a sign of fidelity. <!-- fact_07 --> The couple hold candles here, which the priest then hands to the kum and the stari svat to hold until the end of the service. <!-- fact_08 -->

At the [Church of Saint Sava](https://hramsvetogsave.rs/vodic-kroz-pravoslavlje/vencanje-u-crkvi-pravoslavno-vencanje/) in Belgrade, the couple's and the kum's church baptism certificates go to the priest, along with confirmation of the groom's free marital status. <!-- fact_12 --> The church asks for the pre-marriage examination about three weeks before the wedding, so the marriage can be announced at three Sunday Liturgies. <!-- fact_12 -->

Requirements differ between dioceses and parishes, so for your own wedding, ask your parish priest.

### 7. Crowning, the common cup and the walk around the table

After the betrothal, everyone moves to the centre of the church. <!-- fact_08 --> The couple's right hands are bound with a white cloth, often kept afterwards, and wreaths or crowns are placed on their heads. <!-- fact_09 --> That gives the service its name: venčanje, from venac, a wreath. <!-- fact_09 --> Once a wreath was used; today it is mostly a crown. <!-- fact_13 -->

Then the common cup. The couple share red wine, taken in three sips. <!-- fact_13 --> The Gabba parish in Brisbane explains that the wine recalls the wedding at Cana of Galilee. <!-- fact_10 --> In a Serbian Orthodox wedding, the "wedding cup" is this common cup the couple share in church.

Finally, the priest leads the couple three times around the table holding the cross. The parish describes the circle as the eternal bond of the spouses and the three rounds as honouring the Holy Trinity. <!-- fact_10 --> If you have read about [the crowning in a Greek Orthodox wedding](/expert-advice/greek-wedding-traditions), much of this will feel familiar.

## The celebration

### 8. The kum's coins outside the church

As the couple leave the church, the kum is often told to throw coins, and sometimes sweets, for the children waiting outside.

What it means is a question for the family that keeps it.

### 9. The welcome at the groom's home

The museum's sequence ends with the bride's arrival at her new home. <!-- fact_01 --> The customs at the door are among the most tender of the day, and among the most varied.

The bride is often told to be welcomed with bread, and a small child is often told to be placed on her lap. What each gesture means is best heard from the family keeping it.

For a wedding in Australia, the welcome can move to the reception venue, with bread at the entrance.

### 10. The kolo

Then the dancing. UNESCO inscribed "Kolo, traditional folk dance" for Serbia on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2017. <!-- fact_100 --> It describes a collective dance in which dancers link to form a chain, usually moving in a circle holding hands with their arms down, performed to music at private and public gatherings. <!-- fact_101 -->

According to [UNESCO's listing for the kolo](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/kolo-traditional-folk-dance-01270), it is danced at celebrations of the most important events in people's and communities' lives. <!-- fact_102 --> UNESCO does not name weddings; a wedding is often told as the place many guests first join one.

The kolo is learnt mainly by joining in, with skilled dancers encouraging others to step in and improve. <!-- fact_103 --> For overseas guests, a few steps practised before the trip are enough.

### 11. Music through the day

Live music is often told as running from the morning procession to the end of the night, with brass bands or folk musicians leading the svatovi and the kolo. UNESCO describes the kolo as performed to the accompaniment of music. <!-- fact_101 -->

Which songs belong to which moment is family and regional knowledge, so ask an older relative and brief your musicians early.

## Customs you may hear about, and how they are told

Two more customs come up often. Neither is church practice, and both vary so much that they are best treated lightly.

### Mirrors and garlic

As old folk belief has it, a bride might carry a small mirror or a clove of garlic for protection on her wedding day. These are stories families tell, not something anyone needs to do, and we make no claim that they work.

### The stolen shoe

At some receptions, the bride's shoe is often told to go missing, taken by guests or the groom's party, and the groom's side wins it back. If you want the game, decide in advance who runs it, so it stays playful.

## Marrying in Serbia as Australians

For Serbian-Australian couples, a wedding in Serbia can mean family close by, and paperwork across two countries. What follows is general information as at November 2026, not legal advice.

### Travel advice as at November 2026

The [Smartraveller advice for Serbia](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/serbia), updated 27 October 2026, is "Exercise normal safety precautions", one level for the whole country. <!-- fact_104 --> It notes unexploded landmines in the southern area bordering Kosovo and advises using well-known roads and paths there. <!-- fact_104 -->

Australians can visit as tourists without a visa for up to 90 days in any 6-month period, though entry conditions can change at short notice. <!-- fact_105 --> You must register with local police within 24 hours of arrival, and your hotel or host normally arranges it. <!-- fact_106 --> If guests are staying with family or in a private villa, confirm the host will register them.

Smartraveller also asks travellers to declare currency and high-value items, such as jewellery and cameras, above a set threshold on arrival. <!-- fact_108 --> Mention it to anyone carrying wedding jewellery or camera gear.

### Paperwork and recognition in Australia

Some countries ask you to prove you are not already married, and each decides what proof it accepts. <!-- fact_110 --> A Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI) is a common one. <!-- fact_110 --> DFAT can issue a CNI in Australia, but some local authorities only accept one issued in their own country. <!-- fact_111 --> The [Australian Embassy in Belgrade](https://serbia.embassy.gov.au/bgde/consular.html) lists CNIs and apostilles among its notarial services; fees apply for most services, and appointments are booked online. <!-- fact_109 -->

So build the paperwork backwards. Ask the Serbian authorities whether they accept a CNI issued in Australia, and whether it needs an apostille or authentication, before you book anything. <!-- fact_111 --> The [Smartraveller guide to getting a CNI](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-certificate-no-impediment-marriage) notes that at least one of you must be an Australian citizen or permanent resident, and that non-English supporting documents need a professional English translation. <!-- fact_114 -->

For the Serbian civil registry steps, and for any questions about same-sex marriage, check with the registry and the embassy, including any fees. <!-- fact_115 --> Smartraveller notes only that same-sex relationships in Serbia are legal but not widely accepted. <!-- fact_107 -->

Back home, you can't register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_112 --> The foreign certificate is usually accepted as proof of marriage but may not work for a name change, so check with your registry. <!-- fact_112 --> Smartraveller's page on [getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) suggests talking to a lawyer to confirm validity. <!-- fact_112 -->

## Bringing Serbian customs to a wedding in Australia or overseas

Many Serbian-Australian couples marry at home, in their parish, and carry the folk customs into a reception venue. The church side starts with your parish priest.

Meet the priest to set the date before you book a reception venue. The Gabba parish does not hold weddings during fasts, on Wednesdays and Fridays, at Easter or in Bright Week, or on 18 January, 11 September and 27 September. <!-- fact_11 --> A Saturday you love may simply not be available.

The parish also asks couples to bring rings, candles and a white cloth. <!-- fact_11 --> The number of candles differs between churches, so confirm it with yours. <!-- fact_13 --> Give the kit to a named person on the day.

The folk customs travel well. A barjak at the head of the bridal party, bread at the reception door, the kum's coins on the church steps and a kolo once the music starts all work in a hotel ballroom or a vineyard. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is a Serbian wedding?

A sequence of family customs around a Serbian Orthodox church service: the prosidba, collecting the bride, the wedding, and her arrival at her new home. <!-- fact_01 --> The church rite is set by the Church; the folk customs vary by family and region. <!-- fact_02 -->

### What does the kum do at a Serbian wedding?

The kum is the first of two church witnesses, with the stari svat. <!-- fact_06 --> He attests at the pre-marriage examination that there is no impediment, exchanges the rings during the betrothal and, with the stari svat, holds the couple's candles until the service ends. <!-- fact_06 --> <!-- fact_07 --> <!-- fact_08 -->

### Why do Serbian couples wear crowns at the wedding?

The crowning gives the service its name, venčanje, from venac, a wreath. <!-- fact_09 --> Wreaths were once used; today it is mostly crowns. <!-- fact_13 --> For what the crowns mean in your parish, ask your priest.

### What happens with the apple at a Serbian wedding?

As it is often told, an apple is set high and the groom or his party tries to hit it before the bride is led out. It is a folk custom that varies by family, not part of the church service.

### What is the kolo?

A collective circle dance, inscribed by UNESCO for Serbia in 2017, in which dancers link hands in a chain and move to music. <!-- fact_100 --> <!-- fact_101 --> Guests learn it by joining in. <!-- fact_103 -->

### Can Australians marry in Serbia?

Smartraveller rates Serbia "Exercise normal safety precautions" as at November 2026. <!-- fact_104 --> For a legal wedding, ask the Serbian authorities what proof of no impediment they accept and check with the registry and the Australian Embassy in Belgrade, including any fees. <!-- fact_111 --> <!-- fact_109 --> <!-- fact_115 -->

### What should guests wear to a Serbian wedding?

There is no single dress code, so follow the invitation. For the church, ask the couple what their parish expects, and wear shoes you can dance the kolo in.
