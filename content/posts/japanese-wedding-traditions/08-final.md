A Japanese wedding is a meeting of two families as much as two people. Parents are present at every stage, from the first formal exchange of gifts to the letter a bride reads aloud at the end of the reception.

Japanese wedding traditions follow that arc, and this guide walks through them in wedding order: yuinō, the Shinto ceremony and san-san-kudo, the shiromuku and the change of kimono, the chapel ceremony many couples add, then the hirōen with its gifts, its sake barrel and its speeches. It closes with what Australian couples need to know about marrying in Japan, or bringing these customs home. For more [wedding traditions worth keeping](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions), see our wider guide.

## Key takeaways

- A traditional Japanese wedding often pairs a Shinto ceremony in kimono with a Christian-style ceremony in Western dress on the same day.
- The bride's white shiromuku belongs to the ceremony, and a change into a coloured kimono, or a Western gown, marks her return for the reception.
- Guests give goshūgi, a gift of money in a formal envelope, and leave with hikidemono, the gifts the couple give in return.
- For Australians marrying in Japan, Smartraveller advises normal safety precautions, and the legal steps are confirmed with the municipal office and the Australian Embassy.

## What a traditional Japanese wedding looks like today

The modern Japanese wedding is a blend of East and West: a traditional Shinto ceremony, with the bride and groom in elaborate kimonos, is typically followed by a Christian-style observance in formal Western attire. <!-- fact_01 -->

Families still shape the day. Arranged marriage, miai-kekkon, has largely given way to the love match, yet it is still common for a family friend, relative or mentor to act as go-between, the nakodo, even when the couple met on their own. <!-- fact_02 -->

Customs vary from family to family and region to region, and between Shinto, chapel and civil-only weddings. Ask both families early which moments matter most to them, and build the day around those.

## 1. Yuinō: the betrothal gift exchange between families

In Japan, the proposal between two people is followed by a formal step between two families. Yuinō is the betrothal ceremony at which the two families exchange gifts to mark the engagement.

In many families, the groom's side presents a set of symbolic gifts, each wrapped and tied with mizuhiki, decorative cords of twisted paper, and arranged on a low stand. The bride's family receives them and offers gifts in return. Some families invite the nakodo to carry the gifts between the households.

<!-- IMAGE SLOT yuino: Licensed detail photo of a yuinō gift set from a consented family: wrapped gifts with mizuhiki cords arranged on a low lacquered stand on tatami, soft side light, no people or hands only. No money envelopes or visible amounts. Never AI. -->

Today many couples keep a simpler form. The two families meet over a formal meal, sometimes at a restaurant or hotel, to be introduced and to mark the engagement together.

For a Japanese-Australian couple, this is often the moment the families first sit at one table. Decide early who will host, and whether relatives who cannot travel will join by video.

## 2. The Shinto wedding ceremony before the kami

The Shinto wedding is the heart of the traditional day. Japanese people usually hold their wedding ceremonies in Shinto style and pronounce their wedding vows to the kami, the deities of Shinto. <!-- fact_07 --> The traditional ceremony takes place at a Shinto shrine. <!-- fact_03 -->

The ceremony is small and quiet. Close family usually attend, seated on either side of the hall, with the couple at the front before the altar.

### Purification, prayer and the offering

Shinto rites follow a familiar shape. A grand Shinto rite moves through purification (harae), prayers (norito) recited by the chief priest, sacred music and dance, and offerings of small branches of the evergreen sacred tree tied with strips of white paper. <!-- fact_08 -->

At many shrine weddings, the couple and family representatives make that offering themselves, placing a sakaki branch on the altar in turn. A shrine maiden, the miko, often serves the sake and may perform a dance.

### Japanese wedding vows before the kami

Japanese wedding vows are spoken to the kami rather than only to each other. <!-- fact_07 --> At many ceremonies the groom reads the vows aloud from a folded paper, with the bride beside him, and the couple then exchange rings.

Treat the shrine as a place of worship first. Ask the priest what guests may photograph and wear, and brief friends from overseas on quiet, respectful conduct during the rite.

## 3. San-san-kudo: three cups, shared sake

San-san-kudo is the moment most guests remember. The bride and groom drink sake in turn from a set of three flat cups, small, medium and large, served by a miko. At many weddings the parents also drink, and the cups pass between the two families.

Sake is part of Shinto worship. It is common to have communion with the kami by drinking the offered sake after a festival. <!-- fact_09 -->

## 4. The bride's shiromuku, iro-uchikake and the groom's hakama

The bride's ceremony dress is white from head to toe. She wears a white under-kimono and a heavy white outer kimono known as a shiromuku. <!-- fact_03 -->

The groom wears formal black montsuki, a kimono with family crests, with a haori jacket and striped hakama trousers. Family members often wear their own formal kimono, the mothers in black.

### Wataboshi or tsunokakushi headwear

Brides choose between two forms of headwear. The wataboshi is a broad white hood that frames the face. The tsunokakushi is a band of white silk worn over a traditional hairstyle. Both are worn with the shiromuku, and many brides choose one for the ceremony and remove it for the reception.

### Changing into colour

Before joining family and friends for the reception, the bride exchanges the white outer kimono for an elaborate, brightly coloured one. <!-- fact_04 --> Outer kimono (uchikake) are now worn only by brides, and the custom of changing outfits continues, with red kimono worn after the ceremony. <!-- fact_05 -->

<!-- IMAGE SLOT bridal-dress: Licensed photo from a consented wedding or a museum with clear reuse rights: a bride in a richly embroidered iro-uchikake (cranes or pine motifs) in a garden or courtyard, soft light. Never AI. -->

Motifs on these robes are chosen with care. On a red uchikake, flying cranes are symbols of longevity. <!-- fact_04 --> You can see [wedding kimono in the V&A collection](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O126698/) online.

When you hire an uchikake, ask the stylist to explain its motifs, so you can share them with guests in the order of service.

## 5. Chapel weddings and Christian-style ceremonies

Many Japanese couples who are not Christian still marry in a chapel. A Christian-style observance in formal Western attire typically follows the Shinto ceremony. <!-- fact_01 -->

For many brides, the chapel is where the white Western gown appears. Today the "colour alteration" custom can also include a Western-style wedding dress, a popular choice with contemporary brides for their final reception outfit. <!-- fact_05 -->

Couples who want both moments plan two ceremony times and two looks on the same day. Allow generous dressing time between them: a shiromuku takes far longer to put on than a gown.

## 6. The hirōen reception and oiro-naoshi outfit changes

The hirōen is the wedding reception, and it is where Japan's marriage traditions open up to a wider circle. Colleagues, school friends and extended family join the couple, often at round tables, with the couple seated at a head table facing the room.

At many receptions the evening is closely run. A master of ceremonies guides the programme, from the first toast through the speeches, the cake and the entertainment to the closing words from the families.

<!-- IMAGE SLOT reception: Licensed photo from a consented Japanese or Japanese-Australian reception: the couple at the head table in their changed outfits (gown or iro-uchikake), guests seated at round tables. Never AI. -->

### Oiro-naoshi, the change of colour

At some point the couple leave the room and return in new outfits. This is oiro-naoshi, the change of colour, and it has a long record: Utagawa Kuniyoshi's woodblock print The Wedding Colour Alteration Ceremony dates from between 1843 and 1847. <!-- fact_10 --> At the culminating banquet of earlier times, a bride would wear a sequence of kimono, often in white, red and black, with matching designs. <!-- fact_06 -->

Today the bride might move from shiromuku to a red iro-uchikake, then to a Western gown, and the groom may change with her.

Build every outfit change into the run sheet, with a private dressing room close to the reception and a dresser on hand.

## 7. Goshūgi and hikidemono: gifts given and gifts returned

### Goshūgi, the gift of money

At Japanese weddings, guests give money rather than a gift from a registry. The gift is called goshūgi, and it is presented in a formal envelope, the shūgi-bukuro, decorated and tied with mizuhiki cords. Guests hand it in at the reception desk on arrival, where a family member or friend records each name.

Many guests take care that the notes are new and crisp, and the envelope is chosen for a wedding, never a plain one. Ask a Japanese relative or friend what is expected in your circle: the custom is personal, and families have their own sense of what is fitting. The red envelope at a Chinese wedding works in a similar way, which our guide to [Chinese wedding traditions](/expert-advice/chinese-wedding-traditions) covers.

### Hikidemono, the gifts in return

Hikidemono are the gifts the couple give their guests to take home. In many families they come as a set: a main gift, often something for the home, with a sweet or a small treat beside it, packed in a bag at each place. Some couples now offer a catalogue so each guest can choose their own.

## 8. Kagami biraki: opening the sake barrel

Kagami biraki is the opening of a sake barrel at a celebration. At many receptions, the couple stand before a straw-wrapped barrel and break open its wooden lid together with wooden mallets, while guests count them in.

<!-- IMAGE SLOT kagami-biraki: Licensed photo from a consented reception: the couple raising wooden mallets over a straw-wrapped komodaru sake barrel, guests cheering behind, motion and joy. Never AI. -->

The sake is then ladled into small cups and shared for a toast. It travels well to an Australian reception, with a short word from the MC to explain it.

## 9. Speeches and the bride's letter to her parents

Speeches carry much of the feeling at a Japanese reception. At many weddings a guest of honour, often a senior colleague or mentor, speaks first, followed by friends and family who share stories of the couple.

### The bride's letter

Near the end of the evening comes the moment that brings the room to tears. The bride stands and reads a letter to her parents, thanking them for raising her. Then the couple present their parents with flowers or a small gift, a quiet act of gratitude in front of everyone they love.

The groom's father, or a representative of both families, then closes the reception with words of thanks to the guests. If your parents are not used to speaking in public, give them notice, and offer a translator if half the room will not follow the language.

### Words guests use to congratulate the couple

A wedding in Japanese is kekkonshiki. Guests congratulate the couple with "go-kekkon omedetō gozaimasu", the formal way to wish them well on their marriage. In speeches, many guests avoid words linked to parting or repetition, a courtesy that Japanese relatives can explain.

## 10. Senbazuru: a thousand paper cranes

Senbazuru is a string of a thousand folded paper cranes. Folding cranes has a long history in Japanese paper art: one of the earliest known origami instruction books, Akisato Rito's Sembazuru orikata of 1797, showed how to fold linked cranes from a single square sheet. <!-- fact_110 -->

Some couples fold a thousand cranes in the months before the wedding, often with help from friends and family, and hang them as a display at the reception or keep them framed at home afterwards.

## 11. Marrying in Japan as Australians, or bringing the customs home

As at November 2026, [Smartraveller advice for Japan](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/asia/japan) is "Exercise normal safety precautions" in Japan overall. Its one higher level, "Reconsider your need to travel", covers the restricted areas near the Fukushima Dai-ichi nuclear power plant. <!-- fact_102 --> Check it again before you send invitations.

### Travel and timing

Severe heat waves can affect Japan between July and September, and the typhoon season runs from May to November, with most activity between July and September. <!-- fact_103 --> Spring and autumn dates suit outdoor portraits and guests who are travelling far.

Australians don't need a visa to visit Japan for up to 90 days, but entry and exit conditions can change at short notice, and you cannot change your entry status, for example to a spouse visa, without leaving and re-entering. <!-- fact_104 --> Couples planning to stay on after marrying should sort their visas before they fly.

For a wedding in Japan, Kyoto, Tokyo and Hakone offer gardens, city life and mountain views. ### Legal steps

You can't register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases [a marriage overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) is legally recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law; talk to a lawyer to confirm. <!-- fact_106 --> Some countries ask for proof you are free to marry, commonly a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI), and some local authorities only accept a CNI issued in their own country. <!-- fact_107 -->

If a CNI must be issued in the country, the Australian embassy or consulate there can issue it, and fees apply. <!-- fact_108 --> In Japan, the [Australian Embassy in Tokyo](https://japan.embassy.gov.au/tkyo/notarial.html) and the Australian Consulate-General in Osaka provide notarial services by appointment only; mail service is available from within Japan only, and a CNI application sent by mail is accepted only if it is witnessed by an Australian mission or a local notary public. <!-- fact_109 -->

Before you book, put these questions to the municipal office (the kuyakusho or shiyakusho) where you plan to marry, and to the Australian Embassy:

- Which documents does the municipal office need from each of you, and does it accept a CNI issued in Australia or only one issued in Japan?
- Do your documents need an apostille or a certified Japanese translation?
- Does a shrine or chapel ceremony make the marriage legal, or is a separate registration needed?
- What fees apply, at the municipal office and at the Embassy?

## Japanese wedding traditions FAQ

### What happens at a traditional Japanese wedding?

A Shinto ceremony in kimono, typically followed by a Christian-style observance in Western dress, <!-- fact_01 --> then the hirōen reception with speeches, a change of outfits and the bride's letter to her parents.

### What is san-san-kudo?

The sharing of sake from three cups by the bride and groom during a Shinto wedding, and at many weddings by their parents too.

### What is the Japanese wedding kimono called?

The bride's white ceremony kimono is the shiromuku. <!-- fact_03 --> For the reception she changes into a brightly coloured outer kimono, the iro-uchikake. <!-- fact_04 -->

### What gifts do guests give at a Japanese wedding?

Guests give goshūgi, money in a formal envelope tied with mizuhiki cords, handed in at the reception desk.

### What is hikidemono?

The gifts the couple give each guest to take home, often a main gift with a sweet beside it.

### What should guests wear to a Japanese wedding?

Formal dress. Women often wear a cocktail dress or formal kimono and avoid all-white, which belongs to the bride; men wear a dark suit. Ask the couple if the shrine has its own guidance.

### Can Australians have a legal wedding in Japan?

In most cases, yes: an overseas marriage is legally recognised in Australia if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_106 --> Check documents and fees with the municipal office and the Australian Embassy in Tokyo.
