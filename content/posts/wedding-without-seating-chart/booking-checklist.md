# Open seating planning checklist

Use this when you decide how guests will sit and brief your venue, caterer and MC. Ask for the venue's and caterer's answers in writing rather than in a phone call.

## Decide the format
- [ ] How will dinner be served: canapés, shared platters, food stations, one long table or a plated meal?
- [ ] If the meal is plated, or guests choose their main course with their RSVP, plan at least assigned tables
- [ ] Do your guests already know each other, or are there groups who will meet for the first time?
- [ ] Are there blended families or guests who need space? If so, plan their tables quietly
- [ ] Pick your approach: open seating, assigned tables rather than seats, reserved tables with the rest open, long feasting tables, or lounges and standing areas with a few dining tables
- [ ] For a weekend abroad, decide each event separately: welcome drinks, the wedding dinner, the recovery lunch

## Reserve tables for elders and family
- [ ] Reserve a table or two for parents, grandparents and the wedding party
- [ ] Reserve your own seats: a sweetheart table, a head table or two places at a long table
- [ ] If where you place a parent, grandparent or elder is a sign of respect in your family, reserve their table and tell them ahead of time
- [ ] Check sightlines so reserved tables see the speeches and first dance without turning their chairs
- [ ] Ask a friend to bring guests who only know the two of you into their group

## Enough seats, and where the spares sit
- [ ] Ask your venue how many extra seats it would set for your layout
- [ ] Agree where the spare places will sit: spread across tables or kept together as open tables
- [ ] Agree where extra chairs are kept close by, and who adds them during the evening
- [ ] Make sure a family arriving together won't be split up

## Signage and the MC announcement
- [ ] Say it on your wedding website: please sit wherever you like, with family tables marked as reserved
- [ ] A welcome sign at the reception with the same message
- [ ] Mark reserved tables with warm, neutral wording such as "reserved seating" or "accessible seating"
- [ ] Ask your MC to repeat it as guests move to dinner
- [ ] Brief your venue coordinator and MC: which tables are reserved and for whom, where spare chairs are kept, and when guests should be seated
- [ ] Build those moments into your wedding day timeline

## Dietary list to the caterer early
- [ ] Collect dietary needs and allergies with your RSVPs
- [ ] Keep names, meals and allergies in one guest list
- [ ] Give your caterer the list of guest allergies early, whatever your seating
- [ ] Agree whether guests with allergies sit at a known table so their plates reach them

## Accessible seats, with family
- [ ] Reserve seats with clear access for older or less mobile guests, close to the door, the bathrooms and the speeches
- [ ] Seat guests who use a wheelchair with their own family and friends, not all together
- [ ] Leave space between seats, and remove chairs so guests who use wheelchairs can reach the tables

## Questions for the venue
- [ ] Does the catering team need table numbers or a seating plan for plated service?
- [ ] Which table layouts suit the terrace, the garden and any wet weather space?
- [ ] Is there step-free access to the terrace, the garden and the bathrooms for older or less mobile guests?
- [ ] Are there house rules on service times or music that shape when guests need to be seated?
- [ ] How many extra seats would you set for our layout, where will they sit, where are spare chairs kept, and who adds them on the night?
- [ ] Who on your team will guide guests to their seats and to the reserved tables?

## Questions for the caterer
- [ ] How does the kitchen handle allergens?
- [ ] How will special meals be identified and served at the table?
- [ ] Do table numbers help your team serve the right meal to the right guest?
- [ ] When do you need final numbers and the dietary list?
- [ ] For a wedding overseas: how does the venue's kitchen handle allergens, given Australian rules may not apply?
