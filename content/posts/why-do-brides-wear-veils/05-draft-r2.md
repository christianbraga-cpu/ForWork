Why do brides wear veils? Brides wear veils for history, faith and family, and for the moment it is lifted. The veil has been part of bridal dress since Roman brides wore a great flame-coloured one, and it has changed shape and purpose many times since. <!-- fact_01 -->

This guide follows the history of the wedding veil from Rome to Queen Victoria's lace, through its place in faith traditions, and on to the choices that matter now: who lifts it, whether to wear a family veil, and how a veil behaves on a windy clifftop. For cuts and lengths, see our guide to [wedding veil styles and lengths](/style-advice/wedding-veil), and for the wider picture, our guide to [wedding traditions](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions).

## 1. Why brides wear veils today

Ask a room of brides why they chose a veil and you will hear a dozen answers. For some it completes the dress. For others it is a nod to a mother or grandmother, or a requirement of the ceremony they are marrying in. For many it is about one moment: the veil lifted, and the first clear look at each other as a married couple.

### The wedding veil meaning in folklore

In English folklore, the veil is about luck. It is widely counted lucky to wear something on the wedding day that a happy bride has already worn, and women once took it as a compliment to be asked to lend their wedding veils to friends for exactly that reason. <!-- fact_12 --> Many say the borrowed piece should come from a previous wedding whose marriage turned out happily, and the veil is [often singled out as the luckiest](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/acref/9780198607663.001.0001/acref-9780198607663-e-1105). <!-- fact_11 -->

That is why a veil sits so naturally within [something old, something new](/expert-advice/something-old-something-new). A mother's veil can be old, borrowed and a family story all at once.

## 2. The veil in ancient Rome

A Roman bride's appearance was set by ritual. Her hair was arranged in six locks with woollen fillets, she wore a straight white woven tunic fastened at the waist with a "knot of Hercules", and over it all a great flame-coloured veil. <!-- fact_01 --> The knot was to be untied later by her husband. <!-- fact_02 -->

June was the favourite month to marry. The day before, the bride put away her toga praetexta. <!-- fact_02 --> Her dress and appearance were prescribed by custom, down to the veil and shoes. <!-- fact_02 -->

The Roman bridal veil was [flame-coloured, not white](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/acref/9780192801463.001.0001/acref-9780192801463-e-1365), and one part of a carefully staged look. <!-- fact_01 -->

## 3. Medieval and religious veiling

The bridal veil tradition outlived Rome, but it changed. In late antiquity it looked different from its Roman ancestor and carried a different meaning. <!-- fact_03 --> The link between veil and bride likely shaped how church fathers thought about women who entered monastic life: they should be veiled, as befitted "brides of Christ". <!-- fact_03 -->

In the early Christian centuries, the bridal veil became part of the Christian wedding ceremony, adapted from the Roman model. <!-- fact_04 --> At the same time, women consecrated to the service of God "took up the veil" as a [symbol of their marriage to Christ](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/acref/9780198524038.001.0001/acref-9780198524038-e-990) and a sign of chastity. <!-- fact_04 -->

### The medieval wimple

Medieval women covered their heads in daily life too. After the crusaders brought Near Eastern fashions home, Western women took up the wimple as a chin veil. <!-- fact_06 --> Usually of fine white linen or silk, it framed the face and covered the neck and sometimes part of the bosom. <!-- fact_06 -->

The wimple was everyday dress, not a bridal veil. It shows how ordinary head covering once was.

## 4. Queen Victoria and the white lace veil

The bridal look most of us picture today owes a great deal to one wedding. When Queen Victoria married in 1840, she wore rich white satin trimmed with orange-flower blossoms, a wreath of the same blossoms on her head, and a veil of Honiton lace thrown over the wreath, not so as to conceal her face. <!-- fact_07 -->

Victoria helped popularise white, setting a royal precedent with a simple ivory satin dress in the fashion of the day. <!-- fact_08 --> Earlier royal brides had worn white, but often woven or heavily embroidered with gold or silver. <!-- fact_08 --> By 1800 it was already usual for a bride to wear white or cream, a colour that implied [purity, cleanliness and social refinement](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1223045/). <!-- fact_08 -->

### Lace, worn at the back

Victoria wore English lace to promote British manufacture, and encouraged other brides to do the same. <!-- fact_09 --> A Honiton lace veil made in Devon in about 1850 carries a pattern of pea-pods, symbols of fruitfulness and happiness. <!-- fact_09 --> Its bride would have worn it attached to the back of her head, not over her face, a fashion which came later. <!-- fact_106 -->

That is the Victorian veil in one line: long, lace, worn back from a crown of flowers. A bride who wants the most faithful version of that look chooses the same, rather than a blusher.

### Veils kept in the family

A Honiton lace veil worn at St James's Church, Piccadilly, in February 1865 was designed so its pattern sits on the outside when the veil is folded over. <!-- fact_10 --> The matching dress, made more than 20 years after the Queen's wedding, was based on hers. <!-- fact_10 --> The veil, dress and a pair of boots stayed in the bride's family until her daughter bequeathed them in 1947. <!-- fact_107 -->

Orange blossom stayed with the veil into the new century. A veil of 1902 was worn with a wax orange blossom hair ornament and hair piece, arranged on the head together with the veil. <!-- fact_108 -->

## 5. The wedding veil meaning in faith traditions

For many couples, the veil is part of the ceremony itself. Many faiths and cultures have their own bridal head coverings, each with its own meaning, and the right person to explain yours is the officiant or family elder who will stand with you.

### The Jewish badeken

Jewish women covered their hair in public as an act of modesty from biblical times, and the earliest clear evidence for the bridal veil in Jewish custom dates from the early Middle Ages. <!-- fact_05 -->

At a Jewish wedding, the badeken (veiling) follows the short pre-chupah receptions hosted by the bride and groom. A procession led by the groom goes to the bride's reception room, where he covers her face with a veil. <!-- fact_100 --> The fathers and grandfathers of the bride and groom then bless her, and her face stays veiled for the whole chupah ceremony, giving her privacy at a holy time. <!-- fact_101 -->

Jewish tradition traces the custom to the Matriarch Rebecca, who covered her face when she met her groom, Isaac. <!-- fact_102 --> In [Jewish teaching](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/477333/jewish/Badeken-Veiling.htm), the veil shows that the groom values the bride's inner beauty, which never fades, and not only her outward beauty. <!-- fact_103 --> In Chassidic custom, the veil is dense enough to hide the bride's face completely, with no silver or gold thread, because bride and groom wear no jewellery to the chupah. <!-- fact_104 -->

If you are planning a Jewish ceremony overseas, give the badeken its own place in the run sheet. It needs a separate room near the ceremony space, time for family to gather, and an early conversation with your rabbi about how dense the veil should be.

### Christian ceremonies

The bridal veil has been part of the Christian wedding since the early Christian centuries. <!-- fact_04 --> In many church weddings today, the bride walks in with her face veiled and the veil is lifted at the altar. In others, it is worn back throughout. Ask your priest or minister whether the church has a custom of its own, particularly for a Catholic or Orthodox ceremony abroad.

## 6. Who lifts the veil, and when

Who lifts the veil is a choice, not a rule, and couples settle it in a few different ways.

- **The partner, after the vows.** The most familiar version: the veil comes back just before the first kiss.
- **A parent, at the top of the aisle.** A mother or father lifts it as they hand over, so the bride stands at the altar with her face clear.
- **The bride herself.** Many brides prefer to lift it alone, or to wear it back from the start.
- **No lifting at all.** A long veil worn back never covers the face, so the question falls away.

Partners of any gender can lift the veil, and it is worth deciding who before the rehearsal. Practise once with the actual veil and headpiece: a blusher has to lift cleanly over the crown without catching on pins or a comb, and the person lifting it should know how far to fold it back.

Tell your photographer and celebrant which version you have chosen. It is a few seconds long, and it is a photograph many couples treasure.

## 7. The blusher, wearing it back and heirloom veils

The blusher is the short layer worn forward over the face, ready to be lifted during the ceremony. For lengths and how it pairs with a longer veil, see our guide to [wedding veil styles and lengths](/style-advice/wedding-veil). Here the question is how you use it.

### Forward or back

Many brides walk in with the blusher down and lift it, or have it lifted, at the altar. Others fold it back over the crown from the start, so it adds volume behind the head without ever covering the face. Both are right. The Victorian veil was worn back, not over the face; face-covering veils came later. <!-- fact_106 -->

If the veil will be folded forward, check which side the pattern sits on. Some period lace veils were designed so the pattern reads on the outside when folded over, and photographs catch both the back and the front. <!-- fact_105 -->

### Wearing a family veil

An heirloom veil carries a family into the ceremony, and it can be your something borrowed. <!-- fact_11 --> Start the conversation early. A family veil may need cleaning, a new comb or a repair to the edge, and some veils are fragile enough to be carried or displayed rather than worn.

Agree with the owner how the veil will be worn, who carries it there, and when it goes home.

## 8. Wearing a veil at a destination wedding

A veil that floats softly down a chapel aisle behaves very differently on a clifftop. Plan for the setting, not the fitting room.

**Wind.** On a coast or a hilltop, sheer tulle lifts and wraps. Secure the veil firmly to the hair or headpiece, and ask a bridesmaid or your planner to settle it before each walk and each photograph. The movement can be lovely in pictures, so long as someone is ready to catch it.

**Heat.** In summer sun, a heavy lace veil over the shoulders adds warmth. Many brides choose a lighter fabric for an outdoor ceremony and keep the heirloom lace for the portraits.

**Beach and garden.** Long veils trail through sand, grass and gravel. Decide whether yours will be lifted and carried between the ceremony and the photographs.

**Timing.** The usual rhythm is the veil on for the ceremony and portraits, then off for the reception. Plan where it goes when it comes off, and who looks after it.

### Travelling with your veil

Carry the veil in your hand luggage, folded with tissue. As at November 2026, standard travel insurance policies typically don't cover items stolen from checked baggage on an airline, train or bus, or items left unattended. <!-- fact_110 --> Keep bags close in transit, and use the hotel safe for valuables. <!-- fact_111 --> Policies often limit each item's value, so check the item limits for a valuable heirloom veil. <!-- fact_109 --> Check the current [Smartraveller](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/travel-essentials/travel-insurance) guidance before you fly.

If the veil belongs to someone else, ask whether their own insurance covers it overseas before it leaves home.

## 9. Do you have to wear a veil?

No. A veil is a choice, and plenty of brides leave it out. The question is what you want in its place, and how the people around you will feel about it.

**What to wear instead.** Fresh flowers in the hair, a single comb or pin, a hair vine, a headband, or nothing at all. A floral crown echoes the Victorian wreath of orange blossom without the veil. <!-- fact_07 -->

**When the ceremony asks for a head covering.** Some religious ceremonies expect one. Ask your officiant early, and if a veil is not what you want, ask whether a scarf or mantle would suit.

**Family expectations.** A mother who has kept her veil for decades may hope to see it again. Talk before you shop. Many brides find a middle way: wearing the family veil for the ceremony only, carrying a piece of its lace in the bouquet, or wearing it for portraits the day before.

If you are torn, try one on at your fitting. Many brides know at once.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does the veil cover the bride's face?

It depends on the tradition. At a Jewish wedding, the groom covers the bride's face at the badeken, and it stays veiled through the chupah, giving her privacy at a holy time. <!-- fact_101 --> In many other weddings, a blusher is worn forward so it can be lifted during the ceremony. The Victorian veil was worn back, not over the face. <!-- fact_106 -->

### Who takes the veil off the bride's face?

Usually the partner after the vows, a parent at the top of the aisle, or the bride herself. Couples decide in advance and practise once at the rehearsal.

### Can I wear my mother's wedding veil?

Yes, and a veil borrowed from a happily married bride has long been counted lucky. <!-- fact_11 --> Have it checked early for cleaning or repair, and agree with your mother how it travels and when it goes home.

### When do you take the veil off?

Most brides wear it for the ceremony and portraits, then take it off for the reception.

### Is a wedding veil religious?

It can be. The veil has been part of the Christian wedding since the early Christian centuries <!-- fact_04 -->, and the badeken is part of a Jewish wedding. <!-- fact_100 --> Many brides wear one for reasons of family or style.

### What does a veil symbolise in a wedding?

In Jewish tradition, it shows that the groom values the bride's inner beauty, not only her outward beauty. <!-- fact_103 --> In English folklore, a borrowed veil from a happy bride brings luck. <!-- fact_12 -->
