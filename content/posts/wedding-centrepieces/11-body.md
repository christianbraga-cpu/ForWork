## Key takeaways

- Choose the centrepiece for your table shape first: one focal piece for a round table, a continuous run for a long one.
- Keep arrangements low enough to talk over or lift them well clear of eye level, never in between.
- Greenery, candles and repurposed ceremony flowers can carry a design further than more stems.
- Ask the venue about open flame, set-up and pack-down before you fall for a design.

## How to choose wedding centrepieces

Four decisions narrow a mood board quickly. Make them in this order and the shortlist gets much shorter.

### Table shape

A round table asks for a single focal point. Everything radiates from the centre, so the piece needs to read well from every seat, with no back side.

A long or banquet table asks for a continuous line. Instead of one arrangement, you are designing a run: a garland, a rhythm of repeating vessels, or clusters with candles between. A single tall piece on a long table looks marooned.

Mixing the two is common in larger rooms, with a long head table and rounds for guests. Let one material, a colour or a vessel finish, carry across both so the room reads as one design.

### Height and sightlines

This is the rule that matters most. Guests need to see and speak to the person opposite. Keep arrangements low enough to talk over, or lift them high enough that the blooms sit well above seated eye level on a slim stand. The middle height, a solid wall of flowers at face level, is the one to avoid.

### Scale against the room

A low garden bowl that feels generous in a courtyard can disappear in a ballroom with a soaring ceiling. Tall rooms can carry height; intimate rooms and marquees usually suit low, abundant pieces.

Guest count matters too. The more tables, the more each design repeats, and repetition is where a simple idea gains presence. For the layout guests walk into, see our pick of [interactive seating chart displays](/expert-advice/our-favourite-interactive-seating-charts).

### Where the money goes

Stems are the expensive part of most arrangements, so the levers are about using fewer of them well:

- Let foliage do more of the work, with fewer, larger focal blooms.
- Use candles and greenery to fill length on long tables.
- Move ceremony arrangements to the reception rather than buying twice.
- Mix statement tables with simpler ones, saving the most lavish piece for the head table.

## 30 centrepiece ideas by style

Each idea below is a starting point to adapt to your palette. For what is available when you marry, see [which wedding flowers are in season in Australia](/style-advice/fashionable-flowers-opt-for-in-season-or-order-imported).

### Low and lush florals

<!-- IMAGE SLOT low-florals: Close to mid shot along a dressed table showing a low, loose floral arrangement (garden-style or runner of blooms and foliage) sitting below eye level, with guests' sightlines across the table obvious. Natural or warm ambient light. No people, or guests only out of focus and not identifiable. Real wedding or venue styled shoot with recorded venue, town and photographer. -->

**1. The garden bowl.** A wide, shallow footed bowl filled loosely with blooms and trailing foliage, as if cut that morning. Garden roses, ranunculus and sweet peas give the soft, layered look; a few stems allowed to wander out of line keep it natural.
*Suits:* round tables in courtyards, gardens and smaller dining rooms.

**2. Clustered bud vases with an anchor.** One low central arrangement surrounded by a scatter of bud vases, each holding a single stem. It reads as generous without the stem count of a large piece, and guests can carry the small vases home.
*Suits:* round tables, or repeated along a long table.

**3. Floating flowers.** Open flower heads floating in shallow glass bowls, with a candle or two drifting between. Peonies, gardenias or open garden roses suit it; the water catches the light after dark.
*Suits:* small rounds and evening receptions.

**4. Tonal mound.** A low dome of a single colour family, built from several flower types in close shades. Ivory and blush feels classic; rust, terracotta and saffron feels warmer and more Mediterranean.
*Suits:* round tables where you want quiet impact from every seat.

**5. Ikebana-inspired line.** A single sculptural branch or a few stems set in a heavy low vessel, with plenty of empty space around it. It is restrained and architectural.
*Suits:* modern venues, minimal palettes and smaller guest counts.

### Tall and dramatic

<!-- IMAGE SLOT tall-centrepiece: Vertical or 4:5 shot of a tall centrepiece on a slim stand or tall vase with blooms well above seated eye level, room height visible (ballroom, chateau hall or vaulted space). Evening or interior light with candles at the base. No people, or guests not identifiable. Real venue recorded with town and photographer. -->

**6. Blooms on a slim stand.** A full arrangement lifted on a narrow gold, glass or acrylic stand, so the flowers float above the guests and the stem leaves the sightline clear. Hydrangeas, roses and orchids give the volume.
*Suits:* round tables in ballrooms and high-ceilinged halls.

**7. Branches in tall glass.** Flowering or leafy branches in tall clear cylinders, reaching up and out. The effect is airy and natural without needing a dense arrangement.
*Suits:* large rooms, barns and marquees with height to fill.

**8. Candelabra with florals.** A classic candelabra wound with greenery and a few blooms at its base or arms. It brings height and candlelight in one piece and suits a formal, old-world room.
*Suits:* formal round tables and the head table.

**9. Hanging installation.** Greenery or flowers suspended above the table rather than standing on it, with low candles below. The table surface stays clear for food and conversation.
*Suits:* long tables in marquees, barns and courtyards with rigging points.

### Long table garlands and runners

**10. Continuous foliage garland.** A single line of loose greenery running the full length of the table. Olive, eucalyptus, smilax and ruscus all give different textures, from silvery to deep green.
*Suits:* long banquet tables, indoors or out.

**11. Garland with tapers.** The same garland, threaded with taper candles in brass or glass holders at intervals. It is the look for long dinners that run into the night.
*Suits:* long tables at evening receptions.

**12. Repeating low vessels.** A rhythm of matching low arrangements placed down the table at even intervals, often with bud vases between. It feels ordered and generous, and each vessel can move to another table later.
*Suits:* long tables where you want structure rather than a wild garland.

**13. The King's Table.** One long shared table for every guest, styled as a single continuous run of flowers, candles and greenery. Our report on [the King's Table](/trend-report-the-kings-table) explores the format, and our guide to [table settings for every wedding theme](/style-advice/22-amazing-table-settings-for-every-wedding-theme) shows how glassware and place settings sit around the centrepiece.
*Suits:* smaller guest counts in villas, vineyards and courtyards.

### Candlelit

<!-- IMAGE SLOT candlelit-long-table: Wide shot down the length of a long banquet table at dusk or after dark, lit mainly by clusters of taper and pillar candles in hurricanes or candlesticks, with greenery or minimal florals between. Outdoor courtyard, vineyard terrace or barn setting. No people, or only distant non-identifiable guests. Real venue, town and photographer recorded. -->

Candles change a table more than any flower once the sun goes down. A candle is an open flame that can easily ignite combustible items nearby, so [general candle safety guidance](https://www.mfs.sa.gov.au/community-safety/safety-and-education/fact-sheets/candles2/mfs-fact-sheet-candles.pdf) is to keep dried grasses, paper menus and trailing greenery well clear of it. The same guidance calls for sturdy holders that will not tip over, made of material that cannot burn. Your venue sets its own rules on open flame, so ask before you design around real candles.

**14. Tapers at three heights.** Taper candles in holders of three different heights, grouped down the table among low greenery. The staggered flames add movement without any extra flowers.
*Suits:* long tables and evening receptions.

**15. Pillars in hurricane glass.** Chunky pillar candles inside tall glass hurricanes, ringed with a low wreath of foliage. The glass guards the flame, which matters on terraces where draughts can make wax melt unevenly and candles fall over.
*Suits:* outdoor tables, terraces and rounds.

**16. Votive clusters.** Dozens of small votives in glass, massed around a modest arrangement. The glow does the work, and the florals can be lighter.
*Suits:* round tables and the head table.

**17. Flameless candles.** Where the venue restricts open flame, battery-operated candles keep the glow and are a safe alternative to traditional candles. Choose ones with a warm, flickering light and real wax shells so they read well in photographs.
*Suits:* heritage interiors, marquees and any venue that limits flame.

### Fruit, foliage and edible

**18. Citrus and greenery.** Bowls or a scattered run of lemons, limes or blood oranges, some halved, among glossy leaves. The colour is bright by day and warm by candlelight.
*Suits:* long tables at lunch receptions and outdoor terraces.

**19. Fruit still life.** A painterly arrangement of grapes, figs, pomegranates and plums on a footed platter, with a few blooms tucked in. It suits a long, slow dinner.
*Suits:* long tables and family-style service.

**20. Herb pots.** Small terracotta pots of rosemary, thyme or basil down the table, with tapers between. The fragrance is light and savoury, and the pots make simple favours.
*Suits:* rustic and garden settings.

**21. Grazing as the centrepiece.** For family-style service, let the shared food become the centre of the table, framed by a low garland and candles. Keep the styling minimal so dishes have room.
*Suits:* long tables with share plates.

### Non floral centrepieces

**22. Sculptural ceramics.** A group of handmade vases, bowls or vessels in tonal glazes, empty or holding a single stem. It suits couples who collect, and the pieces go home with you afterwards.
*Suits:* modern venues and round tables.

**23. Books and personal objects.** Stacks of vintage books, framed photographs or objects that tell your story, finished with a candle. Keep it spare so it feels personal rather than staged.
*Suits:* smaller weddings and libraries, galleries or private homes.

**24. Lanterns.** Metal or glass lanterns of varied heights, lit from within. They keep the candlelight enclosed, and suit open-air settings.
*Suits:* outdoor tables and garden receptions.

**25. Glass and mirror.** Mirrored plinths or clusters of clear and tinted glassware that multiply the candlelight. The look is crisp, reflective and quietly glamorous.
*Suits:* evening receptions in ballrooms and modern rooms.

**26. A cake as the centrepiece.** Small cakes on stands as each table's focal point, served later as dessert. Read our guide to [a cake that doubles as the centrepiece](/trending-centerpiece-cake), and for more ways to replace flowers, our list of [centrepiece alternatives](/centerpiece-alternatives-wedding-reception-2).
*Suits:* round tables and smaller receptions.

### Destination and local styling

<!-- IMAGE SLOT non-floral-destination: Mid shot of a centrepiece built from non-floral elements that echo the destination, for example lemons and olive branches on a Mediterranean terrace, or local ceramics and candles. Daylight or golden hour, setting visible behind the table. No people. Must be a real, licensed wedding or venue image with venue and town verified, never AI, since it depicts a real destination. -->

**27. Olive branches and lemons.** Loose olive branches laid down the table with lemons and low candles. It is relaxed, silvery and sunlit, and needs very few flowers.
*Suits:* long tables on terraces and in courtyards.

**28. Shells and driftwood.** Pale shells, smooth stones and sun-bleached wood with sand-toned candles. Keep it restrained so it reads as coastal, not themed.
*Suits:* beachside and coastal receptions.

**29. Waratah statement.** For an Australian wedding, the sculptural red waratah makes a bold, native centrepiece with banksia and gum leaves. [Waratah flowering is concentrated in spring](https://agrifutures.com.au/related-projects/farm-based-methods-for-control-of-flowering-in-waratahs/), so plan a spring date if your heart is set on it.
*Suits:* round tables and long tables in bush, vineyard or coastal settings.

**30. Local vessels.** Build each centrepiece around vessels chosen at your destination, such as painted ceramics, copper or woven baskets, filled with the flowers and foliage your florist recommends. Ask to see examples before you commit.
*Suits:* any table where you want the setting to carry into the design.

## Practical details

**Open flame, candles and hurricanes.** Rules vary by venue. Ask what is permitted, whether candles must sit in glass, and who extinguishes them at the end of the night. General candle safety guidance is to extinguish all candles when leaving a room.

**Scent near food.** Heavily scented blooms such as lilies, tuberose and some jasmine can compete with the meal. Keep strong fragrance for the ceremony or the entrance, and choose softer scents for the tables.

**Set-up and pack-down.** Confirm who sets the tables, at what time, and who collects everything at the end. Ask whether vessels, stands and candle holders are hired and must be returned, and what happens to the flowers afterwards.

**Reusing ceremony florals.** Aisle arrangements and urns can move to the reception, but someone has to carry them during the drinks hour. Agree who moves them, and design the ceremony pieces to fit the tables.

## Centrepieces for a destination wedding

When you cannot visit the florist's studio, the right questions do the work. Ask these of the venue and a local florist, and keep the answers in writing.

**Questions for the venue**

- What vessels, stands and candle holders do you hold, and are they included?
- What must we hire in, and who arranges delivery?
- Do you allow open flame, and do candles need to sit in glass?
- Are there rules on fixing anything to ceilings, beams or walls?
- When can the florist access the room to set up, and when must everything be cleared?
- Who is our contact on the day if something needs changing?

**Questions for a local florist**

- Can you work from our mood board and send a sample photo before the day?
- Which flowers and foliage will you source locally, and what would need to be brought in?
- How will flowers be kept fresh between delivery and dinner, especially outdoors?
- What time will you deliver and set up, and will someone stay to adjust pieces?
- Who handles pack-down, and do you take back hired items?
- Can you put the quote, what is included and the timings in writing?
- What deposit do you need, and when is the balance due?

## Frequently asked questions

### How tall should wedding centrepieces be?

Either low enough for guests to talk over or lifted well above seated eye level on a slim stand. Avoid the middle height that blocks faces across the table. Our [table decor guide](/style-advice/wedding-table-decor) covers heights in detail.

### Do long tables need one centrepiece or several?

Several, or one continuous run. A garland, repeating low vessels or clusters of candles carry the length of a long table; a single arrangement in the middle leaves the ends bare.

### Can we reuse our ceremony flowers as centrepieces?

Yes, if they are designed to fit the tables and someone is assigned to move them during the drinks hour. Low urns, aisle pieces and bud vases transfer most easily.

### Can we use candles at our venue?

It depends on the venue, which sets its own rules, so ask before you design, including whether candles must sit in glass hurricanes or holders. Where open flame is restricted, battery-operated candles keep the glow.

### What makes wedding centrepieces expensive?

Stems are the expensive part of most arrangements. Fewer, larger focal blooms with more foliage, candles to fill length and ceremony flowers moved to the reception all use them well. For what drives the bill, read [what drives the cost of wedding flowers](/style-advice/wedding-flowers-cost).

### What are good non floral centrepiece ideas?

Sculptural ceramics, lanterns, mirrored plinths, fruit, books and personal objects all work. Choose one material and repeat it across the room so the tables feel designed rather than collected.
