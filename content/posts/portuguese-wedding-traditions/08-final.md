Ask a Portuguese grandmother about her wedding and she will talk about her padrinhos, the linen she carried into married life and the gold at her throat before she mentions the dress. Portuguese wedding traditions are quieter and more local than most lists suggest, and several customs repeated online have no Portuguese source at all.

This guide follows a Portuguese wedding in order, from the asking to the feast. It separates what dictionaries, UNESCO and official registries record from what is only often told, then covers what Australians need to marry legally in Portugal. It is the Portuguese chapter of [our guide to wedding traditions around the world](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions).

## Key takeaways

- The padrinhos are, by dictionary definition, wedding witnesses, so choosing them is a real role at the ceremony rather than an honorary title.
- The Minho bridal costume and Viana gold filigree belong to the north, so customs vary by region and family rather than applying across Portugal.
- The main customs are the asking and noivado, the padrinhos, the enxoval, the Minho bride and Viana gold, the copo-d'água feast, and music from folk dances to Fado.
- Australians marrying legally in Portugal need a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage, which the embassy says takes at least four weeks, before the registry's process of at least one month.

## What makes a Portuguese wedding different

A Portuguese wedding is built around family roles and the long meal that follows the ceremony, rather than staged moments. The vocabulary tells you a good deal: the asking, the engagement, the witnesses, the trousseau and the feast each have their own Portuguese word.

Customs vary by region, family and faith. The north, and the Minho in particular, holds on to costume and gold traditions you are unlikely to see at a wedding in Lisbon or the south. A Catholic family and a family planning a civil ceremony will also mark the day differently.

If you plan to marry in Portugal itself, in Lisbon, Sintra, the Douro or the Algarve, our notes on [planning a wedding in Portugal](/destinations/europe/portugal) cover the regions in more depth.

## Before the wedding

### The asking and the engagement

The formal asking is the *pedido de casamento*, the term Priberam's Portuguese dictionary gives for the act of asking for a marriage. <!-- fact_04 --> Traditionally, the groom or his family asks the bride's family for their blessing (often told).

The engagement itself is the *noivado*, which the dictionary defines as the state of being committed to marry, and the commitment itself. <!-- fact_03 --> During it, the couple are *noivo* and *noiva*, the same words used for groom and bride on the day.

### Choosing the padrinhos

In Portuguese, a *padrinho* is defined as the person who serves as a witness at a wedding. <!-- fact_01 --> When a family talks about choosing the padrinhos, they mean the people who will stand beside the couple and witness the marriage, not a decorative bridal party.

If you marry in a civil ceremony in Portugal, the registry rules on witnesses are more relaxed than the tradition suggests. Two to four witnesses may take part, but they are not compulsory. <!-- fact_113 --> Families can still choose padrinhos for meaning when the law does not require them.

### The enxoval

The *enxoval* is the set of clothes and linens with which a bride enters married life; the same word is used for a newborn's first clothes. <!-- fact_02 -->

A family piece still has a place today. An embroidered runner on the welcome table, or a grandmother's linen used under the cake, carries the idea without asking anyone to sew.

### The Minho bride and Viana gold filigree

In the Viana do Castelo area of the Minho, the traditional bridal costume is often described as black, worn with embroidery and layered gold. It is a regional dress, not a national one.

<!-- IMAGE SLOT minho_dress: Vertical, Viana do Castelo (festival such as the Romaria d'Agonia or museum display at Museu do Traje), black bridal costume with embroidery and gold, daylight; museum object or consented wearer; name and description must match the sourced text. -->

The gold matters as much as the dress. Filigree (*filigrana*) is ornamental work made from very fine wires and tiny metal beads soldered into a design, usually in gold or silver. Portuguese Wikipedia describes it as often worn in northern Portugal with the traditional bridal costume and with women's costume in Minho folk groups. <!-- fact_05 -->

Póvoa de Lanhoso, in the Minho, is described as Portugal's "land of gold" and its capital of filigree, and the Museu do Ouro de Travassos is dedicated to that goldsmithing tradition. <!-- fact_06 -->

The best-known piece is the *Coração de Viana*, the Viana heart. Portuguese Wikipedia describes it as beginning as a devotional symbol of the Sacred Heart of Jesus rather than a love token, and the story often told links it to Queen Maria I. <!-- fact_07 --> The same article describes filigree motifs as drawing mainly on nature, religion and love. <!-- fact_07 -->

For brides wearing a family heirloom, Portuguese Wikipedia describes traditional Portuguese gold as 19.2 carat. <!-- fact_08 --> Plan your neckline and any other jewellery around the piece rather than the other way round.

## The wedding day and the feast

### The walk to church and the ceremony

In villages, the bride walked to the church with her family and guests (often told).

The ceremony itself may be Catholic or civil. Portugal's marriage process covers a civil marriage, a Catholic marriage, or a civil marriage in religious form, and the couple chooses the type when the process begins. <!-- fact_108 --> We cover what that means for Australians in the legal section below.

### Rice and petals as you leave

Rice or petals are thrown as the couple leave the church or registry, for plenty and a fruitful marriage (often told).

### The copo-d'água feast

The Portuguese name for the reception is the *copo-d'água*, literally "glass of water". Priberam defines it as a meal of sweets and liqueurs offered to visitors or guests at a solemn occasion. <!-- fact_102 -->

### Caldo verde late in the night

Caldo verde, the potato and kale soup of northern Portugal, appears late in the evening for the guests still dancing (often told).

### The groom's tie and the bride's shoe

Two money customs (often told): the groom's tie is cut into small pieces and offered to guests for a contribution to the couple.

In the other, the bride's shoe is passed among guests, who place money in it. We found no Portuguese record of either, so ask your family before you plan them.

### Sugared almonds for guests

Sugared almonds, *amêndoas*, are a small favour for guests, a wish for sweetness in the marriage (often told).

### Music, from folk dances to Fado and Cante Alentejano

Music at a Portuguese wedding is a matter of family taste and region. Two Portuguese musical traditions are recognised by UNESCO, and couples may choose either for a moment in the day.

UNESCO inscribed [Fado, urban popular song of Portugal](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/fado-urban-popular-song-of-portugal-00563), on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2011. <!-- fact_100 --> UNESCO describes it as music and poetry widely practised in Lisbon, usually a solo singer with a wire-strung acoustic guitar and the Portuguese guitarra. <!-- fact_100 --> It is not a wedding tradition, but a single song during dinner can mark the day.

Cante Alentejano, polyphonic singing from the Alentejo in southern Portugal, was inscribed in 2014. <!-- fact_101 --> UNESCO describes it as two-part singing by amateur choral groups without instruments, and links it to social gatherings in both public and private spaces across Alentejo communities. <!-- fact_101 -->

## Customs that are often told but hard to trace

Many lists of Portuguese customs mix local practice with customs from elsewhere, and call them ancient without a source. We have left out the arras coins, the older unmarried sibling dancing barefoot and the cake under the pillow, because we could not trace them to Portugal, and tagged the rest of this guide's customs as often told.

The best source for your wedding is your own family: ask which customs they kept, and which they would rather leave behind.

## Bringing Portuguese wedding traditions home to Australia

For Portuguese-Australian families, the customs that travel best are the ones built on people rather than place. Choosing padrinhos, using the words *noivo* and *noiva* in a speech, and showing a piece of family linen all work at a wedding in Sydney or Melbourne.

Gold is another easy bridge: a Coração de Viana pendant handed down through the family sits with any dress.

The feast is harder to translate. If your family expects a late, unhurried copo-d'água, ask about finish times early when you compare venues.

## Marrying legally in Portugal as an Australian

This section is a summary of official sources as at November 2026, not legal advice. Smartraveller notes that Portuguese government pages in English are often out of date or inaccurate, so confirm current requirements directly. <!-- fact_118 -->

### Civil registration at the Conservatória do Registo Civil

Every marriage in Portugal, whether civil, Catholic or civil in religious form, begins with a declaration of the intention to marry at a civil registry office, the *conservatória do registo civil*. <!-- fact_108 --> The [IRN (Instituto dos Registos e do Notariado)](https://irn.justica.gov.pt/) asks couples to start the process at least one month before the chosen date. <!-- fact_108 -->

At the declaration, you state the type of marriage, the place, the date and time, and the property regime. <!-- fact_108 --> If you choose no property regime, the default is *comunhão de adquiridos*, community of acquired property. <!-- fact_112 --> Discuss it with a lawyer before you sign.

Online applications are only open to Portuguese citizens (and some Brazilian nationals) with Portuguese digital ID, so most Australians start in person or through a representative with a special power of attorney. <!-- fact_109 --> Between the start of the process and the wedding, anyone may raise an impediment at the registry office. <!-- fact_109 -->

For foreign couples, IRN asks for identification documents, a birth certificate issued under the law of your country, and a certificate of capacity to marry issued less than six months before. <!-- fact_111 --> If you do not speak Portuguese and the registrar does not speak your language, an interpreter is appointed. <!-- fact_111 -->

Date, time and place of a civil wedding are agreed with the registrar. A civil wedding can take place at the registry office in normal hours; marrying elsewhere, or on a weekend or public holiday, must be agreed in advance. <!-- fact_113 --> Lisbon and Albufeira, in the Algarve, offer public municipal spaces for weddings. <!-- fact_113 -->

A fee applies, with higher fees for weddings outside the registry office or on weekends and public holidays. <!-- fact_114 -->

### Documents and the Australian Embassy in Lisbon

The [Australian Embassy in Lisbon](https://portugal.embassy.gov.au/) tells Australians to contact the civil registry office in the area where they plan to marry for its requirements, to recheck them because local authorities change them, and to send enquiries in Portuguese where possible. <!-- fact_103 -->

The embassy lists an original birth certificate issued within six months with a Portuguese translation, an Australian passport, and a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI). <!-- fact_104 --> It also says processing takes a minimum of four weeks. <!-- fact_105 -->

The embassy issues the CNI. You complete an application form witnessed by a consular officer or a local notary, and provide your passport, your partner's identification, and divorce papers or a death certificate if either of you was married before. <!-- fact_106 --> Lodging in person is by appointment, and a fee applies. <!-- fact_106 -->

If original divorce papers go to the Portuguese authorities, they may need an apostille, which the embassy can provide by appointment. <!-- fact_107 --> The embassy also suggests an English-speaking lawyer for Australians marrying another Australian or a non-resident. <!-- fact_107 --> DFAT's guidance is to confirm with the local authority whether your CNI needs an apostille. <!-- fact_115 -->

Two points differ between sources, so check them with the registry office and the embassy:

- **The window to marry.** The embassy says the marriage must take place within three months of approval; IRN and the Justice portal say six months. <!-- fact_105 --> <!-- fact_109 --> <!-- fact_112 -->
- **Translations.** IRN says documents in English, French or Spanish need no certified translation, while the embassy lists a Portuguese translation of the birth certificate. <!-- fact_104 --> <!-- fact_111 -->

Same-sex civil marriage has been legal in Portugal since 2010, under Law 9/2010. <!-- fact_116 -->

### Registering your marriage back home

You cannot register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised if it was valid in the country where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_117 --> A foreign marriage certificate is usually accepted as proof of marriage, though it may not work as proof of a name change, so check with your state or territory registry. <!-- fact_117 -->

## Travel and safety notes for guests

As at November 2026, [Smartraveller advice for Portugal](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/portugal) is to "Exercise normal safety precautions", with no higher-level areas, on a page updated 16 October 2026. <!-- fact_119 --> Check it again before you book and before guests travel.

- **Summer heat.** High temperatures and bushfires regularly affect mainland Portugal in summer, including coastal and tourist areas. <!-- fact_119 --> Plan shade and a fallback for an outdoor ceremony.
- **Algarve cliffs.** Falling rocks and cliff erosion affect parts of the Algarve. <!-- fact_119 --> Keep clifftop portraits well back from the edge.
- **Arrival delays.** Biometric registration on arrival under the EU Entry/Exit System can slow the border, especially at Lisbon airport. <!-- fact_119 --> Build time into guest transfers.
- **Older or less mobile guests.** Ask the venue about steps, access and shade, and ask guests to check their travel and medical insurance covers the trip.
- **Dual nationals.** Portugal recognises dual nationality, and Portuguese-Australian guests should check which passport to use. <!-- fact_119 -->

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the most important Portuguese wedding traditions?

The customs with the clearest roots are choosing the padrinhos as witnesses, the enxoval of linen and clothes for married life, and the copo-d'água feast after the ceremony. <!-- fact_01 --> <!-- fact_02 --> <!-- fact_102 --> In the Minho, gold filigree such as the Coração de Viana is described as often worn with the traditional bridal costume. <!-- fact_05 --> Customs vary by region and family.

### Who are the padrinhos at a Portuguese wedding?

The padrinhos are the wedding witnesses; Priberam defines a padrinho as the person who witnesses a marriage. <!-- fact_01 --> At a civil wedding in Portugal, two to four witnesses may take part but are not compulsory. <!-- fact_113 -->

### What food is served at a Portuguese wedding?

The reception is called the copo-d'água, and dishes vary widely by region and family. Caldo verde, a potato and kale soup from northern Portugal, appears late in the night (often told).

### What do guests give as a gift at a Portuguese wedding?

There is no single documented rule. Some families keep money customs such as passing the bride's shoe or cutting the groom's tie, though both are often told rather than recorded. Ask the couple's family what they expect.

### Can Australians get legally married in Portugal?

Yes. You start the process at a Portuguese civil registry office with identification, a birth certificate and a certificate of capacity to marry, which for Australians is the CNI issued by the Australian Embassy in Lisbon. <!-- fact_111 --> <!-- fact_106 --> Allow at least four weeks for the CNI from the embassy, then start the registry process at least one month before your date. <!-- fact_105 --> <!-- fact_108 -->

### Is Portugal a good place for a destination wedding?

Lisbon and Albufeira, in the Algarve, both offer public municipal spaces for civil weddings. <!-- fact_113 --> Smartraveller's overall level for Portugal is "Exercise normal safety precautions" as at November 2026, and it notes high summer temperatures and bushfires on the mainland. <!-- fact_119 --> Check the advice again before you book.
