Most lists of Greek wedding traditions mix two different things. One is the Orthodox service, a sacrament with its own order and its own meaning. The other is the family folklore around it: the bed made by friends, names on a shoe, sugared almonds in odd numbers, a circle dance that pulls in every generation.

This guide takes 12 customs in the order a couple meets them, from the engagement to the dancing at the reception. It says plainly which belong to the Church and which are simply often told. It ends with what Australians need to know about marrying legally in Greece, and about holding the same service in a parish at home.

## Key takeaways

- The Orthodox wedding service has no vows, and the crowning of the couple, not a kiss, is the moment most guests watch for.
- Customs such as the krevati, names on the shoe and koufeta are family folklore that varies by region, so ask your own family how they do it.
- At the reception, the kalamatianos, the money dance and a chorus of "na zisete" often matter more to families than any formal programme.
- To marry legally in Greece, check with the Greek municipality and the Australian Embassy in Athens about a Certificate of No Impediment, an apostille and any fees, as at November 2026.

## What makes a Greek wedding different?

The Orthodox Church describes marriage as a sacrament with no vows or oaths, and not as a legal contract between two people. <!-- fact_01 --> That one detail changes the shape of the day. There is no moment where the couple turns to face each other and recite promises.

Instead, the couple is led into the church in procession, prayed over and blessed, hears readings from Scripture, and is crowned. <!-- fact_02 --> Everything else, from the bed made by friends to the plates at the reception, sits outside the service and changes from family to family and island to island.

So read the list below in two layers. The Church sets the service. Families and regions set the customs around it.

## Before the church: engagement, family and the bed

These customs are often told rather than written down, and each family keeps its own version.

### 1. Proxenio and the engagement

Proxenio is the name often given to the old practice of a matchmade introduction, arranged by family or a trusted go-between. Few couples meet that way now.

The engagement is often marked with a blessing of the rings and a gathering of both families. In many families the rings are worn on the right hand, it is often said. We cover the engagement party, the gifts and the family meeting in more depth in our guide to [Greek engagement traditions](/5-timeless-greek-engagement-traditions).

### 2. The koumbaros and koumbara

The koumbaros (or koumbara, if a woman) is usually called the couple's sponsor. In plain terms, this is the person who stands beside you at the altar and takes part in the rite itself. In the service as it is usually described, the koumbaros exchanges the rings and the stefana over the couple's heads.

The tie often lasts well beyond the day. Many families say the koumbaros is later asked to baptise the couple's first child, though this is a custom, not a rule.

Check with your priest about who can stand as koumbaros before you ask anyone.

### 3. Making the marriage bed (krevati)

The krevati is one of the warmest customs in the lead-up. As it is often told, family and friends gather a few days before the wedding to make the couple's new bed with fresh linen, then scatter it with flowers, sugared almonds, rice and money.

A baby or small child is often rolled across the bed for luck. Families tell different stories about what this means, and the gathering itself, with songs and teasing, is usually the point.

### 4. Names on the bride's shoe

On the morning of the wedding, the bride's unmarried friends often write their names on the sole of her shoe. The story goes that the names worn away by the end of the night belong to the friends who will marry next.

### 5. Dressing the bride and the groom

In many families the bride is dressed at home with her mother, sisters and closest friends in the room, sometimes with songs. The groom's side often has its own version, with friends helping him shave and dress before he leaves for the church.

## Inside the Greek Orthodox wedding service

The Orthodox Church explains its own rite, and the steps below follow the way the Greek service is usually described. Ask your parish priest, as the order and details can vary.

One background point helps. The Orthodox Church in America notes that the marriage service was patterned on baptism and chrismation, and that for centuries there was no separate marriage ritual at all. <!-- fact_05 --> The sacrament is for baptised members of the Church, and an Orthodox Christian may marry a baptised Christian of another tradition in the Church. <!-- fact_04 -->

If one of you is not Orthodox, raise it with the priest first. It will shape the choice of church and the timeline.

### 6. The betrothal and the rings

The service usually opens with the betrothal. The priest usually blesses the rings, and they are then exchanged three times, most often by the koumbaros.

There are no spoken vows at this point or any other. <!-- fact_01 --> If you want personal words, the reception speeches are the natural place for them.

### 7. The candles and the joining of hands

The couple is usually given tall lit candles to hold through the service. The priest then usually joins the couple's right hands, and they stay joined for much of what follows.

### 8. The crowning with stefana

The crowning is usually described as the centre of the service. The stefana are two crowns, usually joined by a ribbon, and the koumbaros usually exchanges them over the couple's heads three times.

The Church explains the crowns as crowns of God's glory: the couple is crowned to be His children and witnesses in this world and heirs of His Kingdom. <!-- fact_03 --> The ribbon is usually described as a sign of the two lives joined.

Tell your photographer and your guests that this, not the rings or a kiss, is the moment to watch.

### 9. The Gospel and the common cup

Scripture readings usually follow, as in the Orthodox rite. <!-- fact_02 --> The couple then usually shares a common cup of wine, sipping from it three times. It is commonly described as a sign of the life they will now share.

### 10. The Dance of Isaiah

The priest then usually leads the crowned couple three times around the ceremony table, the koumbaros following with the ribbon. This short procession is known as the Dance of Isaiah, usually said to be named after a hymn sung as they walk.

Afterwards the priest usually removes the crowns and gives a closing blessing. Guests often throw rice or petals as the couple leaves, a custom many families keep.

There is one moment that is pure folklore. When the priest reads the line about the wife and husband, the bride is often said to step on the groom's foot, so that she has the last word in the marriage.

### How long is a Greek Orthodox wedding service?

The length is not fixed: it depends on the parish, the music and the size of the wedding party. Ask your priest before you set guest transport and the reception start.

## The celebration: koufeta, dancing and the money dance

The reception is where family customs take over, and no two Greek receptions run quite the same way.

### 11. Koufeta and bomboniere

Koufeta are white sugared almonds, and they appear everywhere: on the ceremony tray, in tulle bundles at each place setting, in the bomboniere guests take home.

They are usually given in odd numbers. The reason often told is that an odd number cannot be divided, like the couple. Another story says an unmarried guest who sleeps with koufeta under the pillow will dream of a future spouse.

### 12. The kalamatianos, the money dance and "Na zisete"

The dancing is the heart of most Greek receptions. The kalamatianos and the syrtos are circle dances, and guests link hands or shoulders while one person leads, often waving a handkerchief.

During one of the dances, guests often pin money to the couple's clothes or hand it to them as they pass. It is a gesture of good wishes from family, and many couples now ask a koumbaros or cousin to gather it discreetly.

Throughout the night you will hear "na zisete", a wish for a long life together, called out to the couple.

And then there is ftou ftou. Older relatives may make a light spitting sound towards the bride or the couple. It is often told as a way of keeping off the evil eye, or mati, after praising someone. No one actually spits, and it is meant kindly.

### Plate smashing today

Plate smashing is the custom outsiders most associate with a Greek celebration, but it is not part of the wedding service, and many families never do it at a wedding. Where it does happen, it is usually a burst of high spirits during the dancing. Some couples choose to have petals thrown instead, and your venue will have its own view, so ask before the night.

Other cultures break crockery for luck too. Our guide to [German wedding traditions, from Polterabend to the log](/expert-advice/german-wedding-traditions) explains a very different version of the same impulse.

## What do guests wear and give at a Greek wedding?

Dress for a church first and a party second. Smartraveller advises dressing modestly in churches and religious buildings in Greece. <!-- fact_103 --> For a summer service, that usually means covered shoulders, so a light wrap or jacket is worth packing even when the reception is outdoors.

Bridal dress traditions are mostly about family rather than rules. Many brides wear white, and some add a family piece, a relative's veil or jewellery, for the service.

Gifts follow family custom. Many Greek families give money in a card, and others choose from a registry. If unsure, ask someone close to the couple. On who pays for what, families differ widely, and it is a conversation for the two families, not a rule of the culture.

## Greek wedding traditions from ancient Greece

Ancient Greek marriage customs are a separate subject from the Orthodox service, and they are often blurred together online. The Orthodox Church in America notes that for centuries there was no separate marriage ritual, and that the rite which grew up was patterned on baptism and chrismation. <!-- fact_05 --> That makes it hard to draw a straight line from ancient ceremony to the modern church service, and this guide keeps to the customs couples meet today.

## Marrying legally in Greece as an Australian

This section reflects official advice as at November 2026 and is not legal advice. Rules change, so confirm each step yourself.

Start with the basics. Smartraveller's advice level for Greece is "Exercise normal safety precautions", with no higher-level areas listed, last updated on 30 September 2026. <!-- fact_100 --> Smartraveller notes summer wildfires (June to September) and heatwaves, worth weighing if you are set on a midsummer date. <!-- fact_105 --> With older or less mobile guests, ask about shade, seating and the walk to the church.

Greek-Australian couples have one extra check. Smartraveller notes that someone of Greek descent who was born elsewhere may hold Greek nationality, with obligations such as military service, and advises getting advice from a Greek embassy or consulate before travelling. <!-- fact_104 -->

**Proving you are free to marry.** Some countries ask for proof that you are free to marry, most often a Certificate of No Impediment (CNI) and certificates from a registry of births, deaths and marriages. <!-- fact_112 --> Smartraveller suggests asking the local authorities whether you need a CNI, whether you can get one in Australia, and if it needs authentication or apostille, as some authorities only accept CNIs issued in their own country. <!-- fact_113 -->

**The Australian Embassy in Athens.** The embassy witnesses application forms for, and issues, Certificates of No Impediment to Marriage, and witnesses notices of intended marriage. <!-- fact_108 --> All notarial services are by appointment, though CNI documents can be submitted by post, and CNIs are available for collection within two working days. <!-- fact_109 --> A fee applies, and honorary consuls cannot provide notarial services. <!-- fact_110 --> The embassy does not give legal advice, so you need to know which service you are asking for. <!-- fact_111 --> You will find it at the [Australian Embassy in Greece](https://greece.embassy.gov.au/athn/Notarial.html), 5 Hatziyianni Mexi Street, Athens. <!-- fact_102 -->

**Civil or church ceremony.** Ask the municipality (dimos) and the embassy whether a civil or a religious route suits you. Which documents, translations and notices apply, and whether any rules differ for same-sex couples, is for the Greek authorities. Check with the municipality and the embassy, including any fees, before you set a date. For an Orthodox church wedding, the parish priest and the diocese will have their own requirements, so speak to the priest first.

**Back home in Australia.** An overseas marriage cannot be registered in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law. <!-- fact_114 --> A foreign marriage certificate is usually accepted as proof of marriage but may not be accepted as proof of a name change, so check with your state registry. <!-- fact_115 -->

For current travel advice, read [Smartraveller advice for Greece](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/greece) and its page on [getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas).

## Holding a Greek Orthodox wedding in Australia

Many Greek-Australian couples hold the full service at home, in a Greek Orthodox parish in their own city, then travel later or not at all. The service follows the same rite, and the customs travel with the family.

The first call is to the parish priest. Ask your parish priest about available dates, the documents the parish needs, whether a non-Orthodox partner or koumbaros raises any questions, and any periods in the church year when weddings are not held. Do this before you book a reception venue.

The [Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of Australia on marriage](https://greekorthodox.org.au/services-and-sacraments/marriage/) is a sensible place to begin reading before that first conversation.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the most common Greek wedding traditions?

Inside the church, the crowning with stefana, the common cup and the Dance of Isaiah. Around it, the krevati, names on the bride's shoe, koufeta for guests, and circle dances such as the kalamatianos at the reception.

### Why do Greeks pretend to spit at weddings?

The light "ftou ftou" sound is often told as a way of warding off the evil eye after praising someone. It is a folk custom, not a church one, and no one actually spits.

### What do the stefana crowns mean?

The Church explains them as crowns of God's glory, crowning the couple as His children and witnesses in this world and heirs of His Kingdom. <!-- fact_03 --> The ribbon that joins them is usually described as a sign of the two lives joined.

### Why are koufeta given in odd numbers?

The reason often told is that an odd number cannot be divided, like the couple.

### Why does the bride step on the groom's foot?

It is folklore. As the story goes, whoever steps first has the last word in the marriage.

### What should I wear to a Greek wedding as a guest?

Dress modestly for the church, with shoulders covered, as Smartraveller advises for churches in Greece. <!-- fact_103 --> Then dress for a long party with plenty of dancing.

### What do you say to the couple at a Greek wedding?

"Na zisete", a wish for a long life together.

### What is a good gift for a Greek wedding?

Many families give money in a card, and others use a registry. Ask someone close to the couple what is usual in their family.
