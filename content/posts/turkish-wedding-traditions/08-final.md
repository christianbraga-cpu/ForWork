A Turkish wedding is rarely one day. It begins with a formal visit between two families, can pass through a promise, an engagement and a henna night, and only then arrives at the music, the procession and the marriage itself. Most guides to Turkish wedding traditions list these customs in no particular order and repeat folklore as fact.

This one follows the wedding in order and marks which details are sourced and which are simply often told. It is the Turkish chapter of [our guide to wedding traditions around the world](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions), with a final section for Australians who want to marry legally in Türkiye.

## Key takeaways

- A traditional Turkish wedding moves through kız isteme, söz and nişan, the çeyiz and the kına gecesi before the wedding day itself.
- The kına gecesi is usually held the night before the wedding, or a day or two before, not weeks ahead.
- Customs differ from region to region and family to family, so ask both families what they expect before you plan the programme.
- For Australians, the legal marriage goes through a municipal marriage office in Türkiye, and the paperwork, including a certified certificate of eligibility to marry, should start well before you fly.

## How a Turkish wedding unfolds, from the asking to the last dance

GoTürkiye, the country's official tourism portal, says almost every region of Türkiye has its own wedding traditions, and that traditional weddings, especially in rural areas, carry a long list of customs. <!-- fact_04 --> Read what follows as a common shape, not a rulebook.

In broad order, the steps run like this:

1. **Kız isteme**, the formal asking for the bride's hand.
2. **Söz and nişan**, the families' agreement and the engagement.
3. **Çeyiz**, the trousseau prepared for the new home, often laid out for relatives to see.
4. **Kına gecesi**, the henna night.
5. **The wedding day**: the flag, the procession to collect the bride with davul and zurna, the marriage, the gold and the dancing.

GoTürkiye names May, August and September as the most popular months for weddings. <!-- fact_04 --> If you are flying guests in from Australia, factor those months into your dates early. If older or less mobile guests are coming, ask each venue about shade, step-free access and transfers from the hotel before you choose a date.

## Before the wedding day

### Kız isteme: asking for the bride's hand

Kız isteme is the visit at which the groom's family formally asks the bride's family for her hand. A Ministry of Culture and Tourism folklore study of wedding customs in Hatay, in Türkiye's south, describes how it works there: the groom's side first sends word through an intermediary, then visits with a respected person. <!-- fact_13 -->

In the same study, the bride's family often does not say yes at the first visit, and the groom's side may return two or three times while the bride's family makes its own enquiries. In one village it records, if the answer is yes, a ring is put on straight away. <!-- fact_13 -->

That is one region's example, but the lesson travels: leave room between the first visit and any engagement date.

### The coffee at the asking, and the salt that is often told

Coffee is part of the occasion. UNESCO inscribed Turkish coffee culture and tradition on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013, nominated by Türkiye. <!-- fact_01 --> It describes Turkish coffee as a symbol of hospitality, friendship, refinement and entertainment, with an important role on social occasions such as engagement ceremonies and holidays. <!-- fact_02 -->

UNESCO's description of the ritual is precise: freshly roasted beans ground to a fine powder, brewed slowly in a pot with cold water and sugar until it foams, then served in small cups with a glass of water. <!-- fact_03 --> The grounds left in the cup are often used to tell fortunes. <!-- fact_03 --> More is in [UNESCO's listing of Turkish coffee culture](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/turkish-coffee-culture-and-tradition-00645).

Often told, though we found no cultural or academic source for it, is the story that the groom's cup is salted and he must drink it without complaint.

### Söz and nişan: the promise and the engagement

The Turkish Language Association (TDK) defines "söz kesmek" as to agree and make a firm decision, usually to marry. <!-- fact_19 --> The Hatay study places söz kesme straight after a successful kız isteme: it is the families' first act of announcing their agreement, and everything to do with the wedding is discussed there. <!-- fact_11 -->

In that region, a small family gathering is called söz kesimi and a larger one nişan, the engagement. <!-- fact_12 --> Shopping for the bride-to-be's clothes and jewellery happens a few days beforehand. <!-- fact_12 --> In the past the engaged couple could not see each other; today they do, and some people the researcher spoke to said large engagements have given way to a family gathering with sweets and often just a ring. <!-- fact_12 -->

Often told is a moment where the couple's rings are joined by a ribbon that someone cuts. We found no reputable source for it, so ask your own families whether it is part of their nişan.

### Çeyiz: the trousseau

TDK defines çeyiz as all the goods prepared for a girl who is to become a bride; the word comes from the Arabic cihāz. <!-- fact_10 --> The Hatay study describes it as practical support for the new household and as folk culture through which handcrafts pass between generations. <!-- fact_14 --> A mother there begins preparing her daughter's çeyiz before she is grown, and the items it records include bed linen, towels and hand-made needle-lace (oya) headscarves. <!-- fact_14 -->

The çeyiz is also seen. TDK defines "çeyiz sermek" as spreading out the trousseau so everyone can see it. <!-- fact_16 --> In Hatay today, the study records, the çeyiz is carried in procession by a large crowd to the groom's home the day before the kına gecesi and displayed with the groom's belongings. <!-- fact_15 --> In one village it is decorated with ribbons and flowers, carried by the groom's friends and arranged by the bride's. <!-- fact_15 -->

If your families keep it, give it its own day in the programme.

### Kına gecesi: the henna night

TDK defines kına gecesi as the celebration held, usually on the night before the wedding at the bride's home, while henna is applied to the bride's fingers. <!-- fact_17 --> GoTürkiye says it has been a Turkish tradition for hundreds of years and is held a day or two before the wedding as a small gathering of family and friends, with folk songs, and henna applied to the bride's palm and the groom's little finger. <!-- fact_05 -->

The Hatay study describes it as one of the most widespread marriage customs in Türkiye, usually held among women at the bride's home. <!-- fact_18 --> Less commonly, in some regions the groom also has henna, at a gathering at his family's home, with henna sent on a tray from the bride's side and applied by someone present or by his best man (sağdıç). <!-- fact_18 -->

GoTürkiye says customs vary, but a common thread is making the bride cry, said to show her sorrow at leaving her family. <!-- fact_07 --> She sits on a chair while ballads are sung and unmarried girls circle her holding candles, and the stated purpose is for her to move from tears to laughter in her new life. <!-- fact_07 -->

Then comes the gold. The bride keeps her hand closed, and when the person applying the henna announces that she will not open it, the mother-in-law places a gold coin in her palm as a henna gift. <!-- fact_08 --> Henna itself, GoTürkiye notes, has long been associated with devotion and is believed to protect married couples from evil. <!-- fact_06 -->

The henna night is changing too. A 2020 folklore study by E. Alpyıldız of Hacettepe University's Department of Turkish Folklore notes that the kına gecesi is now also held in new urban forms that work partly like a hen's party (bekarlığa veda), with different dress, music, food and venues. <!-- fact_115 -->

### The bindallı

The bindallı is often told as the henna-night dress. TDK defines it as a long-sleeved, floor-length, open-fronted garment, mostly of purple velvet, embroidered in relief with gold or silver thread (sırma) in branch, leaf and flower motifs. <!-- fact_09 -->

## On the wedding day

### The wedding flag and the procession

In the folklore literature, bayrak asma, raising the flag, is the symbolic announcement that the wedding has begun: a flag goes up at the wedding house while davul and zurna play. <!-- fact_112 -->

Gelin alma (also called gelin göçü) is the collecting of the bride. Alpyıldız describes a party, the gelin alayı, setting out from the groom's house to take the bride from her father's house to the groom's family home, with dancing along the way. <!-- fact_110 --> When the party reaches the bride's home, the music slows to "gelin alma" or "gelin çıkarma" melodies, usually without words, marking her farewell to her family home. <!-- fact_111 -->

Gifts are part of the arrival. Rituals under names such as indirmelik, gifts to persuade the bride to step down from the horse or car at the groom's home, are seen in almost every region, and the drummers may playfully block the way for a tip. <!-- fact_114 -->

### Davul and zurna

Alpyıldız describes the pair as the sign that a wedding is taking place, their sound carrying over wide areas, and quotes the proverb "Düğünde davul zurna, hamamda kurna": davul and zurna at a wedding, the basin at a bathhouse. <!-- fact_113 --> In the gelin alma music, the davul keeps the rhythm and the zurna or clarinet carries the tune. <!-- fact_111 -->

That carrying sound is the point. For a hotel or villa arrival, check the venue's music rules first, and ask whether exclusive use is available if you want the henna night and the wedding under one roof.

### Takı töreni: pinning the gold

Often told in accounts of Turkish weddings is the takı töreni, a moment at the reception when guests give the couple gold. We found no cultural or academic source for its details, so we leave the specifics to your families, who will know how they do it.

### The halay

Often told is the halay, a folk dance in a line. We found no source on its form or meaning, so we leave the details to your families; when the line forms, your Australian guests can simply join it.

## How Turkish wedding traditions change by region and family

The Hatay study, a single region's record, shows how far one place can differ, with söz and nişan named by the size of the gathering rather than as separate steps. <!-- fact_12 -->

Faith shapes the day too, as does whether the families live in Türkiye or abroad. The honest approach is to ask both families early which customs they want to keep, which they would happily skip, and who should lead each one.

## Marrying legally in Türkiye as an Australian

This section is general information as at November 2026, not legal advice. Requirements change and can differ between offices, so confirm everything with the marriage office you book and the Australian Embassy in Ankara or Consulate-General in Istanbul.

### The civil marriage at the marriage office

In Türkiye, marriage applications are handled by a municipal marriage office (evlendirme dairesi). <!-- fact_103 --> Beyoğlu Municipality in Istanbul, for example, lists its own marriage office and the documents it asks foreign nationals for; other offices may differ. <!-- fact_103 -->

At Beyoğlu, the couple must attend the application together on a weekday, between 9am and 12pm or 1pm and 4.30pm, or appoint a proxy with a specific power of attorney that includes a photo. <!-- fact_106 --> Incomplete applications are not accepted. <!-- fact_106 --> No fee is listed on the page. <!-- fact_106 --> Check the ceremony format, whether it can be held at a venue, and any fees with the marriage office and the Australian Embassy.

### Documents Australians are asked for

Beyoğlu Municipality lists these for foreign nationals: three passport photos taken within the last six months; a health report showing no impediment to the marriage, from a family physician or a state hospital (or a private one approved by a family physician); a Marriage Eligibility Certificate or Certificate of Celibacy; and a notarised translation of the passport or photo ID. <!-- fact_104 -->

At Beyoğlu, the eligibility certificate must be certified. If it comes from your consulate in Türkiye, it is apostilled at a district governorate office, or certified by the Turkish Ministry of Foreign Affairs if the consulate is in Ankara; if you bring it from home, it must be certified by the Turkish Embassy or apostilled. <!-- fact_105 --> If it does not show your parents' names, a birth certificate is also required. <!-- fact_105 -->

Smartraveller explains that some countries ask you to prove you are free to marry, most often with a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI). <!-- fact_107 --> DFAT can issue a CNI in Australia, but some local authorities only accept ones issued in their country, so ask the Turkish embassy whether you need one, where to get it and whether it needs authentication or an apostille. <!-- fact_107 --> Smartraveller's [guide to marrying overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/before-you-go/activities/marriage) is the place to start.

Back home, Smartraveller says you cannot register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law; a foreign marriage certificate is usually accepted as proof, though it may not work for a name change. <!-- fact_108 --> It recommends talking to a lawyer to confirm validity. <!-- fact_108 --> More in our explainer on [whether you have to register an overseas marriage at home](/expert-advice/do-you-have-to-register-an-overseas-marriage-at-home).

Türkiye does not legally recognise same-sex marriages, civil unions or domestic partnerships. <!-- fact_102 --> If you are a same-sex couple, read Smartraveller's full Türkiye advice before planning a celebration there.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens at a Turkish henna night?

The kına gecesi is usually held at the bride's home on the night before the wedding, or a day or two before. <!-- fact_17 --> <!-- fact_05 --> Folk songs are sung, unmarried girls circle the bride with candles, and henna is applied to her palm, with the mother-in-law placing a gold coin in her closed hand as a henna gift. <!-- fact_05 --> <!-- fact_07 --> <!-- fact_08 -->

### What gifts do guests give at a Turkish wedding?

Gold features in the sourced customs: the mother-in-law's coin at the henna night is described as a henna gift. <!-- fact_08 --> On arrival at the groom's home, gifts to coax the bride down from the car or horse are seen in almost every region. <!-- fact_114 --> For the reception, follow the families' lead.

### Is Turkish coffee part of a Turkish wedding?

UNESCO, which listed Turkish coffee culture and tradition in 2013, says it plays an important role on social occasions such as engagement ceremonies. <!-- fact_01 --> <!-- fact_02 -->

### Is a Turkish civil marriage recognised in Australia?

Smartraveller says an overseas marriage is legally recognised in most cases if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law, though you cannot register it in Australia. <!-- fact_108 --> Talk to a lawyer to confirm your situation. <!-- fact_108 -->

### Do Turkish wedding customs differ by region?

Yes. GoTürkiye says almost every region of Türkiye has its own wedding traditions, with the longest lists in traditional, rural weddings. <!-- fact_04 --> Family and faith shape them too, so ask both families what they expect.

## Bringing Turkish traditions into your celebration

You do not need every custom to honour a Turkish family. Coffee served properly, a henna night the evening before, davul and zurna leading the way in: a few customs chosen with both families say more than a long list.
