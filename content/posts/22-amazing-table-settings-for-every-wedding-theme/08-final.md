A wedding table is built one place at a time. Before the florals arrive and the candles are lit, every guest sits down to the same small arrangement of plate, cutlery, glass, napkin and name card, and that arrangement tells them how the evening will run.

This guide to wedding table settings works through each piece in the order it is laid, then shows how seven themes change the same pieces. For a wedding abroad, it also sets out what to ask the venue before you fall for a look its cupboards cannot deliver.

## Key takeaways

- A place setting is built from the menu, with cutlery laid in order of use from the outside in and nothing set that will not be used.
- Forks sit to the left, knives and spoons to the right, glasses above the knives, and dessert cutlery either above the plate or brought out with dessert.
- A theme lives in the finish of each piece, not in adding more, and the seven looks below are classic and formal, pared back, garden and romantic, coastal, country estate, winter and candlelit, and playful and retro.
- For a destination wedding, ask the venue what tableware it holds and what must be hired in, then judge the whole setting at a mock-up.

## What goes on a wedding table setting

A wedding table setting is everything laid at a single guest's place: the charger or base plate, the plates that sit on it, cutlery for each course, the glasses, a napkin, and the stationery that tells a guest where to sit and what they will eat. It is the most personal part of the reception table, because it is the part each guest actually touches.

This page covers that setting, place by place. For centrepieces, linen, florals, candles and the room itself, see our guide to [wedding table decor, step by step](https://weddedwonderland.com/style-advice/wedding-table-decor). The two work together: decide the menu and the service style first, then the setting, then dress the table around it.

## The place setting, piece by piece

The conventions below are the ones set out in the Emily Post Institute's [table setting guides](https://emilypost.com/advice/table-setting-guides), an American etiquette reference whose rules are widely used at formal dinners. Treat them as a well-tested framework rather than a set of Australian rules, and adapt them to your menu.

### Charger and plates

The charger is the large decorative plate that anchors each place. It is optional: a charger plate may be used or left out entirely. <!-- fact_08 -->

The bread plate sits to the upper left of the setting, above the forks. The butter knife rests across it, with the blade facing inward toward the diner and the handle pointing right. <!-- fact_07 -->

When the table is cleared for dessert, the unused cutlery goes, along with the bread plate and butter knife, leaving the dessert cutlery in place if it was set from the start. <!-- fact_09 --> Finger bowls, if your menu calls for them, come out after a messy course rather than sitting at the place all meal. <!-- fact_09 -->

### Cutlery, from the outside in

Cutlery is laid in the order it will be used, working from the outside in. <!-- fact_01 --> Forks go to the left of the plate, and knives and spoons go to the right. <!-- fact_02 --> The one exception is an oyster fork, which sits to the right if it will be used. <!-- fact_02 --> Each knife is set with its blade facing inward, toward the plate. <!-- fact_04 -->

The most useful rule for a wedding is the simplest: only set the cutlery your guests will use. <!-- fact_03 --> This is why the setting cannot be finalised until the menu is. A late soup or sorbet course changes every place at every table.

Dessert cutlery has two options. It can arrive with dessert, or it can sit above the plate for the whole meal, with the fork's handle pointing left and the spoon above it, handle pointing right. <!-- fact_05 --> For the most traditional setting, the dessert spoon sits above the dessert fork. <!-- fact_05 --> Setting it from the start saves a cutlery run at dessert, which keeps service moving at a long table.

### Glassware, in the order it is used

The water glass sits above the knife, or slightly to its right. <!-- fact_04 --> Wine glasses, one or perhaps two at a less formal dinner, sit to the right of the water glass. <!-- fact_06 --> At a formal dinner with a different wine for each course, the glasses form a cascade flowing to the right of the setting. <!-- fact_06 -->

A coupe or flute for the toasts can join the line from the start or arrive with the speeches. If your drinks list runs to several wines, count the glasses before you choose a low, wide centrepiece, because both need the same strip of table.

### Napkin

The napkin can sit to the left of the forks or in the centre of the charger. <!-- fact_08 --> Both are correct, and whether the fork rests on the napkin is a matter of style rather than rule. <!-- fact_08 -->

That choice makes the napkin the easiest place to carry a theme: a flat fold in heavy linen for a formal dinner, a loose knot tied with a sprig of herbs for a garden lunch, a crisp rectangle with a ribbon for a winter evening.

### Name card and menu

The name card sits at the top of the setting, above the plate or on the napkin, where a guest finds it before they pull out their chair. A printed menu either rests on the charger, tucked under the napkin, or stands at the top of the setting beside the name card.

Name cards are the last piece to be finalised, because they follow the seating plan. Once your guest list settles, [make your wedding seating chart](https://weddedwonderland.com/expert-advice/wedding-seating-chart) first, then the cards follow from it.

## Match the setting to the service

The style of service changes the setting more than any theme. Decide it with your caterer before you choose a single plate.

**Plated dinner.** Each course arrives individually plated, so the full setting is laid in advance: charger, cutlery for every course, every glass. This is where the outside-in rule matters most, and where a printed menu earns its place, because guests can read the courses in the cutlery.

**Shared or family-style.** Platters are passed along the table, so each place needs less: a dinner plate, a fork and knife, perhaps a spoon, and the glasses. Leave room in the middle for the platters and keep centrepieces narrow.

**Long banquet tables.** A single long table, or several in a row, turns the room into one dinner party. Settings sit closer together and the eye reads the table as a whole, so repetition does the work. ### Classic and formal

- **Plate:** a metallic or cut-glass charger under fine white china with a narrow gold or platinum rim.
- **Cutlery:** polished silver or gold, a full set laid for every course.
- **Glass:** cut crystal, with a separate glass for each wine.
- **Napkin:** heavy white or ivory linen, folded flat and centred on the charger.
- **Stationery:** engraved or letterpress cards on thick stock, set above the plate.

### Pared back

- **Plate:** matte white or stoneware in one colour, with no charger.
- **Cutlery:** brushed steel or matte black, only what the menu needs.
- **Glass:** thin, plain stems, or a single tumbler for water.
- **Napkin:** natural linen in oatmeal, grey or black, folded in a simple rectangle.
- **Stationery:** a single card carrying name and menu, in one clean typeface.

### Garden and romantic

- **Plate:** soft pastel or floral-edged china, mixed rather than matched, over a woven or scalloped charger.
- **Cutlery:** antique silver or vintage patterns with ornate handles.
- **Glass:** etched or coloured glass in blush, sage or pale blue.
- **Napkin:** washed linen in a soft tone, tied loosely with ribbon or a sprig of rosemary.
- **Stationery:** handwritten names, deckled edges, or a pressed flower on the card.

### Coastal

- **Plate:** white or sand-toned ceramic with an irregular glaze, perhaps on a rattan charger.
- **Cutlery:** brushed gold or wooden-handled.
- **Glass:** clear or sea-glass tinted, sturdy enough for a breeze.
- **Napkin:** loose linen in white, sand or pale blue, knotted rather than folded.
- **Stationery:** a card weighted with a small stone or shell, so it stays put outdoors.

### Country estate

- **Plate:** earthenware or speckled stoneware in warm tones, over a timber charger.
- **Cutlery:** aged brass, bronze or horn-handled.
- **Glass:** amber or smoke glass, or heavier rustic stems.
- **Napkin:** rust, olive or ochre linen, folded with a sprig of wheat or eucalyptus.
- **Stationery:** kraft or cotton card in a warm tone.

### Winter and candlelit

- **Plate:** deep green, navy or charcoal china, or white with a gold rim over a dark charger.
- **Cutlery:** gold, which warms under candlelight.
- **Glass:** cut crystal or smoked glass to catch the flame.
- **Napkin:** velvet or heavy linen in burgundy, forest green or midnight blue.
- **Stationery:** dark card with gold foil. For a date near the festive season, a single sprig of pine at each place is enough.

### Playful and retro

- **Plate:** bold colour or graphic patterns, mixed across the table.
- **Cutlery:** coloured handles, mismatched on purpose.
- **Glass:** coupes, tinted glass or patterned tumblers.
- **Napkin:** bright solids or prints, folded into a simple shape.
- **Stationery:** strong type and colour, with a personal line for each guest.

## Table settings for a destination wedding

Abroad, the question is not only which look you love, but which look the venue can actually lay. Whatever tableware a venue holds decides how far a theme can go before pieces need to be hired in. Ask these questions early, and ask for the answers in writing.

- What tableware, glassware and linen do you hold, and for how many guests?
- Do you have chargers, and in which finishes?
- Which pieces would need to be hired in for our theme and guest count, and what would that add to the quote?
- Can we see the actual pieces, or photos of them laid on your tables?
- Who sets the tables, and who clears them between courses?
- Is there step-free access to the tables, with room for a guest using a walking frame or wheelchair?
- Are there limits on open flame, or on bringing in outside suppliers?
- Can we see a full mock-up of one place setting before the day?

## Planning your table settings

### What to check at the trial table

A mock-up is one table laid exactly as it will be on the day, and it is the step that settles the reception. At a wedding abroad, if you cannot be there, ask for clear photos or a video walk-through and go through each check below as you watch.

- **The full setting with the centrepiece in place.** Check that glasses, cutlery and florals do not crowd each other.
- **Sightlines.** Sit down. Can you see and speak to the guest opposite?
- **The light.** Look at the table at the hour the meal will be served, in daylight or by candlelight.
- **Stationery proofs.** Lay the real name cards and menus, and read them from a seated guest's view.
- **Spacing between places.** Ask the venue or stylist what each guest needs for your chosen setting and chairs, and confirm it against your guest count.

### When to decide each piece

Work in this order, because each choice depends on the one before it.

1. **Service style and menu.** These set the cutlery and glassware.
2. **Guest count and table layout.** These set how much of everything is needed and whether the venue holds enough.
3. **Plates, cutlery and glassware.** Start with what the venue holds, then hire in what the look and guest count need.
4. **Napkins and linen.** These carry most of the colour, so choose them against the plates.
5. **Stationery.** Menus follow the final menu, and name cards follow the seating plan, so these come last.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which side does the bread plate go on?

The bread plate sits to the upper left of the setting, above the forks. The butter knife rests across it with the blade facing inward toward the diner. <!-- fact_07 -->

### How many glasses does each guest need?

At a less formal dinner, a water glass plus one or perhaps two wine glasses to its right. <!-- fact_06 --> At a formal dinner with a different wine for each course, there is a glass for each, cascading to the right. <!-- fact_06 --> Add a coupe or flute if the toasts call for one.

### Where does the dessert cutlery go?

Either above the plate for the whole meal, with the fork's handle pointing left and the spoon above it, handle pointing right, or brought out with dessert. <!-- fact_05 -->

### Should the napkin go on the plate or to the left?

Either is correct: to the left of the forks or in the centre of the charger. <!-- fact_08 --> Choose by look. A napkin on the charger shows off a fold or a name card; one to the left leaves the plate clear.

### Do we need chargers at a wedding?

No. A charger plate may be used or left out. <!-- fact_08 --> It adds formality and colour, so it suits a plated dinner and a classic theme more than a shared table.

### Can a venue abroad supply all the tableware?

That depends on the venue. Ask what tableware, glassware and linen it holds, for how many guests, and what would need to be hired in, then get the answers in writing and see the pieces laid at a mock-up before you commit.
