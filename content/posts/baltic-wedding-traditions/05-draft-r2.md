Baltic wedding traditions are usually served up as one list, as if Tallinn, Riga and Vilnius shared a single rulebook. They don't. A Latvian bride is led through a midnight rite, a Lithuanian couple is met at the door with bread and salt, and an Estonian bride was once given the bonnet of a married woman.

This guide takes each country in turn and sits alongside our [wedding traditions worth keeping](/expert-advice/wedding-traditions) and [unusual wedding traditions around the world](/10-unusual-wedding-traditions-around-the-world). For Australian couples, it also covers safety levels, embassies and carrying the customs home.

## Key takeaways

- Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania each have their own language and customs, so a "Baltic" wedding is really one of three national traditions.
- Latvia's mičošana is a midnight ritual, often led by folklore groups, in which the bride symbolically says goodbye to her status as a daughter and takes on the role of wife.
- Lithuania marked the same passage with gaubtuvės, the removal of the bride's wreath, and Estonia with tanutamine, when the bride was dressed as a married woman.
- As at November 2026 Smartraveller rates all three countries "Exercise normal safety precautions", with "Reconsider your need to travel" for areas of Latvia near the border with Belarus, and the Australian embassies covering them sit in Stockholm (Estonia and Latvia) and Warsaw (Lithuania).

## Three countries, three sets of Baltic wedding traditions

Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania each have their own language, their own customs and their own marriage law. So everything below is tied to the country its source names.

The customs also vary by region and by family. Where a custom is passed on by word of mouth rather than documented, we say it is "often told", and we leave its meaning to your family.

## Latvian wedding traditions

A traditional Latvian wedding once belonged to the end of the farming year. In earlier times Latvian weddings were most often held in autumn, after the harvest, and the celebrations could run for several days or even a whole week. <!-- fact_03 -->

At modern Latvian weddings, guests still shout "Rūgts!" (bitter), and the couple are expected to kiss. <!-- fact_03 -->

### 1. Mičošana: from bride to wife (Latvia)

Latvia's official tourism site describes mičošana as a midnight ritual that is still part of modern Latvian weddings, in which the bride symbolically says goodbye to her status as a daughter and takes on the role of a wife. <!-- fact_01 -->

The ceremony is often led by folklore groups, who sing the songs and perform the ritual. <!-- fact_01 --> <!-- fact_02 --> As it is often told, the bride's wreath or veil comes off at this point and a married woman's headdress takes its place, but the details belong to each family and region.

Treat it as a fixed midnight moment in the run sheet, and ask early whether a folklore group can perform. <!-- fact_02 -->

### 2. Stealing the bride, played lightly (Latvia)

Bride stealing is often told as part of a Latvian wedding: friends of the couple spirit the bride away during the celebration, and the groom has to win her back with a forfeit. If you want it, keep it light and agree the limits beforehand.

### 3. The pūrs (Latvia)

The pūrs is often told as the Latvian bride's dowry chest, filled over years with the textiles she would take into married life. What goes in it, and what it means, we leave to the families who still keep one.

## Lithuanian wedding traditions

Most of what follows comes from the Visuotinė lietuvių enciklopedija, Lithuania's national encyclopedia, and describes historic village custom; today's couples choose which parts to keep.

### 4. The hen night and the rue garden (Lithuania)

Ethnographer Nijolė Juta Gornatkevičė, speaking to the public broadcaster LRT in 2024, described the night before a Lithuanian village wedding. <!-- fact_04 --> The girls sat the bride on a bread barrel, combed her hair and pinned two braids on top of her head; the ritual combing marked her move into womanhood, since a married woman no longer showed her hair and wore a hat. <!-- fact_04 -->

In the same account, the dressed bride first went to say goodbye to her rue garden, which symbolised her youth. <!-- fact_05 --> She then made a round of the village with her friends to say goodbye, collecting gifts from the neighbours. <!-- fact_05 -->

### 5. Gaubtuvės: removing the bride's wreath (Lithuania)

Lithuania marked the bride's change of status with its own rite. One of the most important old Lithuanian wedding rites was gaubtuvės, the removal of the bride's wreath. <!-- fact_11 --> Afterwards she no longer had the right to braid her hair, and the rite marked her symbolic passage into her husband's authority. <!-- fact_11 -->

The little rue wreath (rūtų vainikėlis) could also travel. When an older sister married, the groom's groomsmen placed the bride's rue wreath on her younger sister, marking her officially as next to marry. <!-- fact_12 --> Any other meanings given to rue at weddings are often told, and vary from family to family.

### 6. Piršlybos and the matchmaker (Lithuania)

A Lithuanian wedding once began with the piršlys, the matchmaker. A son's wife was usually sought not by his father but by a member of the village community he authorised, who handled the piršlybos (matchmaking). <!-- fact_07 --> During piršlybos the families met the girl's parents and agreed the economic and other terms of the marriage. <!-- fact_08 --> Only after the sužadėtuvės, the betrothal rites, which carried legal weight, was the wedding agreed. <!-- fact_08 -->

By the early 20th century, parents usually chose the groom, and the matchmaker joined the groom, his father and his cousins at the eve-of-wedding gathering, where he was known for his jokes. <!-- fact_06 -->

The matchmaker could also become the evening's scapegoat. In the 19th and early 20th centuries in western Lithuania and the north of central Lithuania, a mock "matchmaker's trial" arose: as the wedding ended, the bride's groomsmen accused him of deceiving her, "judges" sentenced him, and the bride rescued him with a gift of a towel. <!-- fact_10 --> The custom later spread to other regions. <!-- fact_10 -->

### 7. The bread and salt welcome (Lithuania)

Throughout Lithuania, newlyweds have long been welcomed as the most honoured guests, with bread and salt and a drink. <!-- fact_13 --> In the 19th century, the arriving bride was met in the house by her mother-in-law with water and salt. <!-- fact_18 -->

Couples still tend to keep it. The planner LRT interviewed in 2015 said couples rarely give up being met at the reception with salt, bread and water. <!-- fact_14 --> Allow a few quiet minutes at the door for it, and a small table, before guests take their seats.

If your families also share Ukrainian roots, our guide to [Ukrainian wedding traditions](/expert-advice/ukrainian-wedding-traditions) covers that country's customs on their own terms.

### 8. Gates and obstacles on the way (Lithuania)

Formerly, only the couple's own village youth built welcome gates (sutiktuvių vartai) to honour them. <!-- fact_15 --> Since the late 20th century, gates have gone up along the whole route of the returning wedding party, mostly in return for a ransom (išpirka). <!-- fact_15 --> When the groom's party reached the bride's home, the matchmaker with a bottle and the svočia with a karvojus, the wedding loaf, bought their way through. <!-- fact_15 --> Build slack into your transfer times, and give the best man something to hand over at each gate.

### 9. The kraitis and the return visit (Lithuania)

The bride's kraitis, her trousseau or dowry, was carried to the groom's home by the kraitvežiai, the dowry carriers from the groom's side. <!-- fact_17 --> A painted chest to hold it is often told as part of the picture, though we leave its decoration and meaning to family memory.

On the first Sunday after the wedding came the atgrąžtai. <!-- fact_09 --> The newlyweds visited the bride's parents for a small meal with both sets of parents, the matchmaker, the svočia and the women who had pinned the wreath. <!-- fact_09 --> The kraitis, matchmaking and the atgrąžtai all lost their place over the 20th century. <!-- fact_09 --> <!-- fact_17 -->

## Estonian wedding traditions

Estonian wedding customs are recorded in Ülo Tedre's wedding glossary, published in Mäetagused, the folklore journal of the Estonian Literary Museum. <!-- fact_116 --> The terms below are Estonian and the translations are ours.

### 10. The bonnet ceremony, tanutamine (Estonia)

Tanutamine (in northern Estonia also linutama) was the high point of the wedding as a rite of passage: the bride (mõrsja) was dressed in the garments of a married woman, and became a young wife (noorik). <!-- fact_116 -->

Who put the bonnet on depended on where it happened. <!-- fact_117 --> At the bride's home it was a married woman attendant (kaasanaine) or the bride's matron (kõrvane); at the groom's home it was his mother or the best man's wife. <!-- fact_117 --> A later form, mainly in south-eastern Estonia, had the schoolmaster do it. <!-- fact_117 -->

The rite took place at the bride's home in western Estonia, or at the groom's home, mostly out of sight of the guests; only later was it done in public. <!-- fact_118 --> The bonnet (tanu), the mark of a married woman, was put on three times, and an apron was tied on straight after (põlletamine). <!-- fact_118 -->

It has not vanished. An Estonian open-air heritage museum lists the bride's tanutamine among the ancestral customs it can build into a wedding today, alongside a church ceremony, a carriage ride, games, dances and songs. <!-- fact_122 --> If you want it, plan it as a private or semi-private moment, which is closer to how it was done.

### 11. Giving the gifts, veimede jagamine (Estonia)

After tanutamine, the second key rite of an Estonian wedding was veimede jagamine, the giving of gifts, usually at the groom's home on the second day. <!-- fact_119 --> The bride handed out gifts she had made, such as stockings, socks and mittens, binding her family to the groom's, and the custom varied greatly from one locality to the next. <!-- fact_119 -->

### 12. Playful stealing and road blocks (Estonia)

Stealing (varastamine) was a widespread Estonian wedding custom. <!-- fact_120 --> Groomsmen took objects from the bride's home, someone tried to steal the bride on the wedding ride, and the dowry chest was a favourite target. <!-- fact_120 --> Its old ritual meaning later gave way to play, and the stolen item had to be bought back. <!-- fact_120 -->

Village youth also blocked the road (tee kinnipanemine) and asked for a "pass" of drink. <!-- fact_120 --> Present-day games at Estonian receptions, a mock "kidnapping" or a quiz for the new wife among them, are often told, and each family plays them its own way.

### 13. Kihnu weddings (Estonia)

Kihnu and Manija are small islands off Estonia's Baltic coast. <!-- fact_101 --> UNESCO names wedding ceremonies among the traditions Kihnu women keep, alongside songs, games, dances and handicrafts. <!-- fact_100 -->

The glossary records two Kihnu wedding roles: the tönk, a costumed wedding figure and jester, and the umbruk, the bride's attendant or a young girl serving at table. <!-- fact_121 --> How a Kihnu wedding runs today is often told rather than recorded.

One note of respect. UNESCO lists tourists insensitive to the islands' traditions among the threats to Kihnu culture. <!-- fact_102 --> Treat Kihnu as a living community to learn from, with local permission, rather than a backdrop.

## Which Baltic customs UNESCO recognises

UNESCO keeps its own [Intangible Cultural Heritage lists](https://ich.unesco.org/en/lists), and three entries link themselves to weddings in their own words.

**Kihnu cultural space (Estonia).** Inscribed in 2008 on the Representative List, after being proclaimed in 2003. <!-- fact_100 --> Its [UNESCO entry](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/kihnu-cultural-space-00042) is the source of the Kihnu wedding note above. <!-- fact_100 -->

**Suiti cultural space (Latvia).** Inscribed in 2009 on the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding. <!-- fact_107 --> The Suiti are a small Catholic community in western Latvia, and [UNESCO's text](https://ich.unesco.org/en/USL/suiti-cultural-space-00314) names wedding traditions among the space's distinct features, alongside drone singing by Suiti women and colourful traditional costume. <!-- fact_107 -->

**Sutartinės, Lithuanian multipart songs (Lithuania).** Inscribed in 2010 on the Representative List. <!-- fact_106 --> These are polyphonic songs sung by women in north-eastern Lithuania, and [their poetic texts](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/sutartines-lithuanian-multipart-songs-00433) take in many themes, weddings among them. <!-- fact_106 -->

Two more entries belong to the wider culture, not to the wedding itself.

[Seto leelo](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/seto-leelo-seto-polyphonic-singing-tradition-00173), the polyphonic singing of the Seto community in south-eastern Estonia, was inscribed in 2009. <!-- fact_103 --> Singing once accompanied nearly all daily activities in Seto villages, and it remains alive at community events, increasingly on stage. <!-- fact_104 -->

The [Baltic song and dance celebrations](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/baltic-song-and-dance-celebrations-00087), inscribed in 2008 for all three countries, are the one shared Baltic element in this article. <!-- fact_105 --> They culminate in large festivals every fifth year in Estonia and Latvia and every fourth year in Lithuania. <!-- fact_105 -->

## Baltic wedding myths worth leaving out

Some details repeat across the internet that we could not trace to any reliable source: a top hat for the Estonian groom, a canopy held by the bridesmaids, a six-month engagement that was legally binding, and a veil handed on to a younger sister. The sourced versions are close but different. In Lithuania, it was the betrothal rites that carried legal weight, and it was the bride's rue wreath that the groomsmen placed on her younger sister. <!-- fact_08 --> <!-- fact_12 -->

## Getting married in Latvia, Lithuania or Estonia as an Australian

Weddings in Latvia, Lithuania or Estonia are possible for Australians, but each country runs its own process. All of this is as at November 2026.

### Safety levels

When we checked in November 2026, Smartraveller rated [Estonia](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/estonia) "Exercise normal safety precautions" (updated 29 October 2026), and advises not approaching the border zone in eastern Estonia. <!-- fact_115 --> [Lithuania](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/lithuania) is also "Exercise normal safety precautions" (updated 14 April 2026). <!-- fact_115 -->

[Latvia](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/europe/latvia) is "Exercise normal safety precautions" overall, with "Reconsider your need to travel" for areas near the border with Belarus (updated 2 June 2026). <!-- fact_115 --> For a Latvian wedding, look at Riga and other places well away from that border area.

### Paperwork and which embassy to ask

Some countries ask for proof that you are free to marry, and a Certificate of No Impediment to Marriage (CNI) is a common one. <!-- fact_108 --> DFAT issues CNIs through Australian Passport Offices, Australian embassies and consulates overseas, and by mail; at least one of you must be an Australian citizen or permanent resident, and fees apply. <!-- fact_111 --> Some local authorities only accept a CNI issued in their own country. <!-- fact_108 -->

DFAT can't advise on another country's legal process, so the first question goes to the local registry or the country's embassy in Australia: is a CNI needed, must it be issued locally, and does it need an apostille? <!-- fact_112 --> The [Smartraveller guide to getting a CNI](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/our-role/notarial-services/how-get-certificate-no-impediment-marriage) sets out the Australian side.

The [Australian Embassy in Stockholm](https://sweden.embassy.gov.au/) is accredited to Latvia and provides consular and passport services to Australians in Estonia. <!-- fact_113 --> Ask it whether it issues a CNI for a wedding in Estonia or Latvia. The [Australian Embassy in Warsaw](https://poland.embassy.gov.au/) covers Lithuania and provides passport, consular and notarial services by appointment only, so allow time to book. <!-- fact_114 --> [[VERIFY: Warsaw embassy English notarial page not read; confirm CNI service for a wedding in Lithuania]] The consulates in Tallinn and Riga offer limited help, and the honorary consulate in Vilnius does no notarial acts. <!-- fact_115 -->

For the registry steps themselves, check with the registry and the embassy, including any fees.

### Back in Australia

You can't register an overseas marriage in Australia, but in most cases it is legally recognised if it was valid where it took place and would have been valid under Australian law; Smartraveller suggests talking to a lawyer to confirm. <!-- fact_109 --> A foreign marriage certificate is usually accepted as proof of marriage but may not work as proof of a name change, so check with your state or territory registry of births, deaths and marriages first. <!-- fact_110 --> Smartraveller's page on [getting married overseas](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/getting-married-overseas) has the detail.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are Baltic wedding traditions?

They are three sets of customs, not one: Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania each have their own language and wedding traditions. Latvia keeps the midnight mičošana, Lithuania the bread and salt welcome, and Estonia recorded the bonnet ceremony, tanutamine. <!-- fact_01 --> <!-- fact_13 --> <!-- fact_116 -->

### What is mičošana at a Latvian wedding?

A midnight ritual, still part of modern Latvian weddings, in which the bride symbolically says goodbye to her status as a daughter and takes on the role of wife. <!-- fact_01 --> It is often led by folklore groups who sing and perform the ritual. <!-- fact_02 -->

### What happens at a traditional Lithuanian wedding?

Historically, a matchmaker (piršlys) arranged the match, the families agreed terms, and the betrothal came before the wedding. <!-- fact_07 --> <!-- fact_08 --> The couple were welcomed with bread and salt, and the bride's wreath was removed in the gaubtuvės rite. <!-- fact_13 --> <!-- fact_11 -->

### What is the Estonian bonnet ceremony?

Tanutamine was the high point of the Estonian wedding, when the bride was dressed in a married woman's bonnet and apron and became a young wife. <!-- fact_116 --> <!-- fact_118 -->

### Are any Baltic wedding customs UNESCO listed?

Not as wedding customs on their own. UNESCO's entries for Kihnu (Estonia), the Suiti cultural space (Latvia) and sutartinės (Lithuania) name weddings among their traditions or themes. <!-- fact_100 --> <!-- fact_107 --> <!-- fact_106 -->

### Can Australians get married in Latvia, Lithuania or Estonia?

Yes, but each country has its own process. Ask the local registry whether it needs a CNI and an apostille, then the Australian Embassy in Stockholm (Estonia and Latvia) or Warsaw (Lithuania), and confirm any fees with both. <!-- fact_112 --> <!-- fact_113 --> <!-- fact_114 -->
